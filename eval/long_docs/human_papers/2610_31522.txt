Abstract
Hidden future-token leakage can create false architectural gains: an autore-
gressive model may appear substantially better under teacher-forced eval-
uation while its free-running generation deteriorates.
We investigate this
failure in an adaptive residual-routing decoder whose sequence-level routing
summary exposes prefix positions to future tokens despite a correct causal
attention mask. An anomalous 24-layer checkpoint reduces validation loss
from the baseline’s 3.5958 to 1.4259, yet it fails a prefix-invariance audit
and has substantially higher reference-model perplexity during generation.
Layer-wise diagnostics and implementation inspection trace the violation to
a full-sequence summary broadcast through the dynamic routing-query path.
Two independently trained 12-layer causal repairs eliminate detectable leak-
age and return validation loss and reference perplexity to the control scale,
removing the large apparent advantage.
As diagnostic tools, we use the
established prefix-invariance contract, maximum prefix-output discrepancy
(LeakScore), complementary behavioral metrics, and layer-wise profiles. Sup-
porting experiments cover six synthetic implementation pairs spanning five
module families, clean pretrained controls, controlled fault injection, and
cache consistency across numerical precisions. These results illustrate how
hidden future-token access can produce misleading claims of architectural
progress and why lower teacher-forced loss should be interpreted together
with whole-model causal checks and free-running evaluation.
Keywords:
autoregressive models, causal masking, leakage detection,
sequence modeling

1. Introduction
In decoder-only Transformers, causal self-attention restricts the output
at position t to information from the prefix x1:t. The same restriction must
hold for every operation contributing to that output. Sequence-level pooling,
temporal convolution, state aggregation, routing, and cross-time normaliza-
tion can create additional information paths outside of masked attention.
Prior work on linear attention and recurrent-style sequence compression like-
wise makes autoregressive behavior depend on prefix-consistent state aggre-
gation [1]. Adaptive computation and selective state updates further moti-
vate checking the complete dependency structure [2, 3].
We use hidden future-token leakage to describe suffix influence on pre-
fix outputs through an auxiliary operation outside standard masked self-
attention, despite a correct attention mask. Such access can lower teacher-
forced validation loss without improving valid autoregressive prediction.
Broader studies of leakage have shown how invalid information access can
inflate empirical performance claims [4]; here we examine this risk within
the model’s computation. The practical concern is a false architectural gain:
an apparent improvement that depends on information unavailable during
causal generation.
Figure 1 illustrates the dependency structure. At position i, the causal
path uses only x1:i, while a non-causal auxiliary operation introduces an
additional dependency on xi+1:T. The auxiliary operation may also use past
and present inputs; the violation is the future-to-prefix dependency, not the
presence of the auxiliary module itself.
Our motivation comes from a follow-up implementation of CDR-AttnRes
(Content-Conditioned Depth Routing for Attention Residuals), a residual-
routing decoder variant inspired by Attention Residuals [5]. A full-sequence
summary was broadcast as a low-rank correction to the depth-routing query.
An unusually low teacher-forced validation loss suggested a strong improve-
ment, but causal auditing detected leakage and free-running generation had
substantially higher reference-model perplexity than the controls. Localiza-
tion and code inspection identified the query path; independently trained
12-layer repairs removed detectable leakage and the large apparent advan-
tage. Section 7 distinguishes the anomalous 24-layer trigger from the 12-layer
repair comparisons.

2. Related Work
Leakage and empirical reliability. Data leakage can produce overoptimistic
empirical conclusions [4], while overlap between benchmark content and
training data complicates evaluation [6, 7].
These concerns arise at the
dataset or evaluation-protocol level. Our focus is an internal dependency
violation: future tokens influence prefix computation even when the dataset
split and attention mask are correct.
Causality contracts. FBS describes a no-leakage unit test comparing shared-
prefix logits under different suffixes by ℓ∞discrepancy, together with preview-
cache consistency checks [8].
TorchLean formalizes causality-related con-
tracts for attention and state-space sequence models, including prefix preser-
vation, cache-position alignment, and scan consistency. Its diagnostic ex-
amples also address normalization state and padding conventions [9]. We
build on the established prefix-invariance principle. Our empirical emphasis
is a documented case in which a false gain disappears under causal repair,
supported by cross-module diagnosis and layer-wise localization.
Sequence mixing beyond standard attention. Masked self-attention enforces
causal token access in decoder-only Transformers [10]; segment-level and
sparse-window variants support longer contexts [11, 12]. Linear attention and
recurrent-form Transformers aggregate history into prefix states [1]. Causal
convolutions prevent access to future inputs [13], and state-space models
use structured or selective state updates [14, 3, 15]. RetNet, RWKV, and
Hyena explore retention, recurrent formulations of linear attention, and long-
convolution/gating mechanisms, respectively [16, 17, 18].
Their different
computational forms motivate examining the causal structure of state ag-
gregation and temporal mixing beyond the attention mask. Our controlled
violations concern the auxiliary implementations specified in the experiments,
rather than a claim that these cited architectures are non-causal.
4


Adaptive computation. Mixture-of-experts models route tokens to selected
experts [19, 20]. Expert-choice routing assigns tokens to experts by rank-
ing across sequence positions, so a position’s assignment can depend on later
positions [21]. Expert threshold routing addresses causal routing and compu-
tation allocation [22], while Mixture-of-Depths allocates computation across
tokens and layers [2]. These works make the information available to routing
decisions a relevant implementation concern.
Testing, verification, and residual routing. Differential and whitebox testing
detect behavioral inconsistencies [23], whereas certified robustness methods
derive provable bounds under specified perturbations [24], and formalization
frameworks express semantic contracts [9]. Our behavioral audit requires ac-
cess to position-wise outputs, and layer-wise localization additionally requires
access to the monitored hidden states; compatibility with arbitrary check-
points is not assumed. Attention Residuals provides the residual-aggregation
setting for our follow-up CDR implementation [5]. The case concerns the full-
sequence query correction added in that implementation.
3. Problem Definition
We consider a decoder-only autoregressive sequence model f operating
on an input sequence
x = (x1, x2, . . . , xT).
Causal correctness requires that, for any two inputs x and x′ with x1:t = x′
1:t,
the outputs satisfy f(x)1:t = f(x′)1:t. This requirement concerns the complete
computation of f, including auxiliary operations as well as attention.
We use hidden future-token leakage to denote a violation of this condition
mediated by an auxiliary operation outside masked self-attention, while the
attention mask itself is causal.
The term “hidden” refers to a forbidden
dependency that is not visible in the attention mask.
This notion should be clearly distinguished from ordinary train/test leak-
age or benchmark contamination in machine learning [4, 6]. In conventional
train/test leakage, the main problem is that evaluation data, labels, or bench-
mark content improperly overlaps with the training process, which leads to
overoptimistic performance estimates at the dataset or protocol level. By
contrast, hidden future-token leakage is an intra-model causal violation. The
training and validation split may be entirely correct, yet the model can still
behave non-causally at inference time because suffix information flows into
5


prefix computation through internal modules. In other words, ordinary leak-
age concerns whether the model has seen the wrong data, whereas hidden
future-token leakage concerns whether the model computes the wrong de-
pendency structure.
Our audit examines which auxiliary operations violate this contract, how
strongly their outputs depend on suffix perturbations, and where that depen-
dence first becomes detectable. We use the maximum prefix-output discrep-
ancy, denoted LeakScore, together with complementary behavioral metrics
and layer-wise profiles.
4. Audit Methodology
To detect hidden future-token leakage in a module-agnostic way, we use a
simple causal auditing principle based on prefix invariance. The core idea is
that, for a causally correct autoregressive model, changing only the suffix of
an input sequence should not affect the model outputs on the shared prefix.
4.1. Prefix-Invariance Criterion
Let x and x′ be two input sequences such that
x1:t = x′
1:t,
xt+1:T̸ = x′
t+1:T.
If the model f is causally correct, then its outputs on the shared prefix should
remain unchanged up to numerical tolerance:
f(x)1:t ≈f(x′)1:t.
This is the criterion used throughout the paper. The prefix is held fixed
and the suffix is perturbed. A discrepancy exceeding a suitably calibrated
numerical tolerance, with other sources of variation controlled, is evidence of
suffix-to-prefix influence. Because this test is defined only in terms of input-
output behavior, it can be applied uniformly across a wide range of sequence
modules, regardless of their internal implementation.
4.2. LeakScore

suffix perturbation using the following maximum-difference statistic, which
we denote LeakScore:

4.4. Tolerance and Numerical Robustness
Because the test compares floating-point outputs, a small numerical tol-
erance is necessary even for causally correct implementations. In our exper-
iments, we use
ϵ = 10−5
as the default threshold.
This choice is appropriate for our toy auditing
setup in standard float32 evaluation and provides a stable separation be-
tween causal and non-causal modules.
As shown later in the robustness
checks, our main conclusions are unchanged when the tolerance is varied over
{10−6, 10−5, 10−4}: causally repaired variants remain near numerical zero,
whereas non-causal variants consistently yield LeakScore values far above
this range. Thus, the observed separation is not an artifact of a particular
threshold choice.
4.5. Generation Diagnostics
In the CDR checkpoint case study, audit verdicts are compared with
open-ended generation diagnostics. The reference-model perplexity pplref is
computed using a fixed GPT-2 medium reference LM. The frozen evaluation
code scores the saved text, including the prompt and generated continuation,
in windows of at most 1024 tokens with stride 512. For each sample, it ex-
ponentiates the token-count-weighted mean window negative log-likelihood;
the reported value is the arithmetic mean of these sample perplexities over
the 50 prompts. Higher perplexity means the reference model assigns lower
likelihood to the text. This is a model-dependent diagnostic of generation
quality, not a direct measure of semantic coherence or a human evalua-
tion. The text is re-encoded with the reference tokenizer. Sampling entropy

5. Synthetic Auditing Experiments
We next use the prefix-invariance test to audit a set of synthetic sequence
modules beyond masked attention.
The goal of these experiments is not
to maximize task performance, but to isolate whether representative auxil-
iary components can introduce hidden future-token leakage under controlled
conditions. We construct causally repaired and non-causal counterparts for
each module family and evaluate them under the same auditing procedure
described in Section 4.
5.1. Experimental Design
Our synthetic audit covers six implementation pairs across five module
families: pooling (two pairs), temporal convolution, linear attention, dynamic
routing, and cross-time normalization. Each pair contains one non-causal im-
plementation and one causally repaired counterpart, giving twelve implemen-
tations in total. The selected families are intended to span several common
ways in which future information may re-enter prefix computation, includ-
ing sequence-level summary broadcast, temporal mixing, full-sequence state
aggregation, routing based on global context, and cross-time normalization.
The synthetic modules are randomly initialized and audited without
training. Input token IDs are sampled uniformly from the vocabulary; each
pair shares the same sampled prefix and has independently sampled suffixes.
These experiments test whether the implemented computation respects prefix
invariance, rather than evaluating learned language-model quality. For each
audited module, we compare outputs on the shared prefix. We then compute
the LeakScore on prefix outputs using the default tolerance ϵ = 10−5. A
model is considered to fail the audit if the resulting LeakScore exceeds the
tolerance, and to pass otherwise.

6. Real-Model Auditing
The synthetic experiments establish that the audit has high specificity on
controlled modules. We now ask whether the same test remains useful on real
pretrained models, where implementations are black boxes and numerical
noise is a practical concern. This section reports four additional layers of
evidence: negative controls on trusted models, fault injection to establish
sensitivity, cache and precision consistency across runtime paths, and layer-
wise leakage localization of the first leak layer.
Together they address a
limitation of the toy setup: a behavioral test that works on synthetic modules
might, in principle, either fire spuriously on real implementations or become
difficult to interpret once runtime-path and precision effects introduce non-
zero clean discrepancies.
6.1. Experimental Setup
We use three publicly released decoder-only models: the Transformers
GPT-2 and Qwen2.5-0.5B, and the selective state-space model Mamba-
130M [3].
The suffix-perturbation and localization audits use float32 in-
ference with dropout disabled; the cache-consistency experiment separately
evaluates float32, float16, and bfloat16.
The perturbation protocol follows the same fixed-prefix principle as the
synthetic audit, with a separate evaluation grid.
We set sequence length
T = 64, prefix lengths t ∈{16, 32, 48}, random seeds {0, 1, 2}, and generate
8 suffix-perturbed pairs per combination of prefix length and seed, giving 72
pairs per model. Perturbations operate directly on token IDs, so the two
inputs share exactly the same prefix tokens and differ only in the suffix,
13


avoiding tokenizer boundary artifacts. For each pair we report the max and
mean prefix-output discrepancy, the top-1 flip rate, the affected-token rate,
and the pass/fail verdict under tolerance ϵ = 10−5.
6.2. Clean Negative Controls
GPT-2, Qwen2.5-0.5B, and Mamba-130M each pass all 72 tested input
pairs in float32 with ϵ = 10−5; maximum and mean prefix-logit discrepancy
and top-1 flip rate are all exactly zero. No false positives are observed under
this same-path protocol. Exact zero does not imply that floating-point in-
ference is generally noise-free: comparing different execution paths can yield
non-zero discrepancies, as Section 6.4 shows. The clean controls support the
chosen tolerance for this protocol, rather than a universal threshold for other
precisions or runtime paths.
6.3. Fault Injection and Sensitivity
A clean negative control only establishes specificity. To verify that the
audit can actually detect a hidden leak when one is present, we inject a causal
violation into the same models without touching the pretrained weights. Fol-
lowing the failure pattern found in the CDR case (Section 7), the wrap-
per first pools the full hidden-state sequence h1:T into a single summary
s = Mean(h1:T), then produces a correction ∆z = α g(s) broadcast back to
every position, i.e. zi ←zi + ∆z for all i. This is exactly the sequence-level
summary-to-broadcast pattern that our synthetic pooling routers instanti-
ate, and it is applied identically to all three model families. We sweep the
injection strength α ∈{0.01, 0.05, 0.10}; GPT-2 and Qwen are tested at all
three strengths, and Mamba at α = 0.05, using the same 72-pair protocol.
Table 4 shows the results. Two findings stand out. First, sensitivity is
high: at every tested non-zero injection strength, every evaluated model fails
the audit and the pass rate drops from 1.0 to 0.0, while the affected-token
rate stays at or above 0.993, i.e. the shared correction reaches essentially
every prefix position. Second, the continuous diagnostics behave differently
from the binary verdict. The max discrepancy increases monotonically with
α (approximately linearly for Qwen: 1.6 × 10−3, 7.9 × 10−3, 1.6 × 10−2),
yet even the smallest tested injection α = 0.01 already fails the binary test.
The audit is therefore both sensitive at the decision level and quantitative
in the diagnostic scores, which is exactly the combination needed for the
multi-metric diagnosis emphasized throughout this paper.

6.4. Cache and Precision Consistency
Cache consistency tests whether full-prefix evaluation and token-by-token
decoding produce comparable logits for the same input and checkpoint. A
discrepancy between these paths can arise from numerical precision, kernel
