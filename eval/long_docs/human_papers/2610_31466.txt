AnyGameRenderer: Entity-Consistent
Generative Rendering Across Games and an
Entity-Level Benchmark
Ruicong Liu*, Liangyang Ouyang*, Fengbo Lan, Zhixiang Wang, Kaipeng Zhang
Alaya Lab
AI coding agents can now build small playable games in minutes, but the results usually look like
programmer art: gray blockouts, colored geometric primitives, and flat semantic blobs. We present
AnyGameRenderer, a video-to-video generative renderer that turns such footage into high-quality
visuals with characters, motion detail, and effects. Throughout, it keeps the layout, camera, and
every entity’s position and timing intact. Our AnyGameRenderer generalizes across a wide range
of genres that agents build, such as tower defense, auto-battlers, top-down survivors, turn-based
battles, kart racing, and crowd runners. AnyGameRenderer takes plain video as its game-state
input, with no complex representations such as engine G-buffers, per-object class labels, or low-poly
proxies. Gray blockouts, colored primitives, semantic maps, and wireframes all work as input. It
also preserves entity correspondence: group counts stay correct, and entities appear and disappear
on the right frame. We implement AnyGameRenderer by LoRA fine-tuning MiniMax-H3 ref2va,
a 33B reference-video-conditioned video generation model. Training uses ∼10k paired clips from
three sources: (i) multi-stream rendering data from 38 agent-made games exported from GEAR, an
AI game-creation tool; (ii) two-stream rendering data from Unreal Engine, with programmatically
controlled crowds, count changes, and entity scales; and (iii) real gameplay recordings, with pairs
reconstructed via 3D-box detection. To evaluate the entity correspondence, we propose an entity-
level benchmark of 59 held-out cases with 753 entities, scored against ground-truth boxes. Compared
with the base model, AnyGameRenderer better preserves entity correspondence: temporal match
rises from 50.9% to 62.9%, missed count changes fall from 34.7% to 21.3%, and fewer entities are
hallucinated or left unrendered.

Introduction
Coding agents and tools, such as GEAR [1] game-creation system, can now turn a one-paragraph prompt
into a playable game in minutes, complete with game logic, physics, camera, win conditions, and working
HUD [7]. What they cannot produce is good art. The output usually contains gray boxes, colored cylinders,
and cones on flat-shaded ground. Human studios spend months replacing this kind of “programmer
art” through modeling, texturing, and lighting. Splitting a game into an engine that manages state and
a generative model that renders pixels has quickly become a common idea. Concurrent systems such as
CWM [8], PWM [15], Matrix-Game 4.0 [32], StreamRender-H3 [29], Marionette [26], and SolarRenderer [33]
all adopt it in some form. We do not argue for this setting. Instead, we build a rendering layer that is more
general in what it accepts and more faithful in what it keeps.
As shown in Figure 1, AnyGameRenderer takes a crude gameplay recording and a text prompt (optionally
with a style anchor image), and outputs the same gameplay re-rendered in the prompted style, frame-
aligned with the input. It adds characters, motion detail, and effects, while keeping the layout, camera, and

There are three properties that set AnyGameRenderer apart. (1) It generalizes across genres. Prior and
concurrent works are demonstrated mainly on third-person, first-person, or driving footage, with limited
2


coverage of the genres that agents actually produce.
As illustrated in Figure 1, AnyGameRenderer
generalizes across a wide range of genres, including tower defense, auto-battlers, top-down survivors,
turn-based battles, kart racing, crowd runners, arena survival, co-op games, and shooters. In addition, it
accepts diverse input types, including gray blockouts, colored primitives, semantic maps, wireframes, and
coarse mockup.
(2) It keeps entity correspondence. The hard part of game rendering is bookkeeping: twelve enemies become
nine when three die; a reinforcement wave walks in at t=4.2 s; a tiny far-away mob must not dissolve into
fog. AnyGameRenderer substantially improves entity correspondence: group counts stay correct, entities
appear and disappear on the right frame, and fewer small entities are lost. The zero-shot base model often
fails here: entities never appear, colored blocks stay unrendered, and characters appear even when they are
not in the input.
(3) Its input is plain video. Many concurrent systems rely on costly or complex representations, such as
per-object class labels (StreamRender-H3, CWM), state-augmented 3D boxes (PWM), or low-poly engine
proxies (Matrix-Game 4.0). AnyGameRenderer instead accepts whatever a crude game looks like, including
gray blockouts, colored primitives, semantic-colored maps, wireframes, and coarse clay figures, at different
granularities and with one checkpoint.
To achieve these properties, we build paired (crude video, styled video) data from three sources. (i) GEAR’s
multi-stream rendering data covers 38 agent-made games, helping the model generalize across genres and
input types. (ii) Two-stream rendering in Unreal Engine uses scenes scripted with same-color groups that
change count mid-clip, entity sizes from 20 to 600 px, and frame-aligned appearance and disappearance,
teaching the model to keep entity correspondence. (iii) Real gameplay recordings, converted into crude
inputs by 3D-box detection, help the model render realistic scenes. Together they yield 10,330 training clips.
Existing renderers are evaluated at the frame level, with edge agreement, point tracks, or pairwise judges,
which cannot tell whether each entity is rendered correctly. We therefore design an entity-level benchmark
of 59 held-out cases with 753 entities, each annotated with ground-truth boxes, and evaluate it with four
metrics. Temporal match measures whether each entity appears and disappears on time; position match
measures whether it stays at the right place; unrendered rate measures how often crude input blocks are
left unrendered; and hallucination rate measures how often the output contains entities that do not exist in
the input. Compared with the zero-shot base, AnyGameRenderer raises temporal match from 50.9% to
62.9% and position match from 57.1% to 61.9%, and lowers the unrendered rate from 4.1% to 2.9% and the
hallucination rate from 52.1% to 46.0%.
Contributions.
• We propose a video-to-video generative renderer for games. A single checkpoint handles a wide
range of genres and input types, renders them in diverse prompted styles, and preserves entity
correspondence with the input.
• We build a paired dataset with three sources: multi-stream rendering data from GEAR, two-stream
rendering data from Unreal Engine with scenarios designed for correspondence, and detection-based
reconstruction from real gameplay recordings. Together they yield 10,330 training clips.
• We propose an entity-level benchmark and metrics for entity correspondence. The metrics measure
whether each entity appears on time, stays in place, and is neither unrendered nor hallucinated.
On this benchmark, AnyGameRenderer improves temporal match from 50.9% to 62.9% and reduces
missed count changes from 34.7% to 21.3% over the zero-shot base.

Related Work
2.1
AI-driven game creation
Early work uses large language models to generate game content such as levels and rules. MarioGPT [34]
generates Super Mario Bros. levels from text, Word2World [28] turns stories into playable 2D tile worlds,
and LLMGG [13] generates game rules and levels in the Video Game Description Language. Multi-agent
3


frameworks such as GameGPT [6] split game development into roles that collaborate on a full project. More
recently, coding agents build complete games end-to-end. Recursive Game Creator [7] iterates designer,
builder, player and reviewer roles over HTML and Godot projects; OpenGame [17] is an open-source agentic
framework for web games across genres such as action and tower defense; and GEAR [1] turns a short
prompt into a playable game in minutes. While these systems produce working game logic, their visuals
are usually limited to simple shapes and placeholder assets, far from the art quality of commercial games.
2.2
Interactive world models
Another line of work replaces the game engine altogether: an autoregressive video model, conditioned on
player actions, generates the next frames. Examples include Genie [4, 30, 2], GameNGen [36], Oasis [9],
MineWorld [11], GameGen-X [5], GameFactory [41], Hunyuan-GameCraft [18, 35], the Matrix-Game
line [12, 37, 10], and Yume [25, 24].
These models are strong simulators but hard to control in the
ways a game needs: exact entity counts, scripted events, and rules that hold for minutes. Long-horizon
memory [39, 40, 38, 22] reduces drift but does not make the world programmable.
2.3
Generative rendering for games
A growing body of work keeps the engine in charge of game state and uses a generative model only
to render pixels. Neural forward rendering from G-buffers [19, 3, 42, 23] and earlier generative world
renderers [16, 20] follow this split, but require engine-exact inputs. Several concurrent systems extend
it to crude inputs that a game or an agent can produce cheaply. CWM [8] lets a coding agent maintain
world state and renders a 12-class proxy video with a MiniMax-H3 LoRA. PWM [15] converts real AAA
recordings into state-augmented 3D oriented boxes and renders them with a structured spatial ControlNet
on a frozen backbone. Matrix-Game 4.0 [32] renders low-poly engine proxies in real time with a distilled
causal DiT. StreamRender-H3 [29] streams a 13-class semantic map into video. SolarRenderer [33] trains a
control LoRA on a frozen MiniMax-H3 with six types of structural video, passes them without type labels,
and generalizes to structure types unseen in training; it also renders in four steps and supports streaming.
AnyGameRenderer differs from these systems in three ways. First, they are demonstrated mainly on realistic
third-person, driving, or a few self-built 3D scenes; our method generalizes across a wide range of genres,
including tower defense, auto-battlers, top-down survivors, turn-based battles, kart racing, crowd runners,
arena survival, co-op games, and shooters. Second, many of them rely on a fixed intermediate representation,
while we take plain video. Third, none of them measures entity-level correspondence. Frame-level metrics
barely change when a small entity is missing or a group arrives late, and edge agreement even rewards
leaving crude blocks unrendered. We make correspondence, including the count, timing and position of
every entity, the main target of both training data and evaluation.
3
Method
AnyGameRenderer treats game rendering as reference-conditioned video editing: given a crude gameplay
recording (a plain reference video) and a caption saying what each crude entity stands for, it generates the
same gameplay in a target style (Figure 2). We design a caption schema to enforce entity correspondence
(Section 3.2).
3.1
Task setting
Task. Let Vin ∈RT×H×W×3 be a crude gameplay clip and c be a text prompt describing the target style and
what each crude entity represents. AnyGameRenderer produces Vout of the same length and resolution such
that (i) camera, layout, and timing match Vin frame by frame; (ii) every crude entity becomes its finished
counterpart at the same position, apparent size, and heading, appearing and disappearing on the same
frames; and (iii) appearance, including materials, lighting, character design, and environment, follows c and
an optional anchor image Ia.

Base model. We build on the ref2va mode of MiniMax-H3 [27], which edits a reference video according to
a text prompt. We pass the crude clip as <Video 1> and describe the target rendering in the prompt, so any
crude input works. The model runs at 24 fps up to 768p. We disable the audio loss in training and discard
audio at inference.
Conditioning. Each sample has three conditions: 1) the crude clip as reference video <Video 1>; 2) a
six-segment structured caption (Section 3.2); and 3) an optional anchor image. During training, the anchor
is the target’s first frame; at inference, it serves as a style reference or is omitted. The crude clip fixes the
structure, while the caption and anchor set the style. To render the same clip in a new style, we only change
the prompt or the anchor.
Trainable parameters. We train a LoRA [14] of rank 128 on all transformer blocks with the standard
flow-matching objective [21], keeping the base weights frozen. All reported results use 20-step Euler
sampling on base+LoRA.
3.2
Caption schema: the correspondence contract
We keep the base model’s six-segment format (subject_definitions, summary, retention_analysis,
detailed_description, overall_soundscape, non_diegetic_music) and follow four rules, which we call
the correspondence contract.
Subjects are entities; crude shapes are their representation. Each subject is defined as the real entity,
and the crude input is named as its representation, e.g., “every blue ellipsoid in <Video 1> is one raccoon-like
orchard raider, and the whole blue group is a group of identical ones.” The color-to-identity mapping is set per
clip, groups are described at class level with explicit statements that they exist.
Retention analysis fixes what follows the input. This segment declares position, apparent size, timing,
and head count as preserved exactly, and leaves appearance to the prompt. It also describes empty space
positively: “areas empty in <Video 1> stay empty; no character is added that has no primitive in <Video
1>.”
5


Positive assertions only. The base model has no negative prompt, and negative phrasing tends to
produce what it names (“no HUD” reliably produces a HUD). All captions are therefore written as positive
statements.
Programmatic captions with deliberate noise. Captions are generated automatically from engine metadata
and detection results, not written by hand. Each caption lists every character that may appear in the game,
even if some never show up in the clip. We keep this noise on purpose: it teaches the model to decide what
exists from the video, not from the text. As a result, the model rarely adds characters that are named in the
caption but missing from the input.
3.3
Training recipe
Unified format. All sources are resampled to 124-frame clips at 24 fps (5.17 s) and 1344×768, with the
target’s first frame as the anchor and a silent 32 kHz audio track. Latents and text embeddings are cached
once per source.
Optimization. We train a rank-128 LoRA on all blocks with a global batch size of 16. The learning rate warms
up over 100 steps and then follows a cosine decay, with a peak between 2×10−5 and 2.8×10−5; weight decay
is 0.01. We use bf16 precision, gradient checkpointing, and block swapping, while FlashAttention-3 [31]
speeds up each step by about 30%. Training runs on 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB). Each step takes about
129 s, and three epochs over all 10,330 training clips take about 48 hours.
Deployment. We use a LoRA multiplier of 1.0 for in-domain inputs and styles. For out-of-domain text-only
styles (no anchor image, and a style unseen in training), a multiplier of 0.7 recovers the zero-shot visual
quality while keeping the correspondence gains. We recommend it as the default for open-ended styling.
4
Data
We build (crude video, styled video) pairs from three sources: multi-stream rendering data from GEAR
covers different genres and input types; two-stream rendering data from Unreal Engine targets entity
correspondence; and real gameplay recordings add realistic layouts, cameras, and motion. In every pair, the
crude and styled videos share the same camera and layout and are frame-aligned by construction.
4.1
GEAR games
GEAR [1] is a game-creation system that builds small games end-to-end from a prompt and can export
different render passes from a running game. Our multi-stream rendering data uses 38 agent-made games,
covering shooters, survivors, tower defense, kart racing, arena, and co-op designs. This source contributes
3,221 clips. Each clip has five aligned passes: the target render; a primitive pass, where the scene is a gray
blockout and characters are simple geometric shapes; a coarse pass, with a more detailed blockout and
low-poly clay figures; a 12-class semantic map; and depth. Each clip also comes with an anchor image, a
caption, and 3D boxes. We train on the pair of primitive and target.
4.2
Unreal Engine
Each Unreal Engine scene uses two-stream rendering: it is rendered from the same simulation and camera
as a gray blockout with colored blocks for characters and as a full render. The protagonist is always blue,
and other characters use a six-color set.
Rather than collecting generic gameplay, we script scenes around the zero-shot failures (see examples in
Figure 3) to ensure entity correspondence:
• Count changes in same-color groups. Groups of 2 to 20 characters walk in, spawn, walk out, or
despawn during the clip, in dense clusters, columns, or scattered formations. This targets crowds left
as unrendered blocks and entities that appear late or never.

4.3
Real gameplay recordings
Following the reconstruction approach in PWM [15], we convert real gameplay recordings of combat,
driving, and pedestrian scenes into crude inputs. We detect 3D bounding boxes in each frame and render
them over a checkerboard ground as proxies of varied shapes: solid boxes, wireframe boxes, cylinders, and
upright ellipsoids, with about 800 clips per shape. The original recording is the target, and captions come
from the detection labels plus one sentence describing the environment. This source adds real-world scenes,
with realistic camera work and vehicle and pedestrian motion. It contributes 3,199 clips.
The three sources are mixed at roughly equal weight (Table 1), giving 10,330 clips of 124 frames, about 14.8
hours of paired video.

Experiments
We first describe the evaluation protocol (Section 5.1), then evaluate entity correspondence (Section 5.2) and

multi-stream rendering cases from GEAR across 6 held-out games. Each case uses a source clip excluded
from training, rendered in a style or with characters not seen in training. All models are run with the same
seed, 20 sampling steps, and the same captions.

Entity-sync metrics. Input entities are the ground-truth 2D boxes shipped with each crude video; entities
that exist for fewer than 12 frames are ignored. In the generated video, we locate characters with SAM3
open-vocabulary segmentation, prompted with the caption’s subject phrases plus generic person and vehicle
terms. We remove shadow and reflection detections and link the remaining detections into tracks. In each
frame, input boxes and detections are matched with the Hungarian method, and a pair is accepted only if
IoU ≥0.3 or the centroid offset is within half the input box size. We report four main metrics, averaged
over entities:
• Temporal match: the IoU between the frames in which an entity exists in the input and the frames in
which its track is in place. Late or missing appearance, lingering after despawn, and identity swaps
all lower it.
• Position match: 1 −d/(0.5 s) on matched frames, where d is the centroid offset and s is the input box
size.
• Unrendered rate: the fraction of entity-frames in which the input block is still visible as a flat color
patch.
• Hallucination rate: the fraction of detected entity-frames that belong to tracks matching no input
entity.
We also report three secondary statistics:
• On-time appearance: an entity is recognized at the first frame t where it is matched in both t and
t+1, giving an onset delay ∆on = t −tin, where tin is its first frame in the input. An entity is on time
if |∆on| ≤4 frames (≈0.17 s).
• Never appears: the fraction of entities that are never recognized.
• Missed count changes: a count change is a rise of at least two entities in the input within six frames,
nin[t+3] −nin[t−3] ≥2, with target count nin[t+8]. It is reproduced if, within the next 60 frames, the
detected count stays at or above the target minus one for three consecutive frames. We report the
fraction of the 75 count changes in the benchmark that are missed.

5.2
Entity correspondence
Quantitative results. Table 2 reports results on all 59 cases and on each source. Over all cases, our method
improves all four main metrics: temporal match rises from 50.9% to 62.9%, position match from 57.1% to
61.9%, the unrendered rate falls from 4.1% to 2.9%, and the hallucination rate falls from 52.1% to 46.0%.
The secondary statistics show the same trend. On-time appearance rises from 55.9% to 67.6%, entities that
never appear fall from 26.0% to 20.2%, and missed count changes fall from 34.7% to 21.3%.
Gains differ by source. On GEAR’s multi-stream rendering cases, temporal match rises by 13 points and
missed count changes nearly halve. On Unreal Engine’s two-stream rendering cases, temporal match
reaches 96.6%, close to the GT floor of 98.5%, and no entity is missed. Real recordings improve the least:
temporal match rises from 46.5% to 51.1%, the unrendered rate is similar, and missed count changes stay at
20.0%.
Qualitative comparison. Figure 4 shows seven held-out cases. The zero-shot model works well on sparse
scenes with clean captions, but on game footage it fails in three ways. First, it leaves crude shapes in the
output: blue cubes in an orchard, cyan cylinders in an arena, and a blue cylinder in the grass where an elk
should be. Second, it drops the input viewpoint when the style is far from typical game footage, turning a
top-down blockout into a frontal anime group shot or a first-person Minecraft-like scene. Third, it adds
characters named in the caption that have no block in the input. AnyGameRenderer fixes all three in these
cases while keeping the prompted style.
5.3
Generalization across genres
A single checkpoint handles all five genres in our benchmark: first-person shooters, top-down, third-person,
crowd, and vehicle games (Figure 5). AnyGameRenderer improves temporal match over the zero-shot model
in every genre, with the largest gains on crowd and third-person games. It also lowers the unrendered rate
in every genre except crowd games, where both models are already near zero, and improves position match
most on vehicle games. The hallucination rate falls on crowd games but rises slightly on top-down and
third-person games. Top-down games remain the hardest, with low temporal match for both models.
