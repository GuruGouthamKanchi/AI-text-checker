ABSTRACT
Complex objects such as engineering designs and proteins are often difficult to
optimise because their design spaces exhibit high-dimensional structure and ob-
jective evaluations are expensive. Generative models provide continuous latent
representations, but their latent spaces can have thousands to tens of thousands
of dimensions, creating a regime where the latent dimension D greatly exceeds
the evaluation budget. We introduce SABLE, a Bayesian optimisation method for
high-dimensional latent spaces with an isotropic distribution and limited budget.
SABLE uses a Bayesian affine surrogate for prediction and acquisition, treating
higher-order structure as surrogate discrepancy. After N observations, the slope
update has dimension at most N −1, yielding a decomposition of each query
into an in-span component optimised by expected improvement (EI) in at most
N dimensions and an off-span component governed by a scalar norm. We show
that the off-span norm implicitly selected by affine EI can lead to underexplo-
ration, and SABLE instead prescribes it using a finite-horizon schedule, without
restricting search to a learned subspace. A recompression scheme reduces infor-
mation to O(M) pseudo-observations, bounding refitting cost by O(M 2D+M 3)
while keeping parameter inference, prediction, and acquisition decisions to tens
of milliseconds. Across synthetic benchmarks and latent-space optimisation tasks,
SABLE achieves high sample efficiency and reduces decision time by several or-
ders of magnitude at large budgets.

INTRODUCTION
Scientific and engineering optimisation problems often involve design domains that are difficult to
search directly because they are high-dimensional, discrete, or governed by complex physical or
chemical constraints. Pretrained generative models can provide simpler continuous latent repre-
sentations of these domains, making such optimisation problems more tractable. This has enabled
applications in protein and peptide design (Watson et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Das et al., 2021),
molecular discovery (Chenthamarakshan et al., 2023), turbulent flows (Li et al., 2024), and particle-
detector simulation (Amram & Pedro, 2023). In these applications, evaluating candidate quality is
typically expensive, for example because it requires simulation, numerical analysis, or experimental
validation, so only a limited number of proposals can be tested. Bayesian optimisation (BO) is well
suited to this setting because it uses previous evaluations to guide subsequent candidates (Garnett,
2023). Applying BO in the latent space of a pretrained generative model is referred to as Latent
Space Bayesian Optimisation (LSBO) (G´omez-Bombarelli et al., 2018; Kusner et al., 2017; Grif-
fiths & Hern´andez-Lobato, 2020; Tripp et al., 2020; Maus et al., 2022; Moss et al., 2025). Some
approaches train task-specific generative models (Krishnamoorthy et al., 2023), fine-tune pretrained
generators for the objective (Fan et al., 2023; Denker et al., 2025; Yun et al., 2025), or steer genera-
tion using gradients or learned objective models (Gruver et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025; Yun et al.,
2025); we instead keep the pretrained generator fixed and use only objective evaluations.

The latent spaces of modern scientific generative models are typically high-dimensional. For exam-
ple, a diffusion model for Lagrangian turbulence (Li et al., 2024) has D = 6,000 latent variables,
while a CaloDiffusion model (Amram & Pedro, 2023) has D = 40,500. These dimensions can
greatly exceed the number N of affordable objective evaluations. In the regime N ≪D, without ad-
ditional structural assumptions, the data cannot identify even a global linear model, let alone a flexi-
ble global surrogate. High-dimensional BO (Binois & Wycoff, 2022) often addresses this mismatch
through random embeddings or adaptive subspaces (Wang et al., 2013; Papenmeier et al., 2022), ad-
ditive or sparse models (Kandasamy et al., 2015; Eriksson & Jankowiak, 2021), or local modelling
(Eriksson et al., 2019). We instead propose SABLE (Scalable Affine Bayesian Latent-space Explo-
ration), which does not restrict admissible queries to a learned low-dimensional subspace and uses a
Bayesian affine working model inferred from limited data. Higher-order variation is treated as surro-
gate discrepancy, with uncertainty in the corresponding variance scales marginalised. Recent work
develops high-dimensional linear BO and fast spherical EI (Doumont et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026);
SABLE extends this geometry to hyperparameter-marginalised EI, decouples off-span exploration
from fitted uncertainty, which significantly improves sample efficiency, and reduces fitting costs by
over four orders of magnitude at large budgets while remaining in the N < D regime.
SABLE exploits the fact that high ambient dimension does not imply high-dimensional data-
informed geometry. Conditional on the variance hyperparameters, N observations update the poste-
rior of the affine slope vector in at most N−1 dimensions, while the orthogonal directions retain their
prior mean and variance. Likewise, the evaluated designs span at most N dimensions. This geometry
yields an in-span/off-span decomposition of each query. Under the affine working model, off-span
exploration is governed by a scalar, the off-span norm, while EI optimisation over the in-span com-
ponent requires at most N dimensions, independently of D. Standard affine EI implicitly determines
this off-span norm, which we show is often driven close to zero. SABLE instead prescribes the norm
using a finite-horizon schedule based on the remaining evaluation budget and a reference resolution
scale, separating exploration control from the fitted variance scales and any higher-order surrogate
discrepancy they may absorb. The fitting cost would still grow quickly with N, so we introduce a
fast recompression scheme that reduces information to O(M) pseudo-observations. For fixed M,
this bounds the posterior-fitting representation and per-round model-refitting cost independently of
N, while keeping parameter inference, prediction, and acquisition decisions to tens of milliseconds
across the dimensionalities studied, without restricting queries to a learned subspace. Figure 1 sum-
marises the decoupled off-span exploration and bounded-cost model refitting.

SABLE: FINITE-DATA GEOMETRY AND CONTROLLED EXPLORATION
SABLE exploits a property of the affine working model: N observations change the slope posterior
in at most N −1 directions, independently of D (Section 3.1). For acquisition, we use expected
improvement (EI) (Jones et al., 1998). Evaluated designs span at most N dimensions, yielding an in-
span/off-span decomposition with predictively equivalent off-span directions. SABLE lets EI choose
the in-span direction, while a finite-horizon schedule tN, set by a reference-resolution parameter λref
chosen for the local-resolvability regime (Appendix D.5), prescribes the off-span norm. This curbs
affine EI’s tendency to underexplore new directions on non-affine objectives (Section 3.2).
The fitting representation still grows with N, so we use a fast recompression scheme that reduces
information to O(M) pseudo-observations. At fixed M, posterior-fitting storage and per-round
model-refitting cost are independent of N; see Section 3.3. Algorithm 1 summarises the procedure.

BOUNDED-COST MODEL REFITTING
Although posterior updating and acquisition optimisation are data-sized, the fitting representa-
tion grows with N, making posterior and hyperparameter refitting computationally costly.
We
control this growth by recompressing accumulated observations to O(M) pseudo-observations.

Recompression exactly preserves the centred slope–response and intercept cross-statistics, while
discarded spectral information is summarised by pseudo-observations. The resulting representation
has size O(M) independently of N and can be reused across variance-hyperparameter values. A
log-determinant correction accounts for discarded spectral information in approximate marginal-
likelihood evaluation; Appendix E gives details.
The spectral analysis of Appendix E.1 motivates retaining directions with stronger data support:
these produce the largest posterior updates and have the greatest effect on EI, while discarding
weak updates perturbs the acquisition only slightly. Appendix E.4 gives stability bounds for the
conditional posterior and EI approximation under repeated recompression. The analysis extends to
marginal EI when exact and compressed calculations share hyperparameter weights; changes caused
by approximate evidence evaluation are not covered. With compression budget M, the posterior-
fitting representation is bounded in M, rather than growing with N.
3.4
COMPUTATIONAL SCALING
The exploration schedule presented in Section 3.2.2 increases sample efficiency, i.e. it reduces the
number of objective evaluations needed to reach a given value, which we demonstrate in Section 4.
With respect to computational efficiency (algorithmic overhead), with the fast posterior compression
presented in Section 3.3, SABLE addresses a key bottleneck beyond fast acquisitions in Fan et al.
(2026); the cubic growth of model-refitting cost with the number of evaluations N.
Without compression, the posterior-fitting state requires O(ND) storage and, for a fixed hyperpa-
rameter grid, per-round refitting costs O(N 2D + N 3). With compression budget M, these become
O(MD) and O(M 2D+M 3), respectively. Thus, for fixed compression budget M, posterior-fitting
storage and refitting cost are independent of N once recompression is active. For D ≫M, com-
pressed refitting is dominated by O(M 2D), compared with O(N 2D) without compression. Exact
historical posterior-mean readouts defining the EI incumbent require O(NM) auxiliary storage; see
Appendix E.5.

State compression.
On ZonalFunctionBench (Appendix G.1) at D = 10,000, we vary only the
compression budget M across an affine control and three objectives with mild, moderate, and severe
non-affine structure, using ten repeats. ZonalFunctionBench provides random spherical objectives
with controlled higher-order structure and no preferred coordinate system. All variants use marginal-
isation and the prescribed off-span schedule. Figure 2 shows that at N = 5,000, the largest shared
budget, the M ∈{100, 300, 500} variants differ from one another by at most 0.003 in best-so-far
value. Relative to the uncapped representation, their mean gaps are 0.003, 0.047, 0.035, and 0.022
from the affine through severe cases, with no systematic growth over N ∈[1000, 5000]. By contrast,
M = 10 is too aggressive, trailing the uncapped representation by 0.30–0.32 on the non-affine ob-
jectives. The compressed variants continue to N = D = 10,000, providing a long-horizon stress test
beyond the target regime N ≪D. These results support the compression approach of Section 3.3:
retaining strongly data-informed directions preserves optimisation performance.
