ABSTRACT
Looped and depth-grown language models reuse computations across depth and
have attracted interest for their reasoning capabilities. Building on prior sugges-
tive evidence of improved in-context-learning (ICL), we investigate how such
reuse favors ICL, including how it shapes the acquisition, retention, and mech-
anisms of this ability. Our analysis spans pretrained language models up to 3B
parameters and controlled tasks. The experiments provide evidence for a shared
inductive bias toward learning from context: grown and looped models rely more
on information in context and, compared with a full-depth dense reference, ac-
quire ICL earlier in controlled tasks and retain it more strongly when learning
fixed associations in the weights is also possible. Tracing attention heads through
training reveals that depth growth propagates early induction heads across layers.
Ablations show that grown and looped models rely more strongly on induction-
ranked heads for both few-shot performance and later-token prediction. Together,
these findings provide evidence for a shared inductive bias toward learning from
context and suggest that (soft) parameter reuse shapes how this ability develops
and which attention mechanisms support it.

INTRODUCTION
How a language model acquires its depth can shape the computations it learns. Depth growing trains
an initially shallow network and progressively duplicates blocks of layers, allowing later training
stages to reuse computations learned earlier. Looping instead repeatedly applies a shared block
within each forward pass. Originally motivated by training efficiency (Fahlman & Lebiere, 1989)
and parameter efficiency, respectively, both approaches have attracted renewed interest for their
reasoning capabilities (Saunshi et al., 2024; 2025; Geiping et al., 2025). Although grown layers
become independent after duplication, whereas looped layers remain tied, the two approaches can
produce related patterns of iterative computation across depth (Kapl et al., 2026a;b).
This reuse of computation may also favor in-context learning (ICL), as suggested by initial evidence
in both grown and looped models (Kapl et al., 2026b). ICL is the ability to use information supplied
in a prompt or sequence without updating the model’s parameters (Brown et al., 2020). A model
that repeatedly refines its representations might also be better equipped to extract and apply patterns
from context. Yet, improved reasoning performance alone does not establish this ability. Predictions
can rely on associations stored in the weights, on information inferred from the current sequence, or
on both. Moreover, a model’s improvement in predicting later tokens and its ability to learn a task
from demonstrations are related but distinct measurements, with potentially different underlying
mechanisms (Olsson et al., 2022; Yin & Steinhardt, 2025).
Prior work provides promising evidence for a connection between depth reuse and ICL. Looped
Transformers can learn synthetic regression and other data-fitting tasks with substantially fewer
parameters than standard Transformers (Yang et al., 2024; Von Oswald et al., 2023). Studies of pre-
trained language models also report improved adaptation from demonstrations in grown and looped
models for synthetic reasoning tasks (Kapl et al., 2026b). Concurrently, Wang et al. (2026) report

stronger gains with increasing context length and demonstration count in compute-matched looped
mixture-of-experts models. What remains less understood is how these behavioral differences arise
during training: whether growing and looping favor a context-based solution when memorization
is also possible, how they affect the persistence of that solution, and how changes in attention-head
mechanisms relate to the different measures of ICL.
In this work, we investigate these questions through complementary experiments on natural lan-
guage and controlled tasks. We compare dense baselines with depth-grown and looped models
on language-model configurations with dense reference sizes from 360M to 3B parameters. We
measure position-dependent token loss and scaling with the number of demonstrations for few-shot
learning. We then use RegBench (Akyürek et al., 2024) to study learning previously unseen formal
languages, and Omniglot-based tasks (Chan et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2023) to separate learning from
context from learning fixed associations in the weights. Finally, we track induction and function-
vector heads through training and intervene on them in the final models. Our contributions are the
following:
• Grown and looped language models benefit more from context.
In the main 3B
language-model comparison, both achieve larger early-to-late token-loss reductions than
the dense baseline, with the grown model also attaining lower absolute late-token loss.
Both models also achieve higher GSM8K accuracy across all demonstration counts. On
RegBench, grown and looped models predict unseen formal languages more accurately,
with their advantage emerging within the first string (Figures 1 and 2).
• Growing and looping favor earlier acquisition and stronger retention of ICL. In con-
trolled Omniglot tasks where both context and fixed class–label associations can solve
training queries, grown and looped models retain higher ICL accuracy and exhibit lower
in-weights learning (IWL) accuracy than the full-depth dense reference. When labels are
reassigned in every sequence, removing a fixed class–label solution, both acquire ICL ear-
lier than the same reference (Figure 3).
• Depth growth propagates early induction heads across layers. Tracing final induction
heads backward through training shows that strong heads in the grown model predomi-
nantly descend from heads already exhibiting strong induction behavior in early growing
stages. Layer duplication produces repeated patterns of this behavior across depth, while
looped models exhibit analogous patterns across successive applications of their shared
looped block (Figures 4 and 5).
• Grown and looped models rely more strongly on induction heads for ICL. In the main
language-model comparison, ablating induction-ranked heads causes larger drops in few-
shot performance and larger increases in late-token loss than in the dense baseline. These
interventions establish a greater contribution from induction-ranked heads to both behav-
iors, while function-vector heads remain important (Figure 6).
Together, these experiments show that constructing depth through (soft) parameter reuse shapes both
the development of context use and the attention mechanisms supporting it.

RELATED WORK
Grown Models.
Model growth reduces training cost by starting with a smaller network (Fahlman
& Lebiere, 1989). Language-model approaches use layer copying and progressive stacking (Gong
et al., 2019; Reddi et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024), learned transformations between
model sizes (Wang et al., 2023), or masked and function-preserving expansions (Yao et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2015; Gesmundo & Maile, 2023). Growth operators also shape model capabilities:
MIDAS duplicates a central block of layers and improves reasoning at comparable language-model
capabilities (Saunshi et al., 2024). Kapl et al. (2026a) find stronger reliance on later layers in grown
models and propose LIDAS, which duplicates layers around the network’s center independently of
existing block boundaries.
Looped Models.
Cross-layer parameter sharing provides computational depth with fewer weights,
as in Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019), recurrent stacking for translation (Dabre & Fu-
jita, 2019), and ALBERT (Lan et al., 2020), while Takase & Kiyono (2023) study its design choices.
2


Extensions add layer-specific low-rank adapters (Bae et al., 2025) or mixture-of-experts capacity
(Csordás et al., 2024). Recurrence has also been studied for algorithmic length generalization (Fan
et al., 2025), latent reasoning (Saunshi et al., 2025), and inference-time computation scaling (Geip-
ing et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Other work retrofits recurrence into pretrained models (McLeish
et al., 2025; Koishekenov et al., 2025) or explores hierarchical and compact recursive architectures
for iterative refinement (Wang et al., 2025; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025).
In-Context Learning.
ICL enables adaptation from demonstrations without parameter updates
(Brown et al., 2020). Studies show that Transformers infer unseen functions from examples (Garg
et al., 2022), learn gradient-descent-like computations in restricted regression settings (Von Oswald
et al., 2023; Gatmiry et al., 2024), and can be programmed to execute learning algorithms in context
(Giannou et al., 2023). Data properties such as burstiness and dynamic meanings encourage ICL
(Chan et al., 2022), which can decline as persistent associations are learned in the weights (Singh
et al., 2023). Other work connects regular-language learning to in-context n-gram heads on Reg-
Bench (Akyürek et al., 2024) and studies context-dependent representations and their competition
with pretrained semantics through graph tracing (Park et al., 2025). These settings let us probe
context use with and without competing in-weights solutions.
Induction heads support pattern copying and are associated with improved later-token prediction (El-
hage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022). Their formation has been studied in controlled synthetic tasks
(Singh et al., 2024) and language-model pretraining (Aoyama et al., 2026). Function-vector heads
transmit task representations from demonstrations (Todd et al., 2024). Yin & Steinhardt (2025) find
that few-shot performance depends primarily on these heads, many of which first exhibit induction
behavior.
Looped Transformers can match standard Transformers on synthetic data-fitting tasks with sub-
stantially fewer parameters (Yang et al., 2024). Concurrent work (Wang et al., 2026) reports gains
that increase with sequence length and demonstration count in looped mixture-of-experts language
models under matched compute, parameter, and KV-cache budgets .
Most closely related, Kapl et al. (2026b) identify shared depth-wise computational patterns in grown
and looped models, show that grown models can benefit from inference-time repetition, and report
improved adaptation from demonstrations on reasoning primitives. We extend this evidence to ICL
acquisition, retention, and mechanisms across pretrained language models and controlled tasks. We
test whether growing and looping favor context use when memorization is possible, trace induc-
tion heads through layer duplication, and ablate induction and function-vector heads to assess their
contributions to few-shot learning and token prediction.

IN-CONTEXT LEARNING OF DEPTH-GROWN AND LOOPED MODELS
We first examine how growing and looping affect learning from context. After introducing the mod-
els and measures (Section 3.1), we evaluate context use in language models (Section 3.2), adaptation
to unseen formal languages on RegBench (Section 3.3), and ICL with and without an in-weights so-
lution on Omniglot (Section 3.4).
3.1
MODELS AND EVALUATION MEASURES
A dense model trains L independently parameterized Transformer layers from scratch. A depth-
grown model instead begins with a shallower model and periodically duplicates a block of layers,
after which the copies train independently. The main paper focuses on LIDAS (Kapl et al., 2026a),
which selects the central layers without respecting earlier block boundaries. Comparisons with
MIDAS (Saunshi et al., 2024) and at other model sizes (360M and 1.7B) appear in Appendix A.3.
The language models start with block size b = 4 layers and add b layers at each growth event,
copying both weights and optimizer moments while retaining the training schedule.
A looped model reuses the same block of layers several times within each forward pass, with pa-
rameters remaining tied throughout training. We write Loop p-b×r-c for p prelude layers, a block
of b layers applied r times, and c coda layers, borrowing the naming conventions by Geiping et al.
(2025). Its effective depth is p + br + c, with p + b + c unique layers. Our main 3B comparison
uses a 36-layer dense reference (Bakouch et al., 2025), a LIDAS-grown model reaching that depth,

3.3
IN-CONTEXT LEARNING LANGUAGES: REGBENCH DATASET
RegBench (Akyürek et al., 2024) tests adaptation to an unseen formal language. Each input con-
catenates strings sampled from one deterministic finite automaton (DFA), with held-out automata
used for testing. We measure total variation distance (TVD) between the predicted next-symbol dis-
tribution and the uniform distribution over legal continuations. All models apply twelve layers per
forward pass, with two unique layers for the looped model.
In Figure 2(a), the grown and looped models achieve lower TVD than the dense baseline on novel
languages. Their advantage increases over the first few strings, peaking around the fourth (Fig-
ure 2(b)). When zooming in on the test sequences in Figure 2(c), both alternative training variants
adapt faster to the new language by predicting symbols (akin to characters or tokens) within the first
string better after roughly 15 symbols. Additionally, on average, after the first string the grown and
looped model do not continue to increase their advantage with more symbols but keep a constant
improvement. Appendix Figure 12 extends the comparison including DFA accuracy and additional
grown models also evaluated with four training seeds, with the precise setup in Appendix C.

3.4
IN-CONTEXT AND IN-WEIGHTS LEARNING: OMNIGLOT
We next examine learning from context when fixed associations can also be stored in the weights,
or when labels vary between sequences, removing the in-weights learning (IWL) solution. For
that purpose, we use the Omniglot image-classification setup of Chan et al. (2022). Each sequence
contains eight image–label pairs followed by a query image. In the fixed-label setting, every training
class has a persistent label and the query class always appears among the context examples, with
P(bursty) = 1, meaning that every training sequence contains multiple demonstrations of the same
classes. The training query can therefore be answered either by matching it to the current examples
in context or by learning its class–label association in the weights. We distinguish these capabilities
with two probes: ICL accuracy on held-out classes with labels reassigned in each sequence, and
IWL accuracy on training classes whose query class is absent from context. We compare dense
models with 4 and 12 layers, a Loop 4×3 model, and a LIDAS-grown model that expands from 4 to
8 layers at 50k steps and to 12 layers at 100k steps. All models have the same hidden width and use
the same optimizer and batch size. We average over four training seeds for the fixed-label setting
and eight for the ICL-only setting below. Appendix D gives the full setup.
Figure 3(a) shows an early rise in ICL followed by a gradual decline as IWL improves, reflecting
the transience of ICL observed by Singh et al. (2023). Over the shared training horizon, the grown
and looped models retain higher mean ICL accuracy but lower IWL accuracy than the dense 12-
layer model. The grown model has similar ICL and IWL accuracies to its four-layer reference, from
which it first grows at 50k steps. The looped model attains similar ICL accuracy to the grown model
but significantly lower IWL accuracy. These trajectories are consistent with growing and looping
favoring learning from context relative to the full-depth dense model at the same effective depth.
For looped models, this can come at the cost of weaker in-weights learning, while the grown model
appears to inherit both capabilities from its shallow starting model.
To test acquisition when a fixed class–label solution is unavailable (ICL-only), we train a different
set of models with labels 0 and 1 reassigned independently in every sequence, mirroring the ICL
probing setup on training images. Each sequence contains two classes with four examples each,
followed by a query from one of them. In Figure 3(b), ICL accuracy emerges earlier for the looped,
grown, and four-layer dense models than for the dense 12-layer model. By 1M steps, all four tested

configurations almost reach 100%. Thus, this setting does not differentiate how much ICL is attained
but rather the timing and consistency of ICL emergence, given that ICL is the only correct solution
to the training problem.

4.2
INDUCTION HEADS CONTRIBUTE MORE TO CONTEXT USE
The trajectories and locations reveal how induction behavior develops and repeats across depth. A
high prefix-matching score alone does not establish a head’s importance for ICL. We therefore ablate
heads in the final models to test their contributions to few-shot performance and token loss.
Following the mean-ablation protocol of Yin & Steinhardt (2025), we rank final-checkpoint heads
by PS or FV score and replace the outputs of the selected 1–20% with mean activations. For looped
models, each head application is counted and ablated separately. Here we focus on exclusive induc-
tion rankings, which first exclude the top 2% of FV heads, and exclusive FV rankings, which first
exclude the top 2% of induction heads. We evaluate changes from each model’s unablated few-shot
ICL (F1) and ICL-Score. Appendix F specifies the replacement means, indexing, and additional
random controls.
Consistent with observations by Yin & Steinhardt (2025), all three models in Figure 6(a,b) show
greater few-shot ICL degradation from FV-head ablation and greater ICL-Score (token-loss differ-
ence) reduction from induction-head ablation. At larger ablation fractions, exclusive induction-head
ablation reduces few-shot performance and ICL-Score more in grown and looped models than in
the baseline, indicating greater reliance on these heads. FV heads remain important in all models,
but their ablation effects relative to the baseline vary across models and sizes for this comparison
(Appendix Figure 20).
Figure 6(c) shows the NLL increase by token position at 9% ablation. Beyond the first few tokens,
FV-head ablation hurts early-token prediction more than induction-head ablation. The FV-ablation
effect then largely plateaus, while the induction-ablation effect grows with position. Compared with
