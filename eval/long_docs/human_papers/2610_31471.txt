Abstract
Many questions about a video concern one physical object.
Where does the ball end up after the cups are shuffled?
Which card is the queen? How often was the mug covered?
Video-language models (Video-LMs) recognise such objects
in single frames but do not keep them apart over time. On
real shell-game videos, open models from 8B to 235B pa-
rameters, their thinking variants, a reinforcement-learned
tool agent and three training-free tool agents all stay within
ten points of chance. We separate semantic identity binding
from temporal identity maintenance. An object’s identity
should be fixed at a moment when the evidence for it is visi-
ble, and carried afterwards by a tracker. Track, Then Bind
implements this principle without training. A promptable
video tracker follows every candidate object and draws it

1. Introduction
Many questions about a video are questions about one physi-
cal object. Which cup hides the ball after the shuffle? Which
card is the queen once all three are face down? Is the cap that
reappears the one that left? Such questions arise wherever
objects are moved, stacked or put away. Examples are assis-
tants that remember where an item was left, robots whose
own arm hides the object they manipulate, and laboratory
procedures with rows of identical tubes. Answering them re-
quires following one object through occlusion, containment
and look-alike copies.
Video-LMs fail at this. On VET-Bench [1], the strongest
closed models stay at chance on the shell game, and only
a model fine-tuned on synthetic trajectories (SGCoT) es-
capes it. On real cup-game videos from the Perception
Test [2], the same fine-tuned model returns to chance (Ta-
ble 1). Scale and deliberation do not help: Qwen3-VL at
8B, 32B and 235B answers 27.7–33.8% of the 65 shuffled
three-cup videos, and a reinforcement-learned tool agent
answers 33.8%. Three training-free tool agents with Qwen3-
VL-32B answer 30.8–38.5%. They are SpatialClaw [15]
(code around SAM3 [44]), VideoAgent [7] (a tracking-based
object memory) and VideoINSTA [8] (captioned event clips).
On TOC-Bench [3], the 32B thinking variant performs nearly
the same as the direct 32B model (36.9% versus 37.1%).
A central difficulty is that the model must re-derive which
object is which from a sparse sample of frames. Identical
objects that cross or disappear leave almost no visual trace
from which to do so. A promptable video tracker such as
SAM2 [4] has the opposite strengths. It follows each object
mask through motion and brief occlusion, but it does not
know which object a question refers to. We study two design
choices in combining them. When should the system ask
the VLM about an object’s identity? A hidden ball can only
be recognised before it is hidden, a card only while it is
face up. And once the identity is fixed, what should decide
the answer: the VLM’s reading of the video, or the tracked
state?
We answer them with two design rules and test each in
isolation. First, the VLM is asked about an object’s identity
only through a static question about the moment at which
the identity can be seen. A tracker then carries the identity,
and the VLM is not asked again. Second, once the target
is bound, the answer is read from the tracked state as text,
without showing the video again. Track, Then Bind (Fig. 1)
implements both rules without training. Every candidate
carries a track number on the clip that the VLM sees, so its
answer binds the target to one track. A plan made by the
VLM decides which objects to follow and how the target
can be recognised. As an extension to visibility and event
questions that need no identity binding, the same framework
supplies a timeline built from the tracks. One program and
one set of thresholds serve all benchmarks.

2. Related Work
Video-LMs and their temporal limits. Video-LMs pair a
visual encoder with a language model and are tuned on
video instructions [22–24].
Current open families such
as Qwen2.5-VL [20], InternVL3 [21], Qwen3-VL [17]
and Molmo2 [18] add dynamic frame rates, timestamps
and grounding outputs. Benchmarks that isolate time find
that much of their accuracy comes from single frames.
MVBench [25] and NExT-QA [26] probe temporal and
causal actions, EgoSchema [27] and Video-MME [19] long
videos, TempCompass [28] finds that many models barely
exceed chance once single-frame and language shortcuts are
removed, and TOMATO [29] measures a 57-point gap to
humans on questions that need the frame order. VELOC-
ITI [30] finds weak binding of agents to actions across events,
and VSI-Bench [31] limited visual-spatial reasoning. VET-
Bench [1] and TOC-Bench [3] target object identity directly,
the setting of this paper.
Reasoning-enhanced Video-LMs.
Chain-of-thought
prompting [32] and its video variants [33] write intermediate
3


steps before the answer, and self-consistency [34] votes over
sampled chains. On VSI-Bench neither improves spatial
answers [31]. Reinforcement-learned models such as Video-
R1 [35] and Open-o3-Video-v2 [5] add textual or grounded
reasoning traces. SGCoT [1] fine-tunes Molmo2 to write
object trajectories before answering, and RGA3 [6] trains
a Video-LLM to refer to and ground objects with spatio-
temporal prompts. In all of these, identity is held in learned
representations or generated tokens, which our diagnosis
shows is not enough for look-alike and hidden objects. We
use self-consistency as a call-matched baseline (Table 3).
Object permanence. Infants expect a hidden object
to persist [36] and parse scenes into cohesive bodies that
move continuously [37].
In vision, CATER [38] and
CLEVRER [39] test these abilities on synthetic videos with
containment and collisions. Learned models localise con-
tained and carried objects [40], keep tracks through full
occlusion [41], learn intuitive physics from object-centric
codes [42], and segment objects inside containers [16]. Xie
and Alanwar [68] find that video world models keep visi-
ble objects but drop hidden ones. We use a frozen tracker
and VLM to maintain explicit identity associations across
occlusion and containment.
Segmentation and tracking.
Promptable segmenta-
tion [43] extends to video with a streaming memory in
SAM2 [4] and to concept prompts in SAM3 [44]. Memory-
based video object segmentation [45, 46], point tracking [47]
and tracking-by-detection [48] keep identities through mo-
tion and short occlusion, and open-vocabulary detectors [49]
supply the initial boxes. None of them knows which object
a question refers to. Our agent obtains the boxes from the
VLM and the identities from SAM2.1.
Visual prompting with tracks. Marks drawn on an im-
age steer a model to a region: a red circle for CLIP [50], num-
bered regions in Set-of-Mark [10], and free-form prompts
learned by ViP-LLaVA [51]. Coarse Correspondences [11]
draws tracker IDs on video frames and lets the model an-
swer the whole question. Elysium [52], VideoRefer [53] and
Sa2VA [54] instead train Video-LLMs to track, refer to or
segment objects. We draw the same kind of numbered tracks,
but the model only answers a static question about one mo-
ment, and the answer comes from the tracks. Section 5.3
shows that the overlay alone recovers little of the gain at 8B
and part of it at 32B.
Tool-using agents. VisProg [55] and ViperGPT [56]
compose vision modules through generated programs, and
ReAct [57] interleaves reasoning with tool calls.
For
video, agents retrieve frames iteratively [58], summarise
clip captions [8, 59], plan over a symbolic memory [60], or
store object-centric tracking states that an LLM queries [7].
STRAND [9] links per-chunk object states by attribute and
position similarity, making identical objects difficult to sep-
arate. SpatialClaw [15] writes code that calls SAM3 and
Depth Anything 3 [61].
AgentRVOS [12] reasons over
SAM3 tracks to segment a referred, visible object, and Dual-
Agent [13] and ACTrack [14] use a VLM to verify or re-
prompt a tracker. These agents, like ours, keep object state
outside the model, and they leave the final judgement of
which object is which to the model. Our design differs in
when the model is asked about identity and in what decides
the answer once identity is fixed.

4. Method
Track, Then Bind organises inference into five steps (Fig. 2;
Algorithm 1 in Appendix A). A plan decides which steps
run, and all thresholds are the same for every benchmark.
Step 1: Plan. The VLM watches V at 2 fps and fills
a fixed schema. It lists the entities in q with their roles:
the target, its carriers, and other subjects. It states how the
target is recognised, by its look or as hidden in a carrier, and
whose left and right the question uses. A separate text-only
question asks whether q is about the target’s final location.
If the target is recognised by its look and no carrier is named,
all objects of the target’s category become its carriers. The
plan selects these operations from the question using the
same schema across benchmarks.
Step 2: Perceive. The VLM boxes all entities in one call
per frame, on 8 frames spaced evenly over the video. Entities
found on none of these frames receive a second round of 8
frames in between. SAM2.1 [4] is prompted on the earliest
frame that shows the most common number of instances, or
on the largest box for a single object. It propagates every
instance forward and backward at 6 fps. Carrier tracks that
overlap, move more than a quarter of their width per frame,
or end early trigger a second pass. This pass tracks the
carriers and the target at the native frame rate, up to 300
frames. Tracking all candidates jointly separates identical
objects by their trajectories, and the dense pass is spent only
where they cross. All questions about one video share its
boxes and tracks.
Step 3: Find evidence moments. The bind question of
step 4 always names the moment it is about: the target in
view, or the target right after it is hidden. Step 3 tries to locate
that moment te in the tracks, so that the VLM sees a short
clip (Eq. 3). For a target recognised by its look, the moments
are the frames on which the VLM found it in step 2. A mask
track alone cannot tell a face-up card from its back. For a
hidden target, the moment is its last view before its own track
vanishes inside the frame. Small hidden objects are often
lost by the tracker or never vanish cleanly. In that case no
moment is located, and the clip covers the whole video. The
VLM then finds the moment named by the question itself.
Section 5.3 reports how often each case occurs (Table 6) and
when a located moment matters (Table 5).
Step 4: Bind. Each carrier track is drawn as a numbered
box on a 4 fps clip, with at most 96 frames. The clip spans
3 s around a located moment (δ = 1.5 s), or the whole video.
The VLM answers one static question qb and returns k⋆. For
a target recognised by its look it names the numbered carrier
that is the target. For a hidden target it names the carrier the
target is under right after it is hidden. When several moments
exist, the last one with a valid number decides. The question
concerns a single moment, not the history after it, so the
VLM never has to follow the target through the shuffle.
Step 5: Read out. A bound target is answered from
facts. The agent states them in plain words, for example “the
hidden object was hidden under mug #2 ... so the hidden
object is under the first mug from the camera’s left”. If q
uses a person’s point of view, one more VLM call on four
frames asks whether that person is opposite the camera. The
facts then also give the person’s left-to-right position. These
facts are F in Eq. 4. The VLM answers q from them alone,
without the video, making the tracked facts the evidence for
read-out. Questions without a bound target use the timeline
L of Eq. 5. Per-subject visibility segments, an ordered list
of events and the coarse direction of motion are appended to
the benchmark’s prompt. The VLM then answers with the
benchmark’s own frames, template and parser. An answer
that cannot be parsed falls back to the direct answer.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks. We evaluate on three benchmarks that ask
about object identity. VET-Bench [1] contains 50 synthetic
shell-game videos (cup) and 50 card-monte videos (card).
The Perception Test [2] cup-game subset (PT) has 107 real
test videos. The VET-Bench authors filtered it to remove
single-frame shortcuts. Following them, we also report its
65 videos with three cups and at least one swap (PT-65).
TOC-Bench [3] asks about object consistency in real videos
along ten dimensions, such as event counts, durations and
reappearance. Accuracy is scored with each benchmark’s
own answer parser.
Development and test data. Design choices and thresh-
olds are selected on development data. These are 195 cup-
game videos from the Perception Test validation split, 56
card-monte videos rendered with the VET-Bench generator,
and 478 TOC-Bench questions. We split the 2,323 TOC-
Bench questions by video into these 478 development ques-
tions and 1,845 test questions. The configuration is frozen
and then run once on the test sets. Each ablation on the test
sets changes one step of the frozen configuration.
Baselines. Open Video-LMs answer directly with each
benchmark’s prompt, frames and parser. We run them in
vLLM [67]. The footnote of Table 1 also gives Qwen3-VL
answering in Hugging Face Transformers, the engine of our
