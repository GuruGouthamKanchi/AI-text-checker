MEASURING THE VERIFIER’S FLOOR: HOW MUCH APPARENT RARE-WORD
TEXT-TO-SPEECH ERROR IS ASR ERROR?
Kirill Borodin1, Vasilii Kudryavtsev1, Nikita Vasiliev1, Grach Mkrtchian2
1BitmanagerAI, Dubai, UAE
2lab260, Yerevan, Armenia
kborodin.research@gmail.com
ABSTRACT
Text-to-speech (TTS) is usually evaluated by transcribing its
output with an automatic speech recognition (ASR) system, so
the word error rate also counts recognizer errors. We measure
these errors on human recordings of the test sentences. On
LibriSpeech and FLEURS, the evaluation recognizer misses
33–67% of very rare words even in human speech, and all
three recognizers miss 22–42%. Checking every word with
two more recognizer families lowers the apparent TTS error on
words that at least one recognizer hears two- to three-fold (very
rare LibriSpeech words: from 10–13% to 3–5%), yet very
rare words remain the hardest. Ignoring words the recognizer
misses in human speech is biased: the other recognizers find
TTS errors far more often on these words (4–6% vs. 0.2–0.3%)
and overturn about half of the errors this mask keeps. As half
of the remaining adjudicated errors may be spelling variants, a
listening test is needed.
Index Terms— speech synthesis, evaluation, word error
rate, speech recognition, rare words
1. INTRODUCTION
The standard test of whether a text-to-speech (TTS) system
says the right words is the ASR round-trip: the synthetic speech
is transcribed by a speech recognizer, and the word error rate
(WER) is computed against the input text [1, 2]. Recent sys-
tems also use the recognizer at inference: they generate several
candidates and keep the one it transcribes best [3, 4, 5].
This test measures two systems at once. If the recognizer
cannot transcribe a word even when a person says it, an error
on this word tells us little about the TTS system. Such words
are mostly rare words, names and foreign words, which is
exactly where TTS errors are reported to concentrate.
A recent study [4] calls rare words “a genuine limit rather
than a sampling problem”, but notes that it “did not measure
its false-positive floor on ground-truth audio”. We measure
this floor. We use corpora in which every test sentence has its
original human recording, and we run the same recognizers on
human and on synthetic speech. The error on human speech
is a diagnostic baseline, not a strict bound, because synthetic
speech can be easier or harder to recognize.
We compare three estimates of TTS word error: the stan-
dard round-trip, a single-recognizer mask that ignores words
the recognizer misses in human speech, and a three-recognizer
adjudication in which two more recognizers check every word.
Our main findings are:
1. The recognizer error depends strongly on word fre-
quency. In human LibriSpeech recordings it misses
1.5% of common but 32.9% of very rare words.
2. Adjudication lowers the apparent TTS error two- to
three-fold. Very rare words remain the hardest, though
about half of the remaining errors may be spelling vari-
ants.
3. The single-recognizer mask is biased. It removes the
words on which the other recognizers find TTS errors
most often, and they overturn half of those it keeps.
2. RELATED WORK
ASR transcripts are widely used instead of listener transcripts
to test TTS intelligibility [6], although ASR WER agrees only
loosely with listeners [7]. Yu and Kang [5] show that best-of-
N results depend on the recognizer family used as judge. Luo
and Wan [8] report the opposite error to ours: a recognizer can
hide real TTS mistakes by writing what the text should say.
The closest work is Appendix C of [9] (not in the ICML
version [7]). It compares ASR WER with listener transcripts
of synthetic and human speech for one TTS system, at corpus
level. We add the dependence on word frequency, word-level
checks with three recognizer families, and their effect on rare-
word results such as [4], whose system we do not re-run.

recording and N synthetic versions. Three recognizers, A, B
and C, transcribe all of them. C is the evaluator (the verifier)
whose round-trip we study; A and B are only used to check C.
Texts are normalized with the Whisper English text normal-

A word is judged if at least one recognizer hears it in the

The mask can fail in two ways. The ignored words may
also be TTS errors, and an error it keeps may still be a mistake
of C. Adjudication reduces both problems but does not remove
them. It leaves out words that no recognizer hears, and a
recognizer may guess a word from context [8]; both lower the
error. Spelling variants and shared recognizer failures raise it.
Table 1 shows four real words. All recognizers mishear
Phoebe in every synthetic version, so all estimates count an
error. For Tessa and stile only C fails in the synthetic speech,
so adjudication counts no error. Veolan is heard only by A
in the human recording and by no recognizer in the synthetic
speech: the mask ignores it, but adjudication counts it.
3.3. Word frequency
We measure word frequency with the wordfreq library [10] on
the Zipf scale, where z=3 means once per million words. Very
rare words have z<2.5 and common words z≥4.5. Tokens
with digits are not assigned to a band.
The LibriSpeech sentences were selected with a simpler
corpus measure: a word is rare if it occurs at most twice in
about 11k LibriSpeech transcripts. This measure is also the
x-axis of Fig. 1. Per sentence, it correlates with the share of
words below z=3 at r=0.60, and we repeat the trend analysis
with this Zipf share.
4. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Data. The main set has 500 LibriSpeech [11] test and dev
sentences of 15–30 words (mean 20.8). The share of rare
words varies from 0 to 41%, while the mean length stays
between 20.0 and 21.5 words across the bins of Fig. 1.
The first 300 sentences had only 37 with more than 15%
rare words and gave unclear trends. We then added the 200
remaining sentences with the highest shares (18–41%). This
was decided after seeing the first results, so the trend analysis
is exploratory.
We repeat the measurement on the first 200 FLEURS
en_us [12] test recordings with 12–30 words and 3–16 s of
audio. They cover 161 sentences (39 recorded twice) with
more names and numbers.
Systems. F5-TTS [2] generates N=8 versions with ran-
dom seeds (N=4 on FLEURS) and clones the voices of five
LibriSpeech test-clean speakers. Kokoro-82M [13] is deter-
ministic, so its N=4 versions use four voices. Since N and the
kind of variation differ, we do not compare the two systems.
The recognizers are A: Whisper large-v3-turbo [14],
B: wav2vec 2.0 large [15] (CTC, no language model) and
C: OWSM v3.1 [16], all with greedy decoding.
Statistics. Words in a sentence are not independent, so
95% confidence intervals (CIs) come from a percentile boot-
strap over sentences (4,000 resamples). Repeated FLEURS
sentences are resampled together. A cell with no errors gets
the 95% Clopper–Pearson upper limit, which assumes inde-
pendent words.
Trends are Pearson correlations over sentences between
the share of rare words and the error per judged word. No
parameters were fitted to these data, but the word matching
rule was revised after inspecting alignments from these sets.

5.2. TTS error under the three estimates
Table 3 compares the estimates on the same judged words.
With any-of-N, adjudication lowers the error from 0.63% to
0.22% for F5-TTS and from 0.72% to 0.22% for Kokoro. The
ratio of standard to adjudicated error is 2.8 [2.1, 4.1] and 3.2
[2.4, 4.8] (3.3 [2.3, 5.6] on FLEURS).
By definition the adjudicated error cannot exceed the stan-
dard error, so the size of the gap is the result. Part of it comes
from pooling, because an adjudicated error must survive 3N
transcripts instead of N.
The gap remains for a single candidate and for the candi-
date a best-of-N system would keep. For the latter the ratio is
1.9 [1.5, 2.4] and 2.5 [2.0, 3.4] (2.1 [1.6, 3.2] on FLEURS);
this is conservative, as the selection also removes some of C’s
own errors.
Rare words remain the hardest. On very rare LibriSpeech
words, the adjudicated error is 3.4% for F5-TTS (10 of 298)
and 4.7% for Kokoro (14 of 298), against 0 of 7,889 and 3 of
7,889 common words (Table 2).
Part of this evidence is weak. 4 of the 10 and 5 of the 14
very rare errors fall on the 32 words that only one recognizer
hears in the human recording. On the words that all three hear,
each system has errors on 3 of 215 words, mostly spelling
variants (Sec. 5.3).
The error also rises with the share of rare words in a sen-
tence (Fig. 1, Table 3). The adjudicated trend is significant for
Kokoro with the corpus measure and for both systems with
the Zipf share. With the corpus measure, it does not differ sig-
nificantly from the standard trend (+0.063 [−0.010, +0.145]
and +0.032 [−0.045, +0.107]).

5.3. What the remaining errors are
There are 23, 23 and 18 adjudicated errors in the three sets,
mostly names, archaic or foreign words and, on FLEURS,
abbreviations.
In 15, 8 and 12 cases the recognizer transcripts point to
a different pronunciation, for example Phoebe as “fuby” or
BCE as “beasts”. The other 8, 15 and 6 are spelling variants
or homophones (Bessy/Bessie, buoy/boy) or text artifacts. No
transcript-based metric can tell these apart from mispronuncia-
tions.
Counting only the first group, the TTS error is 1.7%, 1.7%
and 5.7% on very rare words and 0.15%, 0.08% and 0.30%
overall, so rare words are still the hardest.
5.4. The single-recognizer mask is biased
Table 4 uses A and B as judges. On the words that C misses
in human speech, they find a TTS error in 3.9% (F5-TTS) and
4.9% (Kokoro) of cases, but only in 0.2% of the words that C
hears. The mask thus removes the words that TTS gets wrong
most often, by more than an order of magnitude.
The mask also keeps false errors. Of its kept errors on
words that A or B hears in human speech, A or B hears 16
of 30 (F5-TTS) and 23 of 36 (Kokoro) in a synthetic version,
so adjudication overturns about half. Words that C misses are
also hard for A and B, who both hear only 30% of them in
human speech (against 99% of the words C hears), so part of
the contrast may be shared difficulty.
Computing the mask only over the words C hears (94–
96% of words) changes its rate by less than 0.03 points, so
the denominator does not drive Table 3. With the corpus
measure, neither the mask’s trend nor the difference between
the adjudicated and the mask trend (+0.037 [−0.034, +0.108]
and +0.068 [−0.017, +0.154]) is significant, so we claim only
that the mask is biased.
6. DISCUSSION
We first planned to adapt N to each sentence and to stop when
C hears all words of a candidate. With this rule, 66–75% of
the sentences (recordings on FLEURS) are accepted by the
second candidate. Of the sentences never accepted, 69–80%
contain a word that C misses in the human recording and in
all N versions, so C cannot tell whether TTS or C is at fault.
Our results suggest four practices for round-trip evaluation:
(i) report the recognizer error on the human recordings, by
word frequency; (ii) do not simply drop the words that the
recognizer misses there; (iii) check disputed rare words with
other recognizer families or, better, with listeners; (iv) report
N=1 next to any-of-N.
7. LIMITATIONS
No listening test. All TTS-error decisions come from rec-
ognizers, which share blind spots. A listening test on these
23+23+18 errors, a sample of removed errors and control
words has been prepared but not yet run.
Few errors. With 23, 23 and 18 adjudicated errors, the
rare-word estimates and trends have wide intervals.
Scope. We use English read speech from two corpora. B
and C were trained on LibriSpeech, and C is both evaluator
and judge.
8. CONCLUSION
An ASR round-trip scores the recognizer as well as the TTS
system, and the recognizer’s own errors are concentrated on
rare words. With three recognizers on human and synthetic
speech, the apparent TTS error on judged words is two to
three times the adjudicated error, very rare words remain the
hardest, and ignoring words one recognizer misses is a biased
correction that discards true errors and keeps false ones. A
listening test is needed to confirm these results. We will release
code, sentence lists and all transcripts.
