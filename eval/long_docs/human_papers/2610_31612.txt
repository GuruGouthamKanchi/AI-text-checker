Introduction
Federated learning [MMR+17, KMA+21] is motivated by the tension between two important goals
in modern machine learning:
1. to train machine learning models from large and diverse data sets, and
2. while doing so, to preserve the data privacy, security, and ownership of the data, since data is
increasingly distributed across a network of devices.
Motivated by such considerations, Qiao and Valiant [QV18] introduced the following problem
which they called learning from untrusted batches. There exists an unknown discrete distribution 𝜇
on [𝑘] and data is drawn in 𝑛batches, each of size 𝑚. When all batches are drawn i.i.d. from 𝜇and
can be used without restriction, the empirical distribution achieves total-variation error 𝛼, provided
that the number of batches satisfies

However, in federated learning, we can rarely assume that all the batches are sampled i.i.d. from
𝜇, and instead must consider the possibility that some are corrupted, possibly in an adversarial way.
To address this question, Qiao and Valiant studied the problem with the twist that an 𝜂-fraction
of the batches are adversarially corrupted. Again, it is helpful to compare to a naive baseline. If
we ignore the batch structure and treat the data as a single dataset of 𝑛𝑚samples, then it is easy
to see that we cannot learn the distribution to accuracy better than 𝜂in total variation distance,
no matter how much data we have. Qiao and Valiant demonstrated that when we exploit the
batched structure of the data, it is possible to do much better. In particular, they showed that it is
information-theoretically possible to achieve error 𝑂(𝜂/√𝑚), and that this is the best possible. Batch
information makes it possible to robustly distinguish distributions at much finer scale. Without
batch information, we cannot distinguish two distributions 𝜇and ˜𝜇that are 𝛾-close in total variation
distance in the face of an adversary, because they can convert one distribution to the other by
moving just a 𝛾fraction of the probability mass. But when data is batched and the adversary’s
corruptions must respect the batch structure, the total variation distance between 𝜇⊗𝑚and ˜𝜇⊗𝑚
grows at least as Ω(min{1, 𝛾√𝑚}), meaning the adversary has to corrupt a much larger fraction of
batches to make these distributions indistinguishable.
Qiao and Valiant gave two algorithms that solve the learning from untrusted batches problem,
running in time exponential in 𝑘or 𝑚, respectively. Jain and Orlitsky [JO20b] gave a semidefinite-
programming algorithm based on Grothendieck’s inequality that roughly matches the information-
theoretic bounds and runs in polynomial time. In independent and concurrent work, Chen, Li,
and Moitra [CLM20a] gave similar algorithms and explored extensions to more complex settings,
including improving sample complexity when there are shape constraints on 𝜇.
Qiao and Valiant motivated their model by the need to learn from batches supplied by potentially
malicious agents. Privacy, communication, and computational costs are also central concerns in
federated learning. Keeping raw data local does not by itself ensure privacy: shared gradients can
still leak private training data [ZLH19].
In this work, we revisit the learning from batches problem using the lens of differential
privacy [DMNS06]. There is an unknown discrete distribution 𝜇on [𝑘] and data is drawn in 𝑛

batches of size 𝑚. As is common in federated learning, we think of each batch of data as belonging
to one person (e.g. a batch consists of the data on one person’s device), and want to design a
batch-level differentially private1 algorithm that, informally, hides the information of any one batch
of data. Formally, two datasets are adjacent if they differ in one person’s entire batch. Batch-level
privacy protects a person’s entire contribution, whereas sample-level privacy protects only one data
item. Differential privacy bounds how much the output distribution can change when one person’s
data is replaced.
We pursue three goals. First, we require the strong guarantee of pure batch-level differential
privacy, formally (𝜀, 0)-DP.2 Second, we seek the information-theoretically optimal user complexity.

Several groups have considered private learning from batches, but none give an algorithm
satisfying all three goals. Liu et al. [LSY+20] introduced the problem, giving an efficient algorithm
achieving the weaker notion of approximate differential privacy, or, formally, (𝜀, 𝛿)-differential
privacy. One can strengthen the privacy guarantee of their algorithm to (𝜀, 0)-differentially private
by appealing to basic composition of pure differential privacy instead of advanced composition
of approximate differential privacy [DRV10], but doing so would increase the required number
of batches by a poly(𝑘) factor and would not achieve optimal sample complexity. Ghazi, Kamath,
Kumar, Manurangsi, Meka, and Zhang [GKK+23b] presented a pure differentially private algorithm
with the information-theoretically optimal sample complexity, but their stated construction applies
private hypothesis selection over an exponentially large cover and is not polynomial-time.
We use the efficient robustness-to-privacy reduction of Hopkins, Kamath, Majid, and
Narayanan [HKMN23]. Our contribution is to identify the robust certificate that makes this
reduction work for discrete batches. It is a single degree-6 sum-of-squares (SoS) certificate that
represents discarded batches, mean feasibility, and the mean-dependent covariance of multinomial
batch averages. This certificate simultaneously yields a robust estimator and the score used for
privacy.
