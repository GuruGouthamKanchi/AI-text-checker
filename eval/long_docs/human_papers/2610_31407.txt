Same Entity, Different Script: Diagnosing Cross-Script Entity Matching
Failures in Language Models

Patil Prathamesh Pravin∗

Abstract
Multilingual language models can give differ-
ent identity judgments when the same peo-
ple’s names are written in different scripts. We
study this effect using 601 audited identity pairs
with attested Devanagari and Latin forms, hold-
ing the question and relation fixed. Qwen2.5-
7B’s same-person accuracy falls from 35.1%
in Latin to 8.5% in Devanagari under Marathi
prompts, while different-person accuracy re-
mains above 98%. Evaluation across five mod-
els reveals distinct response biases, and reduced
tokenization fragmentation does not reliably
close the gap. Replicating the design on 374
audited Malayalam pairs built from the same
entities reproduces the effect in a second script
and language family: same-person accuracy
falls to 5.3%, about half the matched Devana-
gari rate, while the margin still ranks matching
above non-matching names at 0.925 AUROC.
Latin-trained probes recover label information
from Devanagari states, exceeding a string-
feature baseline under similarity-matched neg-
atives. Scalar calibration recovers 87% of the
raw model–probe balanced-accuracy gap, iden-
tifying decision criterion placement as a ma-
jor contributor. Activation patching localises
causal sensitivity to the second entity in Qwen’s
middle layers; one shared sensitivity direction
transfers about half the full-state margin cor-
rection. A fact-independent intervention at this
site raises held-out Marathi same-person accu-
racy from 6.5% to 33.5%, with 98.5% different-
person accuracy. Together, these results dis-
tinguish script-dependent discrimination from
criterion placement and identify a causal site
for improving identity judgments.

Introduction
An identity judgment should remain consistent
when the same people are presented through cor-

responding names in different scripts. Yet multi-
lingual models can reject a matching pair in De-
vanagari and accept its attested Latin forms (Fig-
ure 1). This matters for multilingual entity match-
ing: a system must connect aliases, birth names
and pseudonyms even when a user’s writing sys-
tem differs from the model’s most familiar surface
forms.
We study this problem through controlled iden-
tity verification. Both candidate names are sup-
plied; only their script-associated surface forms
change. The question language, people and relation
remain fixed. Across 601 audited pairs, Qwen2.5-
7B’s Marathi same-person accuracy drops from
35.1% in Lat–Lat to 8.5% in Dev–Dev, while it
continues to reject almost all different-person pairs.
The contrast isolates a failure to affirm identity
within a task the model can partly solve through
other surface forms. A second corpus in Malay-
alam, a different script and a Dravidian rather than
Indo-Aryan language, shows the effect is not spe-
cific to Devanagari or to Indo-Aryan languages.
Our analysis connects three levels of evidence.
First, we establish the behavioural gap across
scripts and prompt languages, separating discrim-
ination from response bias. Five-model evalua-
tion and romanization controls show why tokeniza-
tion efficiency alone is insufficient. Second, Latin-
trained probes and output-score calibration estab-
lish how much label information remains available
when the model answers incorrectly. Third, activa-
tion patching and subspace interventions identify
where that information can influence the decision.
The resulting account is actionable. In Qwen,
the second entity’s middle-layer state is a strong
intervention site, and a shared sensitivity direction
transfers roughly half the full-state correction. A
vector learned from training entities raises held-out
Marathi same-person accuracy more than fivefold.
Controls on ranking, script condition and position
explain this gain: much of it comes from moving

Related work
Multilingual models develop shared, English-
aligned representations (Wendler et al., 2024; Wu
et al., 2025), yet factual judgments can vary across
languages. Qi et al. (2023) examine consistency
across translated queries; Ai et al. (2025) study
reference in code-mixed coreferential statements
across language families and scripts, including ac-
tivation patching. Our design fixes the question
language and varies the two supplied names, fol-
lowing one identity relation across behavioural and
internal measurements.
Wang et al. (2025) localise cross-lingual fac-
tual inconsistency near the transition to language-
specific final-layer representations and propose a
linear shortcut. We examine entity surface forms
and find strong middle-layer sensitivity at the sec-
ond entity. Testing a related linear intervention
against output calibration and other script condi-
tions clarifies what its accuracy gain changes. Ro-
manization can improve non-Latin processing (Hu-
sain et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Saji et al., 2025);
our comparison of automatic and attested forms
separates shorter token sequences from reliable be-
havioural recovery.
We combine activation patching (Meng et al.,
2022; Dumas et al., 2025; Nanda and Bloom, 2022)
with linear probes (Panchal et al., 2026). The con-
trolled task makes it possible to compare label de-
codability, output calibration and causal transfer on
corresponding inputs.

Independently varying the two spans yields Dev–

conventional name forms, so the manipulation in-

A second script. A Malayalam corpus covers
380 of the same Wikidata entities, with both names
given in Malayalam and in the original Latin forms.
A Malayalam speaker audited all 380 items and
all 380 distractors, rejecting six whose fields car-

leaves 374. Of the 760 Malayalam strings, 171
are exactly attested in Wikidata Malayalam labels
or aliases, 18 are spelling variants of an attested
form, and 571 are machine-transliterated and then
audited; none of the transliterations was rejected.
The same speaker checked the three Malayalam
question templates. Splits of 222/76/76 are inher-

corpus rather than recomputed, so no probe, vec-

is transferred here. We evaluate Qwen2.5-7B be-

Languages and scoring. Marathi and Hindi
corpora cover the same identities and have byte-
identical Latin forms; 528/601 Devanagari pairs dif-
fer. Each corpus is queried in its own language and
English, with three paraphrases and both yes/no let-
ter assignments. We score the final-position logit
margin m = zyes −zno without sampling. Be-
havioural accuracy pools the six prompts per item
and reports positive and negative pairs separately.
For entity-level probe and calibration comparisons,

4
The script gap
4.1
Behaviour across models and languages
Qwen’s deficit is concentrated on matching
names. Under Marathi prompts, Dev–Dev posi-
tive accuracy is 8.5%, compared with 35.1% in
Lat–Lat; negative accuracy remains at least 98.6%
across the evaluated conditions (Table 1). The gap
persists under Hindi and English prompts. It in-
cludes a discrimination loss: Lat–Lat exceeds Dev–
Dev in margin AUROC by 0.037 [0.025, 0.049] in
Marathi, 0.038 [0.026, 0.051] in Hindi and 0.023
[0.014, 0.033] in English over all 601 items.
The effect spans all three paraphrases and sub-
stantive relation tiers. Even among 154 pairs with
over 80% Latin-form similarity, Marathi accuracy
is 10.8% in Dev–Dev and 32.1% in Lat–Lat. The
gap is larger among the 58 least-similar pairs: 31.3
points versus 21.3 in the most-similar band (Ap-
pendix D; Table 57). Thus, near-duplicate spellings
do not account for the phenomenon.
The behavioural polarity varies by model.
Gemma accepts most positives and many nega-
tives, whereas Qwen strongly favours rejection.
Using the signal-detection measure d′ to separate
these biases, the Lat–Lat advantage excludes zero
for Qwen in Marathi, Gemma in all three prompt
languages and Llama in English (Appendix E).
Llama’s Marathi and Hindi outputs exhibit fixed-
letter instruction failures and are excluded from
this interpretation. Mistral-Small-24B has a 25.8-
point Marathi positive-accuracy gap, versus 6.9 for
the Indic-adapted Sarvam-M-24B. Their negative
accuracies and checkpoint versions differ, making
this a descriptive family-and-size comparison (Ap-
pendix Z).
Mixed scripts reveal a further distinction. Lat–
Dev exceeds Lat–Lat positive accuracy by 9.7
points in English and 11.1 in Hindi without better
per-template ranking (Appendix V). Script configu-
ration therefore affects criterion placement as well
as discrimination, motivating the calibration tests
below.
The gap is not specific to Devanagari. On
374 Malayalam items and the same 374 entities in
Devanagari, Qwen’s native-script same-person ac-
curacy is 5.3% under Malayalam prompts against
10.4% under Marathi prompts, while negative accu-

4.2
Tokenization and familiar forms
Devanagari names are tokenized roughly three
times as finely as Latin names. Fragmentation cor-
relates weakly with the Latin-script benefit, and
longer names are harder to retrieve in isolation (Ap-
pendix F). Nevertheless, surface features predict
script-sensitive items at only 0.56–0.60 out-of-fold
AUC, versus 0.86–0.96 after adding relation states
(Table 18). Five automatic romanizations reduce
Qwen’s mean name length from 16.5 tokens to 7.7–
10.4, but replacement yields no reliable recovery.
Substitution of the attested Latin form reproduces
Lat–Lat within 0.004 in all nine primary model–
language cells. The tested reductions in fragmenta-
tion are therefore insufficient; spelling fidelity and
familiarity remain relevant (Appendix G).
The automatic schemes reproduce the attested
4


Latin spelling exactly for only 0.8–4.5% of names.
Thus, moving into Latin characters and presenting
a familiar Latin name are different manipulations.
The contrast also appears across models: Gemma
and Llama use substantially fewer Devanagari to-
kens per character than Qwen, yet their behaviour
still depends on script. Together, the prediction and
substitution tests motivate examining relation-level
states beyond surface token counts.
5
Available information and decision
calibration
We fit ridge classifiers (α = 10) on L2-normalised
Lat–Lat hidden states and evaluate on Dev–Dev, so
no Devanagari state enters probe training. Layers
are selected on validation and accuracy is measured
on the 120 test items. Cross-language transfer also
succeeds across Qwen, Gemma and Llama (Ap-
pendix H).
Label information transfers across scripts.
Qwen’s question-mark probe reaches 0.925 bal-
anced accuracy and 0.867 same-person recall on
Marathi Dev–Dev inputs. Averaged over layers
10–18, it recovers 78.9% of exact Marathi failures.
At the second entity, the validation-selected probe
reaches 0.933 balanced accuracy (Table 4). These
measurements establish accessible label-relevant
structure in states associated with incorrect deci-
sions.
Similarity-matched negatives strengthen the
comparison.
On the original negative map, a
five-feature string classifier reaches 0.933, match-
ing the probe. Its features are sequence similar-
ity, token and character Jaccard overlap, relative
length difference and final-token agreement. We
rebuild distractors to approximate each positive
pair’s name similarity; matching within 0.05 is pos-
sible for 144/601 items, including 25 test items
(Table 3). Across all 120 test items under this
partially matched map, the question-mark probe
exceeds the string classifier: 0.908 versus 0.796 in
Marathi, a difference of +0.112 [+0.067, +0.163],
and +0.083 [+0.025, +0.142] in English. On the
tightly matched subset, model separation remains
within 0.01 of its original value. The signal ex-
tends beyond these five string features; richer or-
thographic signals and familiarity are not separated
from identity retrieval by this test (Appendix T).
Both classifiers are refitted on the rebuilt train-
ing negatives, and the probe layer is reselected on
validation. This compares their predictive infor-
mation under the same modified task. Where the
string classifier fails, the probe is correct on 65.3%
of Marathi pairs and 75.5% of English pairs. The
comparison therefore tests complementary infor-
mation as well as aggregate accuracy.
Calibration explains much of the behavioural
shortfall. A single constant added to the model’s
margin, selected for balanced accuracy on valida-
tion, raises Marathi entity-level balanced accuracy
from 0.525 to 0.879. This recovers 87% of the
distance to the E2 probe’s 0.933. The probe re-
tains an advantage of +0.054 [+0.017, +0.092] in
Marathi and +0.050 [+0.021, +0.079] in English.
These are comparisons at their selected operating
points, with false-positive rates reported in Table 4.
Substantial discriminative information reaches the
model’s own output score, and criterion placement
accounts for much of its failure to use that informa-
tion in the final answer.
6
Localising causal sensitivity
We replace a Dev–Dev residual state with the corre-
sponding state from a Latin-bearing donor run and
measure the change in m. The causal gate requires
failure in Dev–Dev and success in the donor condi-
tion across paraphrases and answer keys. Held-out
gates contain 12 Marathi, 15 Hindi and 24 English
items. The layer band 10–18 was fixed from a
pilot; intervals resample items, and flip rates are
descriptive at these sample sizes (Appendix I).
The second entity is the strongest site. E2
patching raises margins by 2.66–3.16 across lan-
guages, while E1 has little effect (Table 5). Re-
verse patching damages the working run.
The
matched correct-versus-wrong-entity contrast es-
tablishes an entity-specific component in Marathi;
the Hindi and English intervals include zero. Joint
E1/E2/question-mark patching across layers 12–17
flips 50%, 60% and 71% of held-out Marathi, Hindi
and English items. Matched-negative accuracy re-
mains 100%. At layers 18–20 the joint effect falls
below one margin point, while sensitivity shifts
toward the question mark.
The donor-language control further separates
name form from question language. At the peak
over layers 10–18, an English Lat–Lat donor
changes the Marathi margin by 6.26, comparable
to 5.97 from a Marathi Lat–Lat donor; an English
Dev–Dev donor gives 0.11. These peak values use
a different aggregation from Table 5, so they sup-
port the donor contrast rather than an additional site

6.1
Position, name and computation
Swapping the canonical name and alias preserves
E2 dominance: Marathi E2 patching changes the
margin by 4.79 in the original order and 3.78 af-
ter swapping, while E1 stays within 0.42 of zero
across all six language–order combinations. The
effect follows the second slot, the first position that
can attend to both names (Appendix K). Nonce
names also show E2-over-E1 sensitivity, placing
the finding in relational comparison more broadly
(Appendix U).
Cumulative attention patches transfer more cor-
rection than MLP patches: 5.41 versus 0.51 in
Marathi, with the same ordering in Hindi and En-
glish. These interventions are not an additive de-
composition of residual effects. Multi-head patches
exceed individual-head patches, consistent with dis-
tributed transfer (Appendix J). Exploratory Llama
and Mistral-Small sweeps also show E2-over-E1
sensitivity at different depths and smaller magni-
tudes (Appendices Q and R).

A shared direction transfers correction

Dev and Lat–Lat local gradients have cosine sim-
ilarity 0.37–0.61 across layers 12–17 in Marathi,
against at most 0.004 in magnitude for random di-
rections. Their partial alignment motivates testing
which shared subspaces carry the donor correction
(Appendix L).
