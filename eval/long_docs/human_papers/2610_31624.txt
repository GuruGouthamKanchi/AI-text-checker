ABSTRACT
Instrumental convergence is often invoked as a general reason to expect capable agents to acquire
or express power-seeking subgoals: self-preservation, resource acquisition, deception, or resistance
to oversight which poses an important threat to the society. For LLMs, reinforcement learning is
the first stage of post-training that directly optimizes behavior against an objective; therefore, it is
the stage where convergent instrumental behavior, on IC grounds, likely to appear. Does reinforce-
ment learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) make a model more likely to express instrumental-
convergence behavior, or does this depend on the training regime, evaluation frame, and stage of
post-training? We study this question as a checkpoint-conditioned behavioral trajectory rather than
as a single before/after safety score. Using InstrumentalEval, we evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think across
SFT, DPO, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final RLVR model. The trajectory is non-
monotonic: instrumental rate rises from 25.0% at SFT to 46.1% near RLVR step 200, then falls
to 11.8% at the final checkpoint. Paired item-level tests and step-wise regression support the fi-
nal SFT-to-RLVR reduction (p = 0.015 by paired t-test; p = 0.03 by Wilcoxon signed-rank test;
β2 = −15.34 by step-wise regression). Prompt framing does not explain this pattern: a harmful-
prompt indicator combined with other framing effects explains only 15% of within-checkpoint score
variance on average, and Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) removes a detectable framing direc-
tion without changing the SFT-to-final-RLVR gap. Together with preliminary 32B endpoint results
(35.53% SFT to 22.37% RLVR final), these findings indicate that RLVR does not monotonically
increase instrumental behavior in this setting: the rate rises early, then falls below the SFT baseline.
The result is specific to this model family and benchmark, and judge agreement is moderate, so
absolute rates should be read cautiously.

Introduction
Instrumental convergence describes the tendency of capable, goal-directed agents to pursue similar intermediate goals
across a wide range of objectives. Preserving the ability to act, acquiring resources, avoiding shutdown, and shaping
the environment can all help an agent continue pursuing its objective [16, 1, 2, 21]. This creates a potential connec-
tion between capability and risk: as a system becomes more capable and goal-directed, it may become more likely to
pursue instrumental strategies, including when its objective appears benign. Applying this argument to large language
models requires understanding how goal-directed behavior arises in their training and deployment. Language mod-
els can produce reasoning that justifies deception, resists shutdown, preserves goal integrity, or supports appearing
aligned for strategic reasons [8, 13], including in settings with limited persistence or ability to act on the environment.
Reinforcement learning makes this question particularly relevant because it directly optimizes behavior against an
objective signal. At the outer-alignment level, we can ask whether the reward or verifier specifies the behavior we
want. At the inner-alignment level, we can ask whether the learned model develops internal objectives or heuristics
that diverge from the intended training objective [10]. IC-like behavior provides a possible observable connection
between these questions, while establishing a misaligned inner objective requires further evidence. Reinforcement
learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) provides a useful setting for studying this connection. It is widely used in
post-training reasoning models because it allows optimization against automatically checkable outcomes in mathe-
matics, coding, and other domains with verifiable answers [3, 11, 22]. Its use of task-local verifiers, reference-policy

direction. These findings establish a nonmonotonic relationship between post-training stage and IC-like outputs for
this model, benchmark, and judging protocol, with later RLVR reversing the early increase and reducing the final level
below SFT.

Conceptual Frame
2.1
Instrumental convergence as a conditional thesis
The instrumental-convergence argument begins from a structural observation, certain intermediate states make success
easier. A system that can preserve itself, acquire resources, avoid interruption, or strategically manage oversight may
be better able to pursue its objective [16, 2, 21]. This implies that, under some conditions, capability and goal-
directedness may make such behaviors instrumentally useful.
Recent philosophical work has questioned how direct this inference should be. [20] argue that the timing and dom-
inance of convergent drives may be more constrained than canonical presentations suggest. [5] similarly argues that
instrumental divergence is also natural: agents with different goals may pursue different intermediate strategies. These
critiques do not dissolve the safety concern, but they change the empirical burden.
2.2
InstrumentalEval as a behavioral probe
InstrumentalEval operationalizes instrumental convergence through six subtasks derived from classic convergent-drive
categories [8]. The subtasks ask whether the model expresses reasoning associated with evading shutdown, hacking
computer systems, running copies of itself, hiding unwanted behavior, strategically appearing aligned, and deceptive
behavior. In this paper, we use these subtasks as an output-level probe of IC-like reasoning and evaluate them with a
judge model. The judge prompt for evaluation is available in table 9.
A positive score on any of these subtasks indicates that IC-like reasoning appeared in the output. However, it does not
establish that the model holds a corresponding internal goal or that the behavior would persist across contexts, as this
distinction requires looking inside the model.

2.3
RLVR as a constrained post-training regime
RLVR optimizes a policy using rewards from a rule-based verifier rather than a learned preference model. A simplified
objective is
J (θ) = Ez,y∼πθ(·|x) [fverifier(y, y∗)] ,
(1)

where fverifier evaluates whether a response satisfies a task-specific criterion [23]. In mathematics, this may be symbolic
equivalence; in coding, it may be execution against tests.
OLMo-3-7B-Think is not trained by RLVR alone. Its post-training pipeline includes SFT, DPO, and RLVR check-
points released across the training trajectory [15]. This matters because a final checkpoint can hide intermediate
dynamics. A before/after comparison between SFT and final RLVR would miss whether IC-like behavior rises during
early RLVR and falls later.
KL regularization against a reference policy in DPO structure can limit drift from the pretrained distribution. This
constrained optimization structure is central to our interpretation: RLVR in this instrumental-convergence evaluation
context may not create open-ended power-seeking pressure in the same way as broader RL settings. It may instead
narrow the behavioral distribution around verifier-rewarded reasoning patterns.

3.2
Prompt framing as a nuisance variable
InstrumentalEval prompts vary not only in the drive they test but also in how the scenario is framed. Some prompts
present the relevant behavior in overtly harmful language, and some describe the target in the context of social good.
Prompt framing is the most tractable confound addressed here. The concern comes when safety benchmark scores that
conflate framing sensitivity with behavioral tendencies risk misrepresenting a model’s actual risk profile [19].
To analyze prompt framing effect, we manually label each of the 76 tasks with a binary harmful/neutral framing
indicator Hi, roughly indicating goal-directness of the prompt and inc. These splits contain 41 harmful and 35 neutral
prompts. Because every checkpoint is evaluated on the same items, the framing labels are fixed across checkpoints by
construction.

Experiments and Results
4.1
Results
We asked whether instrumental-convergence behavior is stable across the OLMo-3 post-training trajectory, or whether
the same benchmark items elicit different IC-like outputs at different checkpoints. We first examine the raw checkpoint
trajectory. We then test whether the trajectory is explained by prompt framing, and finally check whether the main
pattern is stable across judge models. Table 1 reports the full checkpoint trajectory; additional per-checkpoint framing
and judge analyses are provided in Appendix A.4.
Table 1 shows how instrumental behavior changes across all evaluated checkpoints. IR begins at 25.00% at SFT,
increases to 38.16% after DPO, and reaches its highest observed value of 46.05% at RLVR step 200. The intermediate
checkpoints show fluctuations around this early increase. Later checkpoints trace a decline, reaching 22.37% at step
800, 17.11% at step 1375, and 11.84% at the final release. These results show that the effect of post-training depends
on the checkpoint at which the model is evaluated: early training increases IC-like outputs, and later RLVR brings
the final model below the SFT baseline. Since the benchmark contains 76 items, a single binary outcome changes IR
by roughly 1.3 percentage points. The confidence intervals and complete checkpoint sequence help place these local
changes in the context of the overall trajectory.

Taken together, these results show that RLVR changes the observed IC trajectory, but not by monotonically increasing
instrumental convergence. In OLMo-3-7B-Think, DPO and early RLVR increase IC-like outputs, while later RLVR
reverses this pattern and the final checkpoint falls below the SFT baseline. Prompt framing is detectable in the score
matrix, but neither the per-checkpoint linear framing analysis nor the INLP projection explains the final reduction.
The result is therefore a behavioral checkpoint-trajectory claim, not a broad safety claim: for this model, benchmark,
and judging protocol, the final RLVR checkpoint produces fewer IC-like outputs than the SFT checkpoint.
4.2
Extension to the larger model
We next examine whether the endpoint reduction also appears at a larger model scale. We evaluate the SFT and final
RLVR checkpoints of OLMo-3-32B-Think, which belongs to the same model family and training pipeline. Table 6
shows that IR decreases from 35.53% at SFT to 22.37% at the final RLVR checkpoint. Applying the paired tests in
Equations 5 and 6 gives p = 0.034 for the paired t-test and p = 0.029 for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. This prelim-
inary evaluation supports an endpoint reduction at both scales within the OLMo-3 family. Establishing whether the
32B model also exhibits the early rise and later decline observed at 7B requires intermediate-checkpoint evaluations.

Discussion
Our checkpoint evaluation shows that IC-like behavior rises early in RLVR training and declines at later checkpoints.
This trajectory suggests that the relationship between optimization and instrumental behavior depends on the training
regime, with the objective, update geometry, reference constraint, and evaluation setting providing possible expla-
nations for the observed pattern. The canonical instrumental-convergence argument concerns sufficiently capable
goal-directed agents, and applying it to verifier-trained language models requires understanding how these conditions
arise in a particular training setting. RLVR may strengthen reasoning while narrowing the distribution of expressed
behaviors, allowing capability and IC-like output expression to move in different directions. From an outer-alignment
perspective, a verifier that rewards task-local correctness may leave important safety constraints unspecified while also
limiting incentives for broad instrumental strategies. For example, correctness on a math or code task provides limited
direct incentive for resource acquisition, self-preservation, or strategic deception. This makes the scope of the verifier
relevant both to the completeness of the reward specification and to the behaviors that optimization encourages. From
an inner-alignment perspective, the lower final IR establishes a reduction in IC-like responses under the benchmark
and judging protocol. The relationship between this reduction and any learned goal-like structures remains an open
question. The strategically appearing aligned subtask makes this question particularly clear: reasoning about appear-
ing aligned to preserve future influence resembles the behavior associated with deceptive alignment [10, 6]. A decline
on this subtask could reflect reduced deceptive-compliance reasoning or a lower probability of expressing that reason-
ing. Mechanistic analysis of the checkpoint sequence would help distinguish these explanations and assess whether
the observed behavior corresponds to an internal objective. Our findings complement work identifying conditions
under which RL-trained systems express reward-seeking, deception, goal misgeneralization, and ethically problematic
strategies [12, 17, 4, 6, 9]. We examine how such behavior changes along a verifier-based RLVR trajectory in one
open model family, where the early increase is followed by a decline below the SFT baseline. This checkpoint-level
analysis also extends the question motivating InstrumentalEval in [8], who ask whether RL-based language models are
more likely to pursue instrumental goals. Tracking released checkpoints reveals training dynamics that endpoint com-
parisons can obscure. Existing work on RLVR provides possible mechanisms for these changes. Sparse update results
suggest that RL fine-tuning can concentrate changes in limited subnetworks [14], and work on reasoning capacity sug-
gests that RLVR can increase the probability of solutions already available to the model [24]. If IC-like continuations
have relatively low probability in the SFT distribution, later RLVR could further reduce their sampling probability
while preserving the underlying representations. In this sense, our evaluation identifies checkpoint intervals in which
mechanistic analysis may be especially informative. The early-RLVR peak and subsequent decline provide concrete
settings for studying how changes in expressed behavior relate to changes within the model.

Limitations
Our evaluation has several limitations that are important for interpreting these results. We primarily study OLMo-3-
7B-Think, and while the preliminary experiments with a larger model in Section 4.2 partially address the question of
scale, both models belong to the same family. The observed trajectory may therefore depend on the model parameters,
scale, or release trajectory, and replication on RLVR-trained models from other labs is needed to understand how
broadly the result applies. Similarly, InstrumentalEval provides a focused behavioral probe through 76 tasks across
six convergence categories, but this limits statistical power and captures only part of the space of IC-relevant behavior.
In this sense, our evaluation provides evidence about a particular behavioral expression of instrumental convergence
rather than a complete test of the thesis. Our framing controls are also limited to linearly recoverable structure: the R2
and INLP analyses support the conclusion that a simple linear framing direction does not explain the main trajectory,
but do not rule out nonlinear effects, interactions between framing and subtask, or differences in how judges respond
to prompt tone. An analysis stratified by subtask or using a richer classification of prompt tone could help address
these questions. Moreover, average inter-judge agreement is moderate at 68.75%, with stronger agreement at the final
RLVR checkpoint and weaker agreement near the early-RLVR peak. This supports the direction of the final result,
although absolute IR values should be interpreted cautiously and would benefit from human validation on a stratified
sample. Finally, our evaluation does not establish whether the verifier is outer-aligned. A narrow verifier may limit
opportunities for broad instrumental strategies while failing to specify important safety constraints, and we measure
only one behavioral consequence along the released checkpoint sequence. Comparing alternative verifier designs is
therefore a useful direction for understanding which aspects of the observed trajectory are specific to this verifier and
which may generalize to RLVR more broadly.
Broader Impact Statement
This work should not be used as evidence that RLVR-trained systems are broadly safe. Our result is limited to one
model family, one benchmark, and one judging protocol. It suggests that IC-like behavior can decrease along a verifier-
based RL trajectory, not that RLVR solves inner alignment, outer alignment, deceptive alignment, or instrumental
convergence in general.
The positive use of the work is diagnostic. Checkpoint-level evaluations can reveal nonmonotonic safety trajectories
that endpoint comparisons miss. We hope this encourages future work to evaluate post-training methods as trajecto-
ries, combine behavioral benchmarks with mechanistic analysis, and treat instrumental convergence as a conditional
empirical phenomenon rather than as a slogan.
