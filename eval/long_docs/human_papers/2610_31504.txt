Structure-preserving reduced-order models of thin panels in supersonic
and hypersonic flow
Miguel A. Aguil´oa,∗
aMorphorm LLC, 100 Sun Avenue Northeast Suite 650, Albuquerque, NM 87109-4670, USA
Abstract
We report structure-preserving reduced-order models of both challenge problems of the
AePW-4 High-Speed Working Group [1]. They are the AFRL/SSC RC-19 clamped thin
panel at Mach 1.92 and the UNSW HyMAX cantilever plate at Mach 5.85. For each problem
one construction gives two models. The reference model is a physics-based Ritz–Galerkin
model of the plate. The learned model keeps the reference model’s exact potential, fits
the remaining generalized forces, and trains only its dissipation. Each model is built from
the data package the working group distributes for its case. Neither package holds a
time-resolved response or repeated runs. There is no ensemble to learn from. The physics
is therefore carried by the form of the model rather than by data. The learned model’s
energy balance holds for any weights by construction. On the trained model, at random
states, its relative residual is 2.2 × 10−15. On RC-19, inviscid shock relations applied to the
package’s wedge geometry and schlieren shock angle predict a pressure ratio of 1.521. The
ratio derived from the pressure-sensitive paint that Brouwer et al. [2] applied to the rigid
panel is also 1.521. On the periodic case we ran the trained model thirty times, each from a
different initial push. The mean dominant frequency is 1.0 % from the 236 Hz measured by
Brouwer et al. [3]. Brouwer et al. simulated the experiment with their own reduced-order
model. We take one periodic and one chaotic condition from their simulations. At both,
the trained model gives the same regime as their model in all sixteen runs, eight from each
buckled shape. It does so without their model’s 113 Hz spectral peak, which the experiment
does not show. Because the response is chaotic, any model loses a single trajectory after a
horizon set by the largest Lyapunov exponent. Measured displacements therefore correct
the model through one more generalized force, which enters the same energy balance. We
tested this correction in a twin experiment at the experiment’s 5 kHz measurement rate.
With an offline smoother it holds the panel state to 0.0125 thicknesses without noise and
0.0352 with 10 µm of noise. On HyMAX, with nothing fed back, the learned model tracks
the reference model’s trailing-edge displacement for 65 ms to a relative error of 3.67 %.

1. Introduction
Thin panels on high-speed vehicles are loaded by the flow, heated by it, and driven by it
into flutter [4, 5, 6]. A heated clamped panel buckles, and above the buckling temperature
it has two stable buckled shapes. Each sits in a well, and a well is a minimum of the panel’s
potential energy. In supersonic flow it can oscillate about one of them, switch between them,
or do both in no repeatable order [7]. Which of these it does depends on the temperature
rise and on the pressure across it. A design has to know the answer over the whole operating
envelope, not at one point. High-fidelity coupled simulation resolves one condition at a
cost of hours. Linear reduced-order models are affordable over the envelope but cannot
represent a panel that crosses between buckled wells. A learned surrogate can be affordable
and nonlinear. However, a surrogate whose energy change does not equal the work done on
it cannot be defended to a reviewer. Nothing in its training says where it stops being valid.
The AePW-4 High-Speed Working Group [1, 8] poses two challenge problems that test
these needs directly. The AFRL/SSC RC-19 clamped thin panel at Mach 1.92 has periodic
and chaotic responses and a case with an impinging shock. The UNSW HyMAX cantilever
plate sits at Mach 5.85 under an impinging shock in a short-duration facility. The group’s
stated objectives include assessing coupled tools predictively. They also include identifying
the gaps in high-speed experiments that future tests should close.
This work builds learned reduced-order models of both problems. Three observations
organize it. First, the data packages the working group distributes for the two problems
hold statistics, not trajectories. The RC-19 package supplies measured mode shapes, time-
averaged pressures and the nine points at which the response is reported. It holds no
time history and no repeated runs. The response statistics at those points are published
separately [3, 9]. A method that learns its physics from examples needs an ensemble that
does not exist. The physics must therefore be carried by the model’s form, leaving little
to learn. Second, the panel’s non-conservative forces are linear in its state. Piston theory
and Rayleigh damping are both linear. Their coefficients are therefore fitted once, by least
squares, and frozen, and only the dissipation is trained. Third, the response is chaotic. Any
model of it, however accurate, loses a single trajectory within a horizon set by the largest
Lyapunov exponent λ. That exponent is the exponential rate at which two neighboring
2


trajectories separate. Tracking beyond that horizon needs measurement. The correction
that measurement applies to the model must enter the energy balance as a generalized
force, like the aerodynamic force. Our working hypothesis is the following.
Consider a reduced-order model of a thin panel in high-speed flow, built from the
data a challenge-problem package provides and from published constants. Let it
have four properties.
(i) Its elastic and membrane energy is expressed in the panel’s measured mode
shapes. Its nonlinear stiffness force is exactly the gradient of that energy.
(ii) Its aerodynamic and structural damping forces enter as generalized forces,
with coefficients fitted by least squares and then held fixed.
(iii) Its only trained term is a dissipation that can remove energy, but never
add it, for every value of its parameters.
(iv) It is advanced in time by a discrete-gradient step. That step replaces the
energy gradient by its average along the step, which balances energy exactly
at every step.
Such a model will satisfy four claims.
(P1) Its energy changes exactly by the work of its generalized forces minus its
dissipation, for any trained weights and under any measurement correction.
(P2) At the simulation operating conditions of Brouwer et al. [3], it reproduces
the regimes of their reduced-order model. It does so outside its training
temperature and from both buckled wells.
(P3) It matches the experiment’s response statistics wherever the package sup-
ports a comparison.
(P4) It tracks the chaotic panel beyond its tracking horizon, given observations
at the rate and points the experiment already uses.
We test the hypothesis on both challenge problems, with each claim’s acceptance
conditions fixed before its run. RC-19 carries the weight of the test, because its package
supports comparisons with experiment. P1 is checked at random states with amplitudes
from 10−6 to 106, and along every trajectory. P2 is tested at the two simulation operating
conditions of Brouwer et al. [3], sixteen runs each, eight from each well. P3 is tested against
the measured response frequency and displacement standard deviation. P4 is tested in a twin
experiment at the published sampling rate. There the reference model generates synthetic
observations of its own trajectory. HyMAX then tests whether the same construction
serves a second configuration unchanged. Every prediction was registered before its run.
Four of the nine registered predictions broke (Table 11), and they are reported as findings
(Section 9). P1 and P2 held. P3 held for the frequency, and failed for the displacement
standard deviation on the attached-shock case, which Section 7.7 traces to data the package
lacks. P4 held in the twin experiment and awaits a measured time series.

Place in the series. The earlier reports in this series [10, 11, 12] reduce fields on a mesh
with Whitney forms. These are finite-element bases whose discrete derivatives mirror the
continuous ones. This property is called the discrete de Rham structure. Thin panels are
better described by their mode shapes. This report therefore uses a modal Hamiltonian
reduction, in which the energy is written in the amplitudes of the mode shapes. It keeps
the same commitments: exact energy accounting, dissipation that cannot add energy, and
discrete balances that hold exactly.
Contributions. This work contributes:
1. A reference model built on the RC-19 measurements (Sections 5.1 and 5.2). Its basis
is the seven mode shapes measured on the panel before the test, made orthogonal by
L¨owdin’s symmetric construction. Of all orthonormal sets, it gives the one nearest to
the measured shapes. Its membrane stiffness comes from solving the in-plane elasticity
problem for every pair of modes. Its temperature field comes from the surface pressures
measured by fifteen Kulite transducers on a rigid panel. Those pressures give the
adiabatic wall temperature, which is the temperature an insulated wall reaches under
the boundary layer. A fin equation then gives the panel’s temperature distribution.
It models steady conduction from the heated panel into its cooler frame, calibrated
on the panel and frame thermocouples.
2. A learned model whose energy balance holds for any parameters (Proposition 2). The
rate of change of the panel’s energy always equals the power of the generalized forces
minus the power the learned dissipation absorbs. Training cannot violate this balance,
because it follows from the form of the model. The dissipation is positive definite
for every parameter value. It can therefore only absorb energy (Lemma 2). The
generalized aerodynamic force is fitted by least squares and then frozen (Section 5.8).
On the trained model, at random states with amplitudes from 10−6 to 106, the worst
relative residual of the balance is 2.2 × 10−15.
3. A discrete-gradient step for the learned model (Proposition 3). The training record is
a family of reference model trajectories, generated with the average-vector-field (AVF)
discrete gradient. The generalized-α method is the common alternative. The two
steps carry different truncation errors and numerical damping. A model integrated
differently from its data drifts from them even when its forces are exact. Proposition 3
gives the learned model the same discrete-gradient step that generated its training
record. Each such step removes that drift and balances the model’s energy exactly,
for any trained weights. The trained models reported here were integrated with
the generalized-α step (Section 5.9). The discrete-gradient step applies to them
unchanged.
4. Assimilation as a generalized force (Section 5.11). Measured displacements at the
instrumented points correct the model through one more generalized force. It is
proportional to the error in the displacement and in its rate. Each time step still
balances the panel’s energy exactly, with the work of this force reported as a separate
4


term (Corollary 1). The two feedback gains are not tuned. They are set from the
linearized equation for the model-to-truth error so that its largest Lyapunov exponent
is negative. The nine published measurement points observe all seven modes: no
combination of modes is invisible at all nine points. In a twin experiment at the
published 5 kHz rate, the correction with an offline smoother holds the model’s state
to 0.0125 thicknesses without noise. A causal hold of the last sample reaches 0.325
(Section 7.6).
5. Comparisons as statistics (Sections 7.2 and 7.3). A thirty-member ensemble of the
trained model, at the periodic case’s operating condition, places the mean dominant
frequency at 238.4 Hz. That is 1.0 % from the measured 236 Hz. At the simulation
operating conditions of Brouwer et al., it returns the regimes of their reduced-order
model. It does so without that model’s 113 Hz peak, which the experiment does not
show either. We also measure how an ensemble mean depends on the range of initial
perturbations, or kicks, it samples. Every mean is quoted with that range.
6. A measured bound on the aerodynamic enrichment (Section 7.4). The reduced-order
model of Brouwer et al. replaces freestream quantities in piston theory by local ones,
which scales the aerodynamic operators by 5.4 %. Rather than build that enriched
model, we measured what the scaling changes in our reference model. We ran it at
the periodic case’s experimental operating condition in four ensembles of 192 runs
each. They combine unscaled and scaled aerodynamic operators with release from
each buckled well. A two one-sided equivalence test [13] compared the mean dominant
frequency with and without scaling. It declares equivalence when the 95 % interval of
the difference of means lies inside a band, here ±2 %, registered before the runs. The
mean frequency moved 0.40 % in the lower well and 0.51 % in the upper well, inside
the band. The fluctuation amplitude moved 2.4 %. Its elasticity, the percent change
in amplitude per percent change in the coefficients, is 2.4/5.4 = 0.44.
7. A measured data requirement for the attached-shock case (Section 7.7). Forced at
the measured pressure fluctuation level, the reference model’s displacement standard
deviation there is 0.06 thicknesses. The measured value is 0.943, about sixteen times
larger. Ruling out every admissible cause by measurement isolates one remaining
mechanism. That mechanism is the dependence of the surface pressure on the panel’s
deformed shape. Testing it requires time-resolved surface pressure, or a computation,
on the deformed panel, which the package lacks.
8. One construction across two configurations (Section 8). The HyMAX model uses the
RC-19 construction unchanged. With no observations fed back, it tracks the reference
model’s trailing-edge displacement for 64.9 ms. Its relative root-mean-square error
over that window is 3.67 %. It also detects, rather than assumes, which modal bases
its data support. A six-mode basis is declined, because its whitened coordinates keep
only eleven of its twelve state directions.

2. The challenge problems and what their packages hold
Both packages are distributed by the working group and marked Distribution A. They
are used as distributed, and no number in this report is fitted to a measured flutter response.
Two structural calibrations are stated where they are made: the thermal effectiveness on
the buckling temperature rise, and the HyMAX bending stiffness on the first frequency.
2.1. AFRL/SSC RC-19 clamped thin panel
The facility and the panel. The experiment [14, 15, 1] runs in a blowdown supersonic tunnel
with a test section 131.1 mm high and 152.4 mm wide, at a nominal Mach number of 2.0.
RANS simulations in the package give 1.92 ± 0.02 at the panel [1]. The panel is a steel
plate of 253.9 × 127 × 0.635 mm, set flush in the tunnel floor (Figure 1). A frame of
304.7 × 152.4 mm, 12.85 mm thick, clamps it on all four edges. A sealed cavity behind the
panel holds the cavity pressure pc, which sets the static pressure differential across it. The
incoming boundary layer is 8.6 mm thick. In the heated runs the stagnation temperature is
about 388 K at a stagnation pressure of 346 kPa. The flow heats the panel faster than its
frame. The panel’s temperature therefore rises above the frame’s. The frame constrains
the panel’s thermal expansion, which puts the panel into compression. It buckles once the
rise exceeds about 5.6 K, which is the flat panel’s buckling temperature rise reported in [3].
One thermocouple on the panel and one on the frame measure their temperatures. The
package’s temperature rise ∆T is the difference between the two readings.
The cases. The package defines three cases. Two have no shock on the panel and are
labeled periodic and chaotic. The periodic case runs at a cavity pressure of 50.4 kPa and
a temperature rise of 12.76 K. Brouwer et al. [3] report its measured dominant frequency
as 236 Hz. The chaotic case runs at 51.5 kPa and a temperature rise of 14.74 K. Its cavity
pressure is 0.49 kPa above the RANS reference surface pressure ps = 51.0 kPa of [3]. Brouwer
et al. [3] report its measured dominant frequency as 257 Hz. The third case, attached-shock,
mounts a wedge of length 110.7 mm and height 7.8 mm in the test section. Its leading edge
is 86 mm upstream of the panel’s. The wedge places an oblique shock, measured at 35.7◦in
the schlieren, on the panel. That case runs at a cavity pressure of 68.9 kPa and is labeled
periodic.
The instrumentation and what the package holds. For each case the package supplies the
geometry of the tunnel, the wedge and the panel, and the operating conditions with their
error bars. It supplies seven mode shapes measured before the test on an eleven by eleven
grid, with their frequencies and damping (Figure 2). The panel’s edges were free during
these modal measurements. It supplies the time-averaged surface pressure measured on a
rigid panel by 15 Kulite transducers and 28 scanner taps. For the attached-shock case it adds
the schlieren and a pressure-sensitive paint field [2]. The response was measured by digital
image correlation at 5 kHz [1]. The package defines nine extraction points (Figure 1b). It
does not hold the mean and the standard deviation of the displacement at those points. We

2.2. UNSW HyMAX cantilever plate
The facility and the plate. The HyMAX experiment of UNSW Canberra [16] runs in the
TUSQ Ludwieg tube of the University of Southern Queensland. The test flow is a free
jet of compression-heated air at Mach 5.85. A forebody 100 mm long carries a flat plate
of 6061-T6, 130 × 80 × 2 mm (Figure 3a). The plate is attached by a bolted joint at its
hinge line and is free at its trailing edge, 230 mm from the forebody’s leading edge. The
shock is produced by a static shock generator [17]. The generator is a wedge 110 mm long
with an included angle of 5◦. Its leading edge sits 10 mm upstream of the forebody’s and
40 mm above its surface. Its angle of attack is αg = −3◦. The package defines the flow
deflection as αg plus the wedge angle, −3◦+ 5◦= 2◦. That is the case the working group
makes mandatory, and 10◦is optional. The inviscid oblique shock from its leading edge
stands at 11.16◦. That is the weak-branch solution of the oblique-shock relation [18] for a
2◦deflection at Mach 5.85. The shock meets the plate’s plane 40 mm/ tan 11.16◦= 203 mm
behind the generator’s leading edge. That is 93 mm behind the hinge, beside the first of the
two shock positions the training trajectories use, xs = 91 and 117 mm. The flow lasts about
200 ms, and its freestream pressure is not stationary over the first 90 ms of that window
(Figure 3b).
What the package holds. The package supplies one run, with its freestream pressure and
temperature and the geometry of both configurations. It holds the surface pressure at four
transducers sampled at 50 kHz, with their calibrations. Three sit on the forebody at 40, 50
and 60 mm, and one on the plate near its trailing edge at 220 mm. Schlieren images show
the flexible plate and a rigid one at shock-generator angles of 2 and 10◦. The transducer on
the plate sits under the impinging shock and reads about twice the freestream pressure.
The one structural measurement is the first frequency, 83 Hz [16]. Xu and Dowell [19]

3. Background and related work
Panel flutter and its reduced-order models. Panel flutter has been studied for decades, with
first-order piston theory [20, 21] as its standard aerodynamic model [4, 22]. Nonlinear panel
flutter at supersonic and hypersonic speeds is reviewed in [5], and its aerothermoelastic
setting in [6]. A heated buckled plate in flutter can be chaotic, as Dowell showed for a
deterministic autonomous model [7]. Reduced-order models of geometrically nonlinear
structures are reviewed in [23]. The RC-19 experiments that the first challenge problem
draws on are reported in [14, 15, 2].
The reduced-order model of Brouwer et al.. Brouwer et al. [3] built a reduced-order model
and the simulations that accompany the RC-19 experiment. Their model projects a von
K´arm´an plate on finite element modes and loads it with piston theory corrected for the
boundary layer [24]. It references the static load to the computed surface pressure and damps
the panel in Rayleigh form. It reproduces the published regimes, with two qualifications the
authors state. Their periodic simulation required a cavity pressure 4.6 % from the measured
one and a temperature rise 44.5 % from it. They attribute both offsets to the distribution
of the temperature rise. Their reduced model also shows a spectral peak near 113 Hz that
the experiment does not show. They establish that the response is chaotic with a positive
largest Lyapunov exponent, and report how it grows with temperature. The exponent is
computed from their reduced model’s trajectories by the linearization of their Equations 19
to 23. Our reference model follows their Sections 2.2 and 5 closely, and the differences are
stated in Section 5.
The other working-group analyses. Piccolo Serafim et al. [9] correlate computations with
the RC-19 measurements in the presence of an impinging shock. Xu and Dowell [19] model
the HyMAX plate computationally, and Currao et al. [16] report the HyMAX experiment,
its response and its first structural frequency. Ganti et al. [25] couple a wall-modeled
large-eddy simulation to a finite element plate for a cantilevered aluminum panel at Mach
2.11. That panel overhangs a backward-facing step and is not the RC-19 panel. Their
simulated response frequency matches the measurement they compare against to within
1 Hz. Brouwer [8] summarizes the working group’s cases and its first round of results.
Structure in learned models. A learned model can be made to respect a physical structure in
two ways. The structure can be added to the objective as a penalty, as in physics-informed
networks [26]. It then holds to the degree the penalty weight buys, and only where the
penalty was sampled. Alternatively, it can be built into the parameterization, and then
it holds for every value of the parameters. Examples are Hamiltonian networks [27], port-
Hamiltonian models [28] and port-Hamiltonian networks [29]. The models of this report
are of the second kind. The panel is open: the flow does work on it, and the work can have
either sign. The port-Hamiltonian form accounts for that work exactly instead of excluding
it. The average-vector-field discrete gradient [30, 31, 32] carries the accounting through
each time step exactly.
