Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have shown strong potential for video anomaly
detection, but dense frame-level inference makes them impractical for real-time deploy-
ment. Existing acceleration commonly relies on uniform sampling, which ignores con-
tent dynamics and can either waste computation on static segments or miss localized
anomalies. We propose MoG-VLM a motion-adaptive anomaly detection framework that
leverages motion purely as a lightweight control signal to decide when VLM inference
is necessary. To this end, we introduce Temporal Gradient Magnitude (TGM) which
performs content-aware sampling to concentrate computation on informative regions
while avoiding redundant processing. We further introduce a hierarchical dual-stream
design in which dense multi-frame VLM reasoning is activated only around frames
with elevated anomaly likelihood, via anchor-triggered context-window expansion with
early termination. A fusion module then reconciles these instantaneous semantic cues
with temporal action dynamics. Across four benchmarks, our method achieves 91.91%,
84.30%, 87.88%, and 78.96% AUC-ROC on XD-Violence, UCF-Crime, MSAD, and
ShanghaiTech, respectively, establishing new state of the art on three benchmarks un-
der the zero-shot, training-free setting. On XD-Violence, we improve over the previ-
ous best result by +0.57% while delivering a 1.31× inference speedup. These results
demonstrate that content-aware allocation of VLM capacity, processing fewer frames
more intelligently rather than all frames uniformly, is a more effective axis of progress
than uniformly densifying VLM inference across frames. Code is available on GitHub:
https://github.com/kdawoud91/MoG-VLM.

damental question: Starting from a coarse uniform sampling baseline, can we adaptively
allocate additional computation only when video dynamics demand it to achieve real-time
VLM-based anomaly detection? Addressing this question requires moving beyond accuracy-
centric evaluation toward a holistic framework that explicitly balances detection performance
with computational efficiency. Specifically, there exists no prior work that systematically
investigates adaptive mechanisms for content-aware resource allocation in vision-language-
based anomaly detection, nor sufficient analysis of the trade-offs between computational cost
and video anomaly detection performance.
In this work, we introduce a motion-adaptive anomaly detection framework that funda-
mentally reimagines how computational resources are allocated across video content. Unlike
classical approaches that employ motion as a direct classification feature [7, 8, 17, 19], we
take advantage of motion purely as a lightweight control mechanism, a semantic activation
signal that determines when VLM inference is necessary. We introduce TGM analysis to
establish a motion quantification mechanism, thereby dynamically adjusting the sampling
density based on detected temporal dynamics. We further introduce an anchor-triggered hi-
erarchical refinement mechanism that preserves computational efficiency while recovering
fine-grained temporal context. Adaptively sampled frames that exhibit high anomaly con-
fidence serve as anchor points, triggering progressive multi-window analysis, ensuring that
dense coverage is applied exclusively where anomalous patterns emerge. In parallel, we
introduce a complementary temporal stream that captures action dynamics through video-
language modeling. This branch processes non-overlapping temporal windows, generating
video-level semantic descriptions that encode motion trajectories and behavioral sequences.
The two pathways converge through adaptive scoring, combining frame-level instantaneous
detections with temporal-level behavioral context into unified predictions.

Related Work
Video anomaly detection (VAD) aims to temporally localise frames containing events that
deviate from expected behaviour. Early supervised methods required frame-level annota-
tions [21], while weakly-supervised formulations reduced the annotation burden to video-
level labels through multiple-instance learning (MIL) [4, 21, 25, 26, 27]. The emergence of


4

large vision–language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) has opened a new
paradigm for VAD by inferring anomaly scores from natural-language scene descriptions at
test time, without gradient-based training. LAVAD [36] pioneered this direction by caption-
ing each frame with a frozen VLM, summarising temporal windows with an LLM, and refin-
ing anomaly scores through cross-modal similarity. Despite strong results, its uniform pro-
cessing of every sampled frame regardless of scene content leads to 71.4 GPU-hours on the
XD-Violence test set alone, allocating identical computation to static backgrounds and fast-
evolving anomalous events alike. Subsequent work has focused on reasoning quality rather
than efficiency. Unified-reasoning [12] introduces a score-based gate that triggers a second
LLM pass with automatically extracted anomaly-tag priors for ambiguous videos, raising
AUC-ROC to 84.28% on UCF-Crime and 91.34% on XD. Yet the underlying sampling stays
content-blind, a fixed stride of 16 frames is applied uniformly, expending VLM inference
on static footage regardless of scene dynamics. Other VLM-based methods relax the strictly
zero-shot constraint. VERA [35] steers a frozen VLM with verbalized guiding questions
optimised from labelled data, without modifying model weights, though the prompt optimi-
sation itself requires an annotated training set. Holmes-VAU [38] and Hawk [22] instruction-
tune multimodal LLMs on anomaly clips to produce rich explanations, but they remain tied
to seen anomaly types and incur high annotation costs. STPrompt [30] learns spatio-temporal
prompts under weak class-level supervision. AnyAnomaly [1] proposes customisable VAD
by casting detection as context-aware visual question answering with a frozen LVLM, and
EventVAD [20] combines graph architectures with multimodal LLMs through temporal rea-
soning. The literature has overwhelmingly prioritised detection accuracy [1, 20, 39] over
computational efficiency, leaving the deployment feasibility of VLM-based VAD unclear.
Among the methods discussed above, only Unified [12] reports timing information. This
gap is significant: real-world video systems must process hours of continuous footage, and
the practical feasibility of VLM-based anomaly detection depends critically on throughput,
not solely on AUC-ROC. The principle of allocating computation only where content de-
mands it has a long history in video understanding. In action recognition, AdaFrame [32]
trains a policy network to adaptively select informative frames, SCSampler [10] routes only
salient clips to a heavy 3D backbone, AR-Net [15] learns per-frame resolution selection,
FrameExit [9] conditions early-exit decisions on intermediate features, and LiteEval [31] al-
ternates between a coarse and fine network via a gating module. The shared design pattern
is a two-stage architecture: a cheap first pass produces a saliency signal, and a more ex-
pensive pass is activated selectively. Our framework inherits this two-stage philosophy but
differs in three respects. First, our gating mechanism is entirely training-free, we threshold a
multiscale Temporal Gradient Magnitude (TGM) via adaptive z-scores, requiring no learned
parameters, whereas the methods above learn sampling policies from labelled data, an im-
practical requirement in anomaly detection where positive examples are scarce. Second, we
target untrimmed footage spanning minutes to hours rather than trimmed classification clips,
where the informative-to-static ratio is far more extreme. Third, motion serves purely as a
control signal that decides when VLM inference is invoked, it does not enter the anomaly
score itself, avoiding the conflation of motion salience with anomaly likelihood.

To identify temporal regions exhibiting anomalous motion patterns, we employ adaptive
z-score normalization over a sliding exponential moving average (EMA) of TGM statistics.
This approach enables context-aware anomaly detection that adapts to scene-specific activity
levels while remaining sensitive to deviations from normal behavior.

Experiments
4.1
Datasets & Evaluation Metrics
XD-Violence Dataset [28]. XD-Violence (XD) is a large-scale anomaly detection bench-
mark that contains 800 videos for testing, covering six violence categories. UCF-Crime
Dataset [21].
UCF is a large-scale anomaly detection benchmark with 290 untrimmed
videos for testing across 13 categories. MSAD [40] is a recent multi-scenario benchmark
offering 360 test videos across 14 scene types with varied camera perspectives. Shang-
haiTech Dataset [14]. ShanghaiTech contains 107 testing videos with abnormal events cap-
tured from fixed cameras. Given its scale and category diversity, we anchor our ablation and
efficiency analysis on XD-Violence and report supporting numbers on the remaining bench-
marks. Evaluation Metrics. We report frame-level AUC-ROC, the standard threshold-
independent metric for video anomaly detection.
4.2
Implementation Details
Motion-Adaptive Sampling. We extract frames at a baseline rate of 1 FPS to establish
coarse temporal coverage. For TGM computation, we employ multiscale temporal analysis
with window sizes W = {1,2,4} frames to capture motion patterns at different temporal
scales. Adaptive z-score anomaly detection uses an exponential moving average (EMA) de-
cay factor of α = 0.95 for baseline statistics tracking, with anomaly threshold θanom = 2.3
standard deviations. When motion anomalies are detected, we increase sampling density
to rhigh = 3 FPS within a temporal window of ∆t = 1 seconds around the detected frame.
Vision-Language Models. We employ Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct for frame-level captioning
and VideoLLaMA3-7B for temporal-level processing, where each input consists of nframes =
10 frames uniformly sampled from a non-overlapping 64-frame window at the video frame
rate. Language-Based Anomaly Scoring. For both frame-level and temporal-level, we
employ Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. The scoring prompts employ chain-of-thought reasoning,
instructing the model to first analyze the caption content, then assess anomaly likelihood on a
