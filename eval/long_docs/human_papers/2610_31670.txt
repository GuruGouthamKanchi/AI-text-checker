Sensorimotor Alignment:
A Platonic Proxy for Embodied Policy Success
Yunfei Xie1, Howard H. Qian1, Shiyi Lan2, Kaiyu Hang1, Chen Wei1†
1Rice University,
2NVIDIA
Abstract
Robotic foundation models are typically evaluated through costly closed-loop rollouts, which
motivates cheap offline proxies for model capability. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis
(PRH) [20] suggests that as representations improve, different modalities such as vision and lan-
guage converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. We ask whether motion trajectories
form another such modality: as embodied models become more capable, should their sensory
representations converge toward the structure of physical motion? To examine this, we propose
Sensorimotor Alignment (S2), a training-free offline metric that measures how well a model’s
sensory representation aligns with the geometry of physical motion. We find that S2 correlates
positively with downstream robot task success across embodied model families, making it
a low-cost offline proxy for prioritizing models before closed-loop evaluation. S2 can also
identify promising vision-language model (VLM) backbones even before costly fine-tuning into
vision-language-action models (VLAs). Beyond this correlation, S2 offers representation-level
explanations for phenomena observed in VLAs and world action models (WAMs). With S2, we
extend PRH to embodied models and make sensorimotor alignment empirically measurable,
offering insight into why an embodied model succeeds beyond its closed-loop success rate.

1. Introduction
Robotic foundation models increasingly build on large pretrained vision-language models [3, 4],
video models [48], and world action models [5, 14, 27]. Yet selecting and evaluating these
models remains expensive: the most reliable test is still closed-loop rollout in simulation [29, 32]
or the real world [1], where evaluation is slow, noisy, and benchmark-dependent. This expense
motivates low-cost offline proxies for robotic capability.
A natural approach is to use existing perception-side proxies, such as visual question
answering scores for VLMs [30, 55] or pixel-level video quality for world models [17, 22, 50].
However, these metrics measure passive perception or generation quality, not whether a
representation is organized for action [23, 34, 45, 57]. Two scenes may look visually different
but require similar robot motions; conversely, two visually similar scenes may demand different
trajectories. For robot task success, a model must produce motions of different tasks even for
scenes alike. This requires aligning the visual representation with the motor representation.
This perspective connects robotics to the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [20],
which argues that as models of different modalities scale and solve diverse tasks, they are
converging to a shared statistical model of reality in each of their own representation spaces. For
example, images and text are both projections of a common underlying reality. As vision and
language models scale, they increasingly agree on the relative distances between data points.
We bring PRH to robotics and ask: can physical motion be viewed as another projection of the
world, such that scaled embodied models better align vision and language representations with
the structure of robot trajectories?
We test this prediction of PRH: the more capable an embodied model is, the more closely its
sensory representations align with its motor representation. In other words, episodes nearby in
sensory representation space should also be nearby in motor representation space.
We formalize this as Sensorimotor Alignment (S2), a training-free metric, illustrated in
Figure 1. For each episode in a robot dataset, a model’s image and text features form its sensory
representation, and the recorded end-effector trajectory forms its motor representation. We
measure alignment by comparing each episode’s nearest neighbors in sensory and motor spaces.
More overlap between the two neighbor sets means stronger sensorimotor alignment.
2


In Section 3, we show that S2 correlates positively with downstream robot task success
across model families, in simulated and real-world settings. The correlation is strong for Vision-
Language-Action models (VLAs) in closed-loop manipulation, indicating that models with
higher sensorimotor alignment tend to perform better. S2 computed on raw Vision-Language
Models (VLMs) also correlates with the success of the VLAs later fine-tuned from them, more
strongly than their general visual question answering (VQA) scores do. For World Action
Models (WAMs), S2 is likewise a better indicator of success than video quality metrics such as
FID [17], SSIM [50], and PSNR [18], and for real-robot policies, S2 computed offline correlates
with physical-robot success under consistent conditions. These results suggest sensorimotor
alignment reflects embodied performance better than passive perception or pixel quality.
Prior work has observed several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs only through closed-
loop results, without explaining them at the representation level. We use S2 to supply that
explanation, as shown in Section 4. For example, fine-tuned VLAs often ignore the language
instruction and act mainly on what they see; we find that their task success correlates with the
S2 of their vision features, not their text features.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Inspired by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, we propose a new view of embodiment:
stronger embodied models should align their sensory representations more closely with
their motor representation. To test this, we introduce S2, a training-free offline metric that
measures alignment between sensory and motor representations.
• Across model families, in simulation and on real robots, higher S2 goes with higher task suc-
cess, making S2 a low-cost offline proxy for choosing models before closed-loop evaluation.
• We further find that S2 explains, at the representation level, phenomena that prior work
observed only through closed-loop results, making it a diagnostic tool for understanding
how design choices shape embodied models.
We make sensorimotor alignment measurable, offering a cheap, representation-level account
of embodied model success that closed-loop success rates alone cannot reveal.
2. Measuring Sensorimotor Alignment
Motivated by the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [20], we hypothesize that the more
capable an embodied model is, the more closely its sensory representations align with its motor
representation. Here, sensory representations are the model’s image and text features, and each
episode’s end-effector trajectory is its motor representation.
To make this alignment measurable, we define Sensorimotor Alignment (S2) by constructing
both representations and comparing their neighborhoods, as follows:
Reference dataset. To measure cross-modal alignment, we use the DROID [25] and Bridge-
DataV2 [47] datasets, which pair sensory inputs with physical motions. From a dataset, we
sample 𝑁episodes and compute both representations from each episode. For episode 𝑖, we
randomly sample one image frame and pair it with the task instruction; this image–text pair
forms the sensory input x𝑖. Sampling details are provided in Appendix A.1.

3. Sensorimotor Alignment Correlates with Robot Task Success Rate
The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) [20] argues that as models of different modalities
become more capable, their representations converge toward a shared statistical model of reality.
4


While the evidence for PRH comes from observational data such as images and text, robotics
provides a harder test case: action is not another passive observation, but a physical trajectory
through the world. We test the prediction that models whose image–text representations align
more closely with end-effector trajectories should achieve higher downstream task success.
We quantify this alignment using Sensorimotor Alignment (S2), which compares episode
neighborhoods in sensory and motor space, as defined in Section 2. A positive correlation
between S2 and task success would provide empirical support for this PRH-inspired view of
embodiment. It would also support using S2 as a low-cost offline proxy for deciding which
models to evaluate in closed loop first.
Section 3.1 establishes this positive correlation across 3 model families and real-robot policies.
Section 3.2 shows that S2 correlates with task success more closely than VQA and video-quality
metrics. Section 3.3 confirms that these findings are robust to experimental design choices.

Vision-language-action models (VLAs). We test 12 VLAs against their SimplerEnv [29]

95% confidence interval of [+0.436, +0.912]. Thus, models with higher sensorimotor alignment
tend to achieve higher task success rates. The correlation holds across reference datasets, model
families, scales, and VLA training setups, as shown in Figure 7 and Appendix B.2.
Vision-language models (VLMs). We ask whether S2 computed on a raw VLM, before any
robot fine-tuning, correlates with the task success of the VLA that is later fine-tuned from that
backbone. We compute S2 for 8 raw VLM backbones from VLM4VLA [57] and compare it with

identify promising VLM backbones even before costly VLA fine-tuning. Appendix B.3 lists the
backbones and the S2 settings.
World action models (WAMs). Finally, we test WAMs, which build on video generators
rather than VLMs. We compute S2 for 6 WAMs on the bimanual ALOHA dataset [59] and
compare it with their success rates on RoboTwin 2.0 [32]. For WAMs, the sensory representation
is the video-generator latent used for action decoding, and all 6 models share the same ALOHA
trajectories and S2 settings. S2 again correlates strongly, 𝑟= +0.718 with one-tailed 𝑝= 0.054, as
shown in Figure 3c. Appendix B.4 lists the 6 WAM models and shared evaluation protocol.

Real-robot policies. We test whether the correlation observed in simulation extends to real
robots. Because real-world success rates depend on many factors, such as the task set, robot,

3.2. S2 Correlates with Task Success More Closely Than Alternative Metrics
Prior work asks whether offline scores correlate with downstream task performance.
VLM4VLA [57] examines the correlation between VQA benchmark scores and the perfor-
mance of the resulting VLA policies, finding weak or even negative correlations. For video
models, Tian et al. [45] find that the quality of predicted video agrees inconsistently with down-
stream performance across tasks. A common limitation is that these metrics measure language
understanding or pixel-level video quality, neither of which depends on the motion the robot
must execute. S2 instead compares the nearest-neighbor sets of sensory embeddings and motor
trajectories. As we show below, S2 correlates more strongly with task success rate than any
VQA or video-quality metric we evaluate.
S2 correlates with VLA success rate where VQA scores do not. We compare S2 with
aggregate VQA scores as offline proxies, using the same 8 raw VLM backbones and evaluation
setting as in Section 3.1. We find that VQA scores show virtually no correlation with VLA
success rate, at 𝑟= −0.154 (right panel of Figure 4). S2, by contrast, correlates strongly, at
𝑟= +0.681 with one-tailed 𝑝= 0.031 (left panel of Figure 4).

from a mismatched embodiment, where a sound metric should lose its correlation with task
success. The correlation drops as expected, validating the design of S2.
