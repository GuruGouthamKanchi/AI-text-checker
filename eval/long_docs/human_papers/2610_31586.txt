Abstract:
Accuracy on medical examinations is common evidence offered for the clinical use of language models, yet
a health system that intends to run an open model on its own hardware needs to know considerably more:
whether the model recognizes when it is likely to be wrong, whether medical fine-tuning improves this ability,
whether its correct answers rest on sound clinical reasoning, or whether it actually uses the images it is given.
We examine these questions for four open models of 8 to 27 billion parameters on the Spanish MIR resi-
dency examination, scoring 1,552 questions from eight editions under the exam’s own rules. Answering every
question, the three larger models would rank among roughly the first 600 to 3,000 candidates, with 41 to 43
specialties still open to them. Abstention, however, never pays, because even their least confident answers are
right more often than the 25% break-even. Medical fine-tuning reduced overconfidence at the cost of accuracy,
without improving how well confidence separates right from wrong answers. Moreover, 13–38% of correct
answers rejected a distractor for a clinically unsound reason that confidence did not reflect, and removing or
swapping the provided contextual images left accuracy unchanged.

INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) now pass medical li-
censing examinations, often by a wide margin (Kung
et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2023; Nori et al., 2023;
Kasai et al., 2023), and the Spanish residency exami-
nation is no exception (Guillen-Grima et al., 2023).
Most of this evidence, however, comes from large
proprietary models accessed through closed APIs,
whose weights are not available and whose prompt-
ing, decoding or training data remain outside the
user’s control. A hospital that cannot send patient data
beyond its own infrastructure must thus instead turn to
open models that can run on its own hardware, which
also gives it the ability to inspect, control and audit
them (Riedemann et al., 2024; Thirunavukarasu et al.,
2023). At the scale that fits on a single workstation
GPU of reasonable cost and memory, i.e., models of a

few tens of billions of parameters, accuracy on medi-

question is whether the performance of these models
is good enough to support clinical decision-making or

Yet, a headline accuracy figure answers none of
the questions that a health system would need to ask
before deploying such a model for clinical decision
support or medical education (Hager et al., 2024). It
says nothing about whether the model knows when it
is wrong, whether medical fine-tuning improves that
self-knowledge or merely changes accuracy, whether
a correct answer is reached through reasoning that a
physician would consider clinically sound, or whether
the model actually looks at the image when a case
comes with one. A model can thus be confidently
wrong, or right for the wrong reasons, without either
showing in its score.
The Spanish M´edico Interno Residente (MIR) ex-
amination makes it possible to address all four ques-
tions under realistic conditions.
Because it penal-
izes wrong answers, a candidate’s score depends on
when they choose to answer or abstain; because it al-
locates training locations and specialties by rank, a

score translates directly into a specialty and hospital
where different candidates would be placed under var-
ious degrees of competitiveness; and because physi-
cians publish per-option commentaries on many of its
questions, and about one question in eight includes a
clinical image, it also lends itself to an analysis of rea-
soning and image use. We evaluate four open models,
namely Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Gemma-3-27B and
MedGemma-27B, the last two of which share a base
model and differ only in medical tuning, and address
the following research questions:
RQ1. Scored like a real candidate, where does each
model land in the national ranking?
RQ2. Does medical fine-tuning improve a model’s
judgement of its own uncertainty, or only its ac-
curacy/practical performance?
RQ3. When a model gets the answer right, does it re-
ject the wrong options for reasons that a physician
would consider clinically sound?
RQ4. Do vision language models actually use the
clinical images they are given, or do they answer
instead from the text alone?
Our results show that a score good enough to win a
competitive residency hides substantial weaknesses.
The three larger models would rank among the first
few thousand candidates and could choose almost
any specialty, yet none of the four can profit from
the exam’s penalty by abstaining, since even their
least confident answers are right more often than the
25% break-even. Medical tuning makes a model less
overconfident but also less accurate, and no better at
telling its right answers from wrong ones. Between
13 and 38% of correct answers dismiss at least one
wrong option for a clinically unsound reason, which
the model’s confidence does not reveal, and withhold-
ing or swapping the clinical image leaves accuracy
unchanged.
In summary, our contributions are: (i) a corpus of
2,595 questions extracted from the last twelve official
MIR exams, from 2014 to 2025, with answer keys,
physician commentary, clinical plates and the pub-
lished score-to-rank data of the 2023 and 2024 calls;
(ii) an evaluation under the exam’s own scoring and
allocation rules of four open models of 8 to 27 billion
parameters, with and without medical tuning; (iii) a
controlled comparison of the effect of medical tuning
on calibration, with an exam sat after the model train-
ing cutoff as a contamination control; and (iv) evi-
dence on model reasoning and image use in questions
with physician commentary and clinical plates.

RELATED WORK
LLMs on Medical Examinations. Since ChatGPT
approached the pass mark of the USMLE (Kung et al.,
2023), medical examinations have become the stan-
dard showcase for clinical LLMs. MedQA (Jin et al.,
2021) and its successors track accuracy (Singhal et al.,
2023; Singhal et al., 2025; Nori et al., 2023), and
national examinations such as those of Japan (Kasai
et al., 2023) and Spain (Guillen-Grima et al., 2023)
have since been added.
The MIR in particular is
available as a benchmark through HEAD-QA (Vilares
and G´omez-Rodr´ıguez, 2019; Correa-Guill´en et al.,
2026), and rheumatologists have rated the reason-
ing of LLMs on its rheumatology questions (Madrid-
Garc´ıa et al., 2023). These studies, however, report
accuracy or at most the raw mark, and we are not
aware of any that scores a model under the MIR’s
penalty and allocation rules, or that separates what a
model gains by answering from what it could gain by
abstaining.
Explanations and Reasoning. A correct answer does
not guarantee correct reasoning. GPT-4V was found
to give flawed rationales on over a third of the im-
age challenges it answered correctly (Jin et al., 2024),
and the explanations a model produces do not nec-
essarily reflect the process that actually led to its an-
swer (Turpin et al., 2023). To this end, CasiMedicos
provides physician-written explanations of MIR ques-
tions that cover the wrong options as well as the right
one (Alonso et al., 2024; Goenaga et al., 2024), which
makes it possible to ask whether a model rejects each
distractor for a clinically valid reason. We grade that
reasoning against the physician’s text with an LLM
acting as a judge (Zheng et al., 2023).
Calibration and Abstention. For a model’s confi-
dence to support deferral, it must be both calibrated
(Guo et al., 2017; Naeini et al., 2015) and able to
separate right from wrong answers (Hendrycks and
Gimpel, 2017), a trade-off between coverage and risk
formalized by selective prediction (Geifman and El-
Yaniv, 2017).
LLMs do carry some knowledge of
their own correctness (Kadavath et al., 2022), al-
though the confidence they verbalize tends to be over-
inflated and sycophantic (Xiong et al., 2024). It has
recently been argued that benchmarks which do not
penalize wrong answers reward guessing, and that pe-
nalized scoring would instead reward honest absten-
tion (Kalai et al., 2025).
The MIR provides such
a benchmark, with a penalty that makes it possible
to ask whether a model can identify the questions it
would answer worse than chance, and whether medi-
cal fine-tuning improves that ability.


Images and Contamination.
Multimodal models
have been shown to score well on medical visual
questions while ignoring or misreading the image
(Yan et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2024).
Examination
results are also exposed to training data contamina-
tion (Sainz et al., 2023), for which comparing perfor-
mance before and after a model’s training cutoff is a
standard control (Roberts et al., 2024).

THE MIR EXAMINATION
3.1
Format and Scoring
The MIR is a single, national, multiple-choice exam-
ination sat once a year by every graduating physician
who seeks a specialty training post in Spain.
Fol-
lowing the Ministry of Health, we name each exam
by the year of its call, even though it is sat early in
the following year, so the 2025 exam, for instance,
took place on 24 January 2026. Papers contained 225
scored questions up to the 2018 exam, 175 in 2019
and 2020 and 200 from 2021 onwards, in each case
with 10 reserve questions that replace any question
annulled by the examining commission. Questions
had five options up to the 2014 exam and four there-
after. According to the official scoring rules, a correct
answer scores +3, a wrong answer −1 and a blank 0,
so that the total mark S is
S = 3c−w,
(1)
where c and w are the numbers of correct and incor-
rect answers, respectively. Published results divide
this mark by three, giving the net score S/3 = c−w/3,
i.e., the number of correct answers net of the penalty
for wrong ones. We report net scores throughout.
Since answering a question that one gets right with
probability p is worth 3p −(1 −p) = 4p −1 points,
against zero for a blank, it follows that

regardless of the number of options. With four op-
tions, a random guess therefore has an expected value
of exactly zero, and a blank is better than a guess that
is right less than 25% of the time. The relevant ques-
tion for a model is thus not whether it abstains often
enough, but whether it can identify those questions.
3.2
From Score to Training Post
Candidates are ranked by a total mark that weighs the
examination at 90% and the academic record at 10%,
with the resulting rank determining the order in which
they choose a specialty and a hospital in the national
territory, so that each specialty’s cut-off is the rank of
the candidate who took its last place. Since the Min-
istry of Health stopped publishing per-candidate re-
sult lists after the 2021 exam, the only public informa-
tion consists of the per-specialty summaries that the
trade press compiles from the allocation (ConSalud,
2024; ConSalud, 2025). For each specialty, these re-
port the mean net score and mean rank of its entrants
and, for the 2023 exam, the net score and rank of its
last entrant, together with the cut-off and the hospital
where the last place went.

DATA
Questions. We parsed the official Version 0 book-
lets of the last twelve MIR examinations, from 2014
to 2025, containing 2,595 questions including 120 re-
serves (Table 1). Answer keys were taken from the
answer tables published by CasiMedicos and from
HEAD-QA v2 (Correa-Guill´en et al., 2026), matched
on question text. Where both sources key the same
question they agree on 730 of 773 cases, and 30 of the
43 disagreements fall in the 2017 exam: the four mod-
els’ unanimous answers agree with it on only 36% of
questions, against 85–93% in every other year. We
therefore start the analysis of RQ1, RQ2 and RQ4 at
the 2018 exam, which leaves only four-option papers.
Annulled questions are excluded throughout.
Physician Commentary.
CasiMedicos also pub-
lishes clinician explanations of questions (Alonso
et al., 2024; Goenaga et al., 2024), and for some of
them, the commentary discusses the wrong options
one by one. We use the 123 official questions from
the 2015 and 2017–2021 exams whose commentary
covers at least two options, since only these can reveal
whether a distractor was rejected for the right reason.
Clinical Plates. We extracted the clinical plates from
the image annexes of the 2019–2025 exams, the only
ones available, obtaining 174 questions with a plate,
of which 171 are scorable.
Specialty Labels. CasiMedicos further assigns each
commented question to a specialty, with 619 of the
1,552 scored questions from 2018–2025 (40%) carry-
ing such a label.
Rank Data. We built a score-to-rank curve for the
2023 exam (88 points, covering mean and last en-
trants) and for the 2024 exam (45 points, mean en-
trants only), together with the cut-offs of their 45 and
44 specialties. The 2025 exam has published cut-offs
but no scores yet, and for the 2022 exam, we found
cut-offs but no per-specialty scores.

METHOD
Figure 1 summarizes the study design. Pass A an-
swers every scored question of the 2018–2025 exams
in label mode, and serves RQ1 and RQ2. Pass B asks
for a reason per option on the questions with physi-
cian commentary (RQ3). Pass C repeats the image
questions with the plate shown, withheld or swapped
(RQ4). The four models are evaluated in parallel on
each pass, and all runs are deterministic.
5.1
Models
We evaluate Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B (Qwen
Team,
2025) together with Gemma-3-27B and
MedGemma-27B (Gemma Team, 2025; Sellergren
et al., 2025), whose sizes and precisions are listed in
Table 2. All four were released in 2025, before the
2025 exam was sat. Since MedGemma-27B is built
on Gemma-3-27B through medical tuning, the pair
isolates the effect of that tuning at a fixed scale and
architecture, and as both accept images, they are also
the two models evaluated in Pass C. All experiments
ran on a single 48GB NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000, the
kind of hardware a hospital can realistically own and
operate. Qwen3’s thinking mode was disabled so that
all four models answer directly.
5.2
Answering Protocol
All prompts are written in Spanish, the language of
the examination. The system prompt introduces the
model as a MIR candidate and asks it to answer in the
language of the question, while the user turn contains
the stem and the numbered options. The model is not
told about the scoring rule, since abstention is applied
afterwards on the basis of its confidence (Section 5.3).
Label Mode. In this mode, used for RQ1, RQ2 and
RQ4, the model is asked only for the number of the
correct option. In a single forward pass we read the
probability qk that the model assigns to each option
number k as the first token of its reply and renormalize
it over the options, ˆpk = qk/∑j qj. The answer is then
argmaxk ˆpk and the confidence maxk ˆpk, thus derived
from the model’s distribution over the options.
Generate Mode. In this mode, used for RQ3, the
model is asked for a reply stating, for every option,
whether it is the one to mark together with a one sen-
tence clinical reason, followed by the final answer and
a stated confidence. Asking whether an option is “the
one to mark” rather than whether it is “true” keeps
the verdict unambiguous on negatively phrased stems
(e.g., “which of the following is FALSE”), which
make up about a fifth of the MIR.
5.3
Scoring and Placement (RQ1)
Each full-paper run is scored according to (1) under
three answering policies: always hands in every an-
swer, oracle hands in only the correct ones and thus
sets the ceiling for a perfectly self-aware model, and
calibrated applies the break-even rule of (2). The lat-
ter requires an estimate of p that the confidence can-
not provide, because the largest of four probabilities
summing to one is at least 1/4, so the rule “answer
if maxk ˆpk ≥1/4” would never leave a blank. We
therefore estimate p from the model’s record on its
other seven examinations, sorting their answers by
confidence into ten equal-sized groups and taking as
ˆP(correct) the accuracy of the group into which a new
answer’s confidence falls, so that no examination’s
policy is fitted on its own answers. From these poli-
cies we derive the abstention gain, calibrated minus
always, and the headroom, oracle minus calibrated,
that is, the part of the net score that only a more accu-
rate model could recover.
To place a model in the national ranking, we fit a
score-to-rank curve for each exam through the pub-
lished points and interpolate linearly between the re-
sulting knots.
A specialty is considered open at a
given rank if its last place went to that rank or a later
one, so the most competitive open specialty is the best
choice still available, and the hospital that took its
last place is where it remained available at the end.
Mapping the 95% interval of the net score through the
curve then gives the corresponding range of ranks.

5.4
Calibration (RQ2)
For each model, we report accuracy, mean confidence,
expected calibration error (ECE, with ten equal bins)
(Naeini et al., 2015), the multi-class Brier score over
the option distribution (Brier, 1950), and the area un-
der the ROC curve (AUROC) with which confidence
separates right from wrong answers (Hendrycks and
Gimpel, 2017). Whereas ECE measures whether con-
fidence matches accuracy on average, AUROC mea-
sures whether it ranks answers correctly. The main
contrast is between MedGemma-27B and Gemma-3-
27B on the same questions. As a contamination con-
trol, we also report every measure separately for the
pre-release examinations, whose questions have cir-
culated publicly for years, and for the 2025 exam,
which was sat after all four models had been released.
5.5
Rationale Grading (RQ3)
Each model performs one generate mode run over the
123 questions with commentaries on two or more op-
tions. For every option that has both a physician span
and a model reason, an external judge, i.e., a Llama-
3.3-70B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) in 4-bit pre-
cision, is shown the question, the options, the key, the
model’s verdict on the option, the physician’s text for
it and the model’s reason, and labels the reason as
same if it gives the physician’s key clinical reason,
valid if it gives a different but clinically correct and
sufficient one, or wrong if it is clinically wrong, un-
supported, irrelevant or too vague, or argues for the
wrong verdict. Because the reasons are graded against
the physician’s commentary, RQ3 is limited to the 108
questions on which the physician’s answer matches
the exam key. Our main measure of interest, right an-
swer, wrong reason, is the share of correctly answered
questions on which at least one distractor was rejected
for a reason labelled wrong, with which we compare
the label mode confidence of these answers with that
of correct answers whose distractor reasons were all
sound.
5.6
Image Conditions (RQ4)
The two vision models answer the 171 image ques-
tions in label mode under three conditions, i.e., with
the question’s own plate, with no plate, and with the
plate of a different question. We report accuracy per
condition, the paired difference between the real plate
and each alternative, the share of answers that change,
and the share of questions answered correctly in all
three conditions, for which the text alone is evidently
sufficient.
5.7
Statistical Analysis
All confidence intervals are 95% intervals computed
over questions, so they express how much a measure
would change had a different set of questions been
asked.
Since every answer is deterministic, this is
the only source of variation. For proportions, namely
the accuracies and the RQ3 rate, we use Wilson in-
tervals (Wilson, 1927).
For the measures that are
not simple proportions (e.g., net score, ECE, Brier
score and AUROC), and for the difference between
two values of any measure, we use a percentile boot-
strap with 2,000 resamples of the questions (Efron
and Tibshirani, 1993). Two kinds of difference arise.
When both values come from the same questions, as
in MedGemma-27B minus Gemma-3-27B and in the
real plate minus each alternative, the questions are re-


sampled once and both values recomputed on them
(a paired bootstrap). When they come from different
questions, as in the 2025 exam minus the earlier ones
and in the RQ3 confidence gap, the two sets are re-
sampled independently.
As for significance tests, two models or two im-
age conditions answering the same questions are com-
pared with McNemar’s exact test (McNemar, 1947),
which considers only the questions on which the two
disagree. The RQ3 rates, in contrast, are computed
over each model’s own correct answers and therefore
over different questions, so we compare them with
Fisher’s exact test. Because this comparison involves
all six pairs of the four models, its six p-values are
corrected with Holm’s adjustment (Holm, 1979). No
other test in the paper is adjusted.

RESULTS
6.1
RQ1: Scored Like a Real Candidate
When answering every question, Gemma-3-27B and
Qwen3-14B obtain an average net score per exam-
ination of 131.3 and 131.0, respectively, followed
by MedGemma-27B with 125.5 and Qwen3-8B with
95.8, with a 95% interval of roughly ±16 on any sin-
gle examination. Since the eight examinations differ
in length, from 172 to 221 scored questions, Table 3
reports the net score per 100 questions over all 1,552
of them, which is 67.7, 67.5, 64.7 and 49.4 for the
same four models. Furthermore, Figure 2 and Table 4
show how these net scores map to the 2023 and 2024
score-to-rank curves. In 2023, Gemma-3-27B’s net
score of 142.7 maps to an estimated rank of about
1,628, at which 43 of the 45 specialties were still
open, the most competitive being Oral and Maxillo-
facial Surgery. Qwen3-14B lands close behind with
the same choice, whereas MedGemma-27B would
reach Cardiology and Qwen3-8B, with 22 special-
ties left, Haematology. In 2024 the three larger mod-
els reach ranks between about 600 and 1,400, with
Plastic Surgery or Ophthalmology still open. These
ranges are nonetheless wide, since a single examina-
tion places a model in the 95% interval of roughly
2,000 ranks, and the 2024 curve, which is based
on only 45 points, places any given score markedly
higher than the 2023 curve (Section 8).
Abstention, on the other hand, never helps. The
calibrated policy answers every question on every ex-
amination, so the abstention gain is zero for all 32
model-examination pairs. This is not because confi-
dence is uninformative, since it separates right from
wrong answers with an AUROC of 0.80 for the three

6.2
RQ2: Medical Tuning and
Uncertainty
All four models are overconfident (Figure 4), with a
mean confidence of 0.91–0.99 against an accuracy of
0.62–0.76 and an ECE of 0.21–0.29 (Table 5). Below
a confidence of 0.9, in fact, accuracy falls under 0.5 in
all but one of the bins with at least ten answers. Med-
ical tuning, however, changed the level of confidence.
Over all eight examinations, MedGemma-27B is 2.3
points less accurate than Gemma-3-27B on the same
questions (p = 0.027) and 0.047 less confident, which
lowers its ECE by 0.026 [0.005, 0.045]. Its AUROC,
however, is essentially unchanged, with a difference
of +0.002 [−0.025, +0.028], so it is no better at telling
its right answers from its wrong ones. Given that no
confidence-based abstention pays on this examination
(RQ1), the lower ECE brings no gain in net score,
whereas the loss of accuracy costs about 3 points of
net score per 100 questions.
Moreover, we find no sign of memorization (Fig-
ure 5). On the 2025 exam, which none of the mod-
els may have seen during training, all are at least as
accurate as on the older exams, by 1.2 to 4.9 points
(Table 5), and its mean confidence matches that on
the older exams to within 0.003.
If anything, the
Qwen models rank their answers better on 2025, with
AUROC differences of +0.075 [+0.007, +0.138] and
+0.082 [+0.010, +0.143]. Likewise, the accuracy gap
