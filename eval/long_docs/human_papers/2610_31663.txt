1. Introduction
The pursuit of accurate, efficient, and stable representations lies at the heart of scien-
tific computing. A key task is to construct suitable approximation spaces. Many classical
methods, such as polynomial, Fourier, and wavelet approximation, prescribe a family of
functions and approximate the target through their linear combinations. Random feature
methods (RFMs) [45, 48] follow a similar principle, using fixed sampled features whose
ability to capture the target depends on how well the initialization distribution matches it
[4, 44, 52]. Feed-forward neural networks (FNNs) offer a more adaptive approach: train-
ing the hidden parameters allows the feature functions themselves to adapt to the target,
yielding strong expressive power [46, 59, 80]. RFMs and fully trainable FNNs thus rep-
resent two endpoints, connected by partially trainable networks in which a proportion of
hidden parameters are trainable and the rest randomly fixed. This paper investigates the
phase transition between these endpoints, addressing the question:
How does the performance of partially trainable neural networks vary as the trainable
proportion increases?
To investigate this question, this paper studies the 𝑃-trainable networks, where the same
proportion 𝑃∈[0,1] of neurons in each hidden layer are trainable and the rest are randomly
fixed. Although arbitrary subsets of network parameters can be made trainable, the present
formulation assigns trainability at the neuron level. Our approximation analysis shows that
the effect of the trainable proportion depends on how well the fixed and trainable neuron
groups match the target function class. The corresponding theoretical bounds and numerical
experiments together identify a distinct phase transition pattern: A small trainable group
can offset the mismatch between the initialization distribution and the target function class,
yielding substantial performance gains at small 𝑃followed by diminishing returns, and
greater initialization mismatch sharpens the transition.
Building on the approximation results for two-layer 𝑃-trainable networks with general
activation functions, the analysis of spherical biased-ReLU features provides the explicit
rates that quantify this dependence on initialization. Figure 1 illustrates qualitative agree-
ment between the predicted and observed phase transitions across initialization scales 𝜏≥1.
Within this family, the chosen admissible mismatch constant increases linearly with 𝜏.
Increasing 𝜏generally sharpens phase transitions. More concretely, for 𝑑= 5 and 𝜏= 10, a
trainable proportion of only about 4.1% captures 90% of the total reduction in the approx-
imation bound from 𝑃= 0 to 𝑃= 1.
A structured three-layer example further demonstrates how a small trainable group
uses randomly fixed downstream neurons through nonlinear composition enabled by depth.

1.2. Related Work
The following discussion reviews approximation and learning theory for RFMs, partially
trainable networks, and fully trainable FNNs, as well as studies of pruning and parameter
freezing. For each topic, it explains the connections to our work and the differences in
model construction, analysis, or research objectives.
Approximation Theory for Random Feature Methods. RFMs approximate the infinite-
dimensional feature representation of a kernel using finitely many random features sampled

from a prescribed distribution [48,49]. Early analyses focused on approximating the kernel
itself, particularly with random Fourier feature analysis [67,68]. Subsequent operator-based
work considered general integral feature maps and studied RFMs via a framework of func-
tion approximation and statistical learning. In this framework, the required feature number
is governed by the spectrum and effective dimension of the associated integral operator
[4, 36, 52]. Beyond sampling from the original feature distribution, leverage-based sam-
pling schemes have been developed at both the population and empirical levels to reduce
the required number of features [2,4,34,35]. When the feature map is specialized to ReLU
neurons, approximation bounds have also been established for two-layer networks with
randomly sampled hidden parameters [19,25].
RFMs also have limitations in approximation capacity. Under standard Gaussian inputs,
approximating even a single ReLU neuron can require exponentially many features or expo-
nentially large output coefficients in the input dimension [74]. Approximation lower bounds
derived using ridgelet analysis show that two-layer RFMs with bounded hidden parameters
can fail to achieve universal approximation when the sampling support omits a nonzero part
of the target’s ridgelet spectrum [32].
Our analysis extends the operator framework in [4] from a single sampling distribution
to two independent groups of fixed sizes sampled from separate distributions, giving unified
approximation bounds over 𝑃∈[0, 1]. Beyond the sampling support restrictions studied
in [32], our mismatch factor quantifies insufficient sampling mass relative to the target
RKHS and its effect on the sufficient network width. The bounds further show how a small,
appropriately chosen trainable group can compensate for the initialization mismatch.
Learning Theory for Partially Trainable Networks. Partially trainable networks have
been studied under various constraints on which parameters are optimized. Semi-random
feature models combine randomly fixed and trainable components within individual units
[27], while other work establishes universal approximation for networks with fixed random
weights and trainable biases [71]. Optimization and function-space analyses also provide
convergence and complexity results for models with a randomly fixed first hidden layer
[8, 9]. Related approximation studies investigate representations with a small number of
target-dependent parameters [58,75], as well as multigrade and layer-wise approximation
constructions [77,78].
Our analysis considers a different form of partial trainability: hidden neurons within
each layer are divided into trainable and randomly fixed groups, and we study the resulting
finite-width approximation properties.
Approximation TheoryforFeed-ForwardNeuralNetworks. Classicaluniversalapprox-
imation theorems establish the density of FNNs with suitable activations in spaces of
continuous functions [12, 24]. Quantitative approximation theory relates approximation
accuracy to network size, target regularity, and architectural constraints. For shallow net-
works, Barron-type and variation spaces provide important frameworks for this analysis
[5, 13, 14, 63], with approximation rates and optimality results available under various

assumptions on the activation and error norm [38,61,62,64]. More general approximation-
space frameworks have also been developed for deep networks [20].
Quantitative bounds for Hölder, Sobolev, and Besov classes have been established using
ReLU and related rectified-power networks [21, 37, 40, 55, 59, 60, 69, 73]. Other results
address analytic and bandlimited functions [15,43], as well as smooth functionals [66]. Fur-
ther studies investigate how activation choices and constraints on network size or parameter
magnitude affect approximation capabilities [26,30,31,56,57,76].
Beyond target regularity, approximation theory also examines the role of input geometry
[6,7,53] and architectural structure, including convolutional networks, dynamical-systems
formulations, Transformers, and structured network models [10,11,33,54,79,81]. Connec-
tions with finite element methods and hierarchical basis constructions provide additional
perspectives [22,23].
Our work complements these studies by examining how partial trainability affects
approximation within a given architecture and target function class.
Lottery Ticket Hypothesis and Parameter Freezing. The lottery ticket hypothesis states
that a randomly initialized dense network contains a smaller subnetwork that, when trained
in isolation, can attain performance comparable to the original network [17]. Related studies
show that suitably pruned randomly initialized networks may contain subnetworks capa-
ble of approximating target networks with little or no weight training [39, 50]. Instead,
parameter freezing trains only a selected subset of parameters while holding the remainder
fixed, often at their random initializations [51, 72]. Studies of parameter freezing inves-
tigate parameter selection rules and freezing ratios, often with an emphasis on practical
performance and training efficiency.
Our setting is related to these studies but differs in both construction and objective.
A prescribed proportion of hidden neurons is trainable. The remaining hidden parameters
stay fixed after initialization, while all neurons are retained in the forward computation.
The objective is to study approximation capacity as the trainable proportion varies, rather
than to search for subnetworks or design parameter selection rules.
Organization. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the
model, approximation task and analytical framework. Section 3 presents the general approx-
imation bound, explicit polynomial rates, and the resulting phase transition phenomenon.
Section 4 specializes the analysis to specific spherical biased-ReLU features. Section 5 pro-
vides the structured three-layer illustration. Section 6 reports the numerical illustrations,
and Section 7 concludes the paper. Supplementary proofs are provided in the appendices.
