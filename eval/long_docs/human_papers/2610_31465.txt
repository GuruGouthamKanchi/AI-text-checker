RheOPT: Few-Shot Non-Newtonian Flow Prediction
via Newtonian Pretraining of RheOFormer
Maedeh Saberi,1 Amir Barati Farimani,2 and Safa Jamali1, ∗
1Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering, Northeastern University, Boston, Massachusetts 02115, USA
2Department of Mechanical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213, USA
Despite the advances in numerical simulations, non-Newtonian flow simulations have remained
computationally expensive because the velocity and stress fields must be advanced together through
rate- and history-dependent constitutive dynamics. Neural-operator surrogates can reduce infer-
ence cost after training, but their training data often require the same expensive simulations they
are intended to replace. This makes data efficient learning especially important in regimes where
only a small number of non-Newtonian simulations can be afforded. Here, we introduce the Rheo-
logical Operator Pretrained Transformer (RheOPT), a neural-operator framework that pretrains a
transformer-based operator on inexpensive Newtonian simulations and then fine-tunes it on a small
number of non-Newtonian samples. Rather than transferring the Newtonian constitutive response
itself, RheOPT reuses geometry-conditioned transport structure that remains useful after the stress
model changes. We evaluate RheOPT in two geometry-matched settings: a 4:1 contraction channel
for Giesekus, Oldroyd-B, and Bingham fluids, and the oscillatory wake behind a triangular obstacle
for Oldroyd-B flow. In the contraction geometry, Newtonian pretraining substantially improves few-
shot prediction, and a model fine-tuned on four target samples can match or exceed a from-scratch
model trained on four times as many. This benefit is reliable under reversible instance normalization
(RevIN) but dataset dependent under Global normalization, a difference we trace to the dynamic
range of each fluid’s velocity and stress fields. In the oscillatory wake, relative L2 error provides a
useful first comparison but cannot by itself verify that vortex shedding is recovered. We therefore
complement it with phase-insensitive diagnostics based on shedding frequency and wake fluctuation
amplitude. These diagnostics show that pretraining recovers the shedding frequencies, spatial struc-
tures, and the wake fluctuation amplitude that are suppressed by the model trained from scratch in
the few-shot regime. Overall, RheOPT establishes Newtonian simulation as a physically grounded
pretraining source for data efficient non-Newtonian operator learning.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Predicting non-Newtonian flow is essential in polymer
manufacturing [1], biological systems [2], soft materi-
als [3], and additive manufacturing [4, 5], but it remains
among the most computationally demanding problems
in continuum mechanics because the stress field is it-
self a dynamic unknown [6].
Unlike Newtonian fluids,
whose stress response is fixed by a viscosity, complex flu-
ids couple the momentum balance to a rate- and history-
dependent constitutive model. The internal stress must
therefore be evolved together with the velocity field. Re-
solving this coupling with finite-element or finite-volume
solvers is expensive, and the cost must be paid again
whenever the geometry, boundary condition, or consti-
tutive parameter changes, since each scenario requires a
full re-computation [7, 8].
This cost has motivated the development of machine-
learning surrogates, and neural operators in particu-
lar [9–12], which learn solution maps between function
spaces and can therefore predict flow fields for new condi-
tions at inference cost rather than solver cost. Our prior
work, RheOFormer [13], established that a transformer-
based neural operator can reproduce spatio-temporal
stress and velocity fields in viscoelastic and viscoplastic
∗s.jamali@northeastern.edu
flows with complex geometries. Reaching this accuracy,
however, comes with a large data requirement, as neu-
ral operators are data-hungry and typically require many
training simulations spanning the range of conditions the
model must later predict.
This creates a practical bottleneck. If many expensive
non-Newtonian simulations are needed before the surro-
gate can be trained, the main computational burden is
shifted from inference to data generation rather than re-
moved. A learned model is therefore most valuable in
the regime where it is also hardest to train: the data-
scarce setting in which only a handful of non-Newtonian
simulations can be afforded.
Newtonian simulations offer a physically motivated
way to address this limitation. They are typically easier
and more robust to generate than non-Newtonian simu-
lations since they avoid the additional constitutive evo-
lution and numerical stiffness associated with complex-
fluid stresses. Meanwhile, they share important structure
with non-Newtonian flows as the geometry, boundary
conditions, and momentum balance organize the velocity
gradients and transport pathways in both cases. These
structures provide the kinematic scaffold on which vis-
coelastic or viscoplastic stresses evolve. This motivates
the central question of this work: can a neural operator
pretrained on Newtonian simulations be fine-tuned, us-
ing only a few non-Newtonian samples, into an accurate
surrogate for complex-fluid flow?

This transfer is not guaranteed. Pretraining and fine-
tuning are separated by a change in the constitutive law
itself.
That change is the defining difference between
Newtonian and non-Newtonian flow, and it is not ob-
vious that a representation learned under a Newtonian
stress response survives the transition and remains use-
ful for viscoelastic or viscoplastic prediction.
Our hy-
pothesis is that Newtonian pretraining transfers not the
Newtonian constitutive response itself, but representa-
tions of momentum transport, velocity gradients, and
boundary-driven flow organization that remain relevant
after the stress model changes. Prior studies for partial
differential equation (PDE) surrogates have transferred
representations across coefficients, fidelities, and Newto-
nian equation families [14–17], while data-driven mod-
els of non-Newtonian flow have generally been trained
on data from the target constitutive model [18–20]. To
the best of our knowledge, Newtonian to non-Newtonian
transfer across a constitutive law boundary has not been
systematically examined, and it is an open question that
RheOPT is designed to answer.
Different factors may determine when this transfer can
be relied upon.
One thing that should be considered
is that non-Newtonian datasets can span wide ranges
of velocity and stress magnitudes across flow regimes.
The normalization used before training can therefore af-
fect whether the pretrained representation remains useful
during fine-tuning or is obscured by scale mismatch be-
tween the Newtonian source data and the non-Newtonian
target data [21]. Second, many complex-fluid flows are
unsteady, so pointwise error alone may not determine
whether the relevant dynamics have been recovered. In
oscillatory regimes such as vortex shedding, a prediction
with the correct shedding structure can be penalized by
a phase shift, while a nearly steady prediction can ap-
pear deceptively accurate if it remains close to the time-
averaged field. Such cases require diagnostics that test
the temporal content of the flow, not only the total field
error [22, 23]. These issues are not separate from transfer.
They determine whether transferred structure is both vis-
ible in the trained model and physically meaningful in the
predicted flow.
We
introduce
RheOPT,
a
Newtonian-pretraining
framework for few-shot non-Newtonian neural-operator
prediction. We evaluate the framework in two canonical
geometries: a 4:1 contraction channel and a flow past a
triangular obstacle. The 4:1 contraction channel provides
a controlled test of physical constitutive transfer across
Giesekus, Oldroyd-B, and Bingham fluids, while the tri-
angular obstacle wake tests whether the pretrained rep-
resentations remain useful for recovering complex vortex-
shedding dynamics.
The main contribution of this work is a controlled
test of Newtonian to non-Newtonian transfer for neural-
operator prediction. In the 4:1 contraction geometry, we
show that geometry-matched Newtonian pretraining im-
proves few-shot prediction across Giesekus, Oldroyd-B,
and Bingham fluids, especially in the strict two snap-
shot input setting.
We then show that the reliabil-
ity of this transfer depends on field normalization: re-
versible instance normalization (RevIN) [21] gives con-
sistent gains across datasets with wide field-magnitude
variation, whereas Global normalization is more dataset
dependent. Finally, we extend the analysis from quasi-
steady contraction flow to the oscillatory wake flow past a
triangular obstacle and use phase-insensitive diagnostics
to show that pretraining helps recover vortex-shedding
dynamics that are suppressed in the from-scratch model.
II.
RELATED WORK
Data-driven modeling of complex fluids has developed
rapidly, driven by the difficulty of characterizing rate-
and history-dependent rheology from limited measure-
ments. A substantial line of work embeds constitutive
physics directly into the learning objective. Rheology-
informed neural networks and related extensions recover
velocity and stress fields, and can identify constitutive
parameters, from sparse rheometric or flow data [24, 25].
Other frameworks such as ViscoelasticNet use learned
models to select among candidate viscoelastic constitu-
tive laws and reconstruct the stress field from an observed
velocity field [18]. Related approaches learn constitutive
response from deformation history [26], and data-driven
surrogates have also been used for more complex stress
prediction tasks [27]. However, across these methods the
model is usually trained for a specific fluid, constitutive
family, or flow configuration, and a change in constitu-
tive model or geometry requires a new target dataset and
retraining.
Operator learning addresses a different part of this
challenge. Rather than approximating one solution, neu-
ral operators learn the mapping between input and out-
put functions. They can therefore predict solutions for
new conditions without retraining, provided those condi-
tions lie within the learned distribution [9–11]. This idea
has been used to learn families of rheological constitu-
tive models and to generalize across parameters within a
given family [20]. Attention-based and geometry-aware
operator architectures further improve expressivity and
support irregular meshes in complex geometries [28–
31].
RheOFormer extended this direction to rheology
by learning full spatio-temporal stress and velocity evo-
lution in viscoelastic and viscoplastic flows in irregular
geometries [13]. These models reduce inference cost af-
ter training and can generalize within the distribution
represented by their training data. However, their train-
ing sets are still drawn from the target physics, so data
generation remains a central cost when high-fidelity non-
Newtonian simulations are scarce.
Pretraining has emerged as a central strategy for
large language models and has recently become an ac-
tive direction in scientific machine learning for reducing
downstream data requirements.
Prior work has trans-
ferred neural-operator representations across coefficients,


3
boundary conditions, and equation families [15, 17]. A
systematic study of pretraining strategies for neural op-
erators found that transfer learning is consistently among
the most effective approaches, and it is most beneficial
when downstream data are scarce [32].
Large multi-
physics models extend the idea further by pretraining
on heterogeneous PDE systems using shared embeddings
and normalization strategies that accommodate variation
across physics domains [14, 33–35].
Recent work has
also explored pretraining on cheaper lower-dimensional
data before transferring to more expensive target prob-
lems [36]. RheOPT follows this data-efficiency motiva-
tion, but uses a different and physically grounded source
of cheaper data.
Transfer learning has also been studied in physics-
informed neural networks.
In this setting, a network
trained on one problem can initialize training on another
through lightweight adaptation [37]. These methods can
accelerate optimization and convergence when source and
target tasks are related, but they are usually confined to
instances of a single physical problem, such as changes of
parameters [38], boundary conditions [39], or fidelity [40].
These methods can reduce optimization cost, but they
generally still require target-specific re-optimization.
Across these directions, a common gap remains. Ex-
isting non-Newtonian learning methods generally train
on data from the same target fluid, constitutive family,
or flow configuration. Neural operators can be geometri-
cally flexible and generalizable across input families, but
their training data are still drawn from the target physics.
PDE pretraining studies demonstrate transfer across sev-
eral forms of distribution shift, but they have not sys-
tematically tested whether a representation learned from
Newtonian flow can be transferred and remain useful for
viscoelastic and viscoplastic prediction. This is exactly
the gap that RheOPT is designed to address by using
Newtonian simulations as a physically grounded pretrain-
ing source for few-shot non-Newtonian operator learning.
III.
METHODS
A.
Problem setup and transfer design
We consider the data-efficient prediction of incom-
pressible, two-dimensional non-Newtonian flows on irreg-
ular domains. Each domain is represented by an unstruc-
tured mesh with node coordinates (x, y). At each node,
the flow state contains the velocity components (vx, vy)
and the independent components of the symmetric stress
tensor (σxx, σxy, σyy).
The model receives a short in-
put sequence of these five physical fields, together with
the node coordinates, and predicts the remaining spatio-
temporal trajectory.
The input window and prediction horizon depend on
the geometry.
For the contraction experiments, the
model receives two snapshots over t ∈[0, 0.2] s, sampled
at ∆t = 0.2 s, and predicts the subsequent evolution
up to t = 5 s. For the triangular-obstacle experiments,
the model receives ten snapshots over t ∈[0, 1.8] s, also
sampled at ∆t = 0.2 s, and predicts the remaining trajec-
tory up to t = 10 s. These two settings provide comple-
mentary tests: the contraction geometry evaluates con-
stitutive transfer in a short observation regime, while the
triangular-obstacle geometry evaluates transfer in an un-
steady wake with vortex shedding.
RheOPT
is
formulated
as
a
geometry-matched
transfer-learning problem. For each geometry, the source
task is Newtonian flow prediction and the target task is
non-Newtonian flow prediction in the same domain. The
non-Newtonian targets are Giesekus and Oldroyd-B flu-
ids for viscoelastic flow and a Bingham fluid for viscoplas-
tic flow.
The source and target tasks therefore share
the geometry, boundary-driven transport structure, and
momentum balance, while differing in the constitutive
relation that determines the stress response.
This de-
sign tests whether geometry and transport constrained
structures, such as recirculation zones, stress concentra-
tions near walls and corners, and wake formation, can be
reused after the constitutive closure changes. Figure 1
summarizes this workflow. RheOFormer is first trained
from a random initialization, θrand, on Newtonian simu-
lations of a given geometry, which yields the pretrained
weights θNewt. The same few non-Newtonian target sam-
ples are then used in two ways: RheOPT is initialized
with θNewt and fine-tuned on the target data, whereas
the baseline trains the same architecture from θrand on
the target data only.

B.
RheOPT architecture, inputs, and training
Realizing this transfer design requires an architecture
that can operate on irregular meshes and propagate dy-
namics over long horizons.
RheOPT builds upon the
RheOFormer architecture [13, 28] as its neural-operator
backbone. RheOFormer is a transformer-based encoder–
decoder architecture designed for complex-fluid flow pre-
diction on irregular meshes.
The encoder applies self-
attention to capture spatial dependencies across all mesh
nodes, producing a discretization-invariant latent rep-
resentation of the observed flow state.
The decoder
uses cross-attention between this latent representation
and query-point coordinates to predict the output fields
at arbitrary locations, decoupling the output discretiza-
tion from the input mesh.
Temporal evolution is per-
formed in latent space through a recurrent propagator,
zt+1 = zt + N(zt), which advances the latent state
without repeatedly re-encoding physical fields at every
timestep. This allows the model to predict long trajecto-
ries on the contraction and triangular-obstacle meshes
without remeshing, interpolation, or geometry-specific
architectural changes.
