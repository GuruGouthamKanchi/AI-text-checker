Abstract
Knowledge graph-to-text (KG-to-text) gener-
ation verbalizes a set of knowledge graph
triples into fluent natural language. While fine-
tuned encoder-decoder models have long been
the standard approach, recent LLMs are be-
coming increasingly competitive. We bench-
mark recent LLMs against fine-tuned encoder-
decoder models, using reference-based met-
rics and graph-grounded reference-free LLM-
based evaluation validated against human an-
notations. We observed that recent LLMs sub-
stantially narrow, and in several settings close,
the gap with fine-tuned models on reference-
based metrics, while performing better on
graph-grounded metrics.
LLM-based eval-
uation scores show limited correlation with
reference-based metrics, suggesting that the
two approaches capture different aspects of
generation quality. Graph-grounded evaluation
reveals that prompted models produce fewer
contradictions and triple omissions than fine-
tuned models, and these errors increase more
rapidly with graph size for fine-tuned models,
highlighting differences between the two ap-
proaches that reference-based metrics may not
fully capture.

Introduction
Knowledge Graphs store information in the form
of structured triples as (subject, relation, object).
The task of verbalising the knowledge graphs into
natural language sentences is a significant and well-
studied problem within the natural language gener-
ation domain. With the growing capabilities of
large language models (LLMs), it is important
to understand how effectively recent models per-
form this task. In this paper advances the eval-
uation of recent pre-trained models on graph-to-
text benchmark datasets, We investigate the per-
formance of five recent model families spanning
diverse architectures: dense decoder-only (Gemma-
4-E4B-it, Gemma-4-31B-it, (Team et al., 2026),
Granite-4.2 (IBM Research, 2026)), mixture-of-
experts (Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it (Team et al., 2026),
Nemotron-3 (Blakeman et al., 2025)), encoder-
decoder (T5-Gemma2 (Zhang et al., 2025)), closed
sourced GPT-5 (Singh et al., 2025) on the following
knowledge graph datasets: WebNLG17 (Gardent
et al., 2017), WebNLG20 (Ferreira et al., 2020)
(English subset), and DART (Nan et al., 2021).
For evaluating the performance of KG-to-Text gen-
eration systems, the most commonly used met-
rics are reference-based like BLEU, METEOR,
ChrF++, and BLEURT. However, BLEU (Papineni
et al., 2002), METEOR (Banerjee and Lavie, 2005),
and ChrF++ (Popovi´c, 2017) judge the generated
text based on surface similarity to the reference text
and do not evaluate its semantic meaningfulness.
BLEURT (Sellam et al., 2020), on the other hand,
is a learned metric designed to mimic human eval-
uation. Additionally, we have found that different
researchers use different sets and implementations
of metrics, which makes it difficult to compare the
results with the published State-of-The-Art (SoTA)
approaches. Therefore, we have used an elabo-
rate set of metrics consisting of BLEU, METEOR,
ChrF++, Rouge-L, BLEURT, BERTScore (Zhang
et al., 2019), BARTScore (Yuan et al., 2021), and
PARENT (Dhingra et al., 2019) and have catego-
rized them into three different categories based on
the function of the metrics. Additionally, we have
also studied LLMs as judge category of metrics as
source- grounded or reference-free judges and ex-
plored how useful and reliable these metrics can be
for evaluating LLM-generated text in terms of crite-
ria that cannot be explicitly judged by the standard
NLG metrics. We frame the following research
questions on the benchmark datasets and try to find
the answers:
RQ1. Under one scoring pipeline with a multi-
reference protocol, where do recent prompted
LLMs (GPT-5 mini, Gemma-4-31B, Granite-4.2-
30B, Nemotron-3-Ultra) and a LoRA-adapted (Hu
arXiv:2610.31529v1  [cs.CL]  22 Oct 2026


et al., 2021) encoder-decoder (T5-Gemma2) model
stand against fully fine-tuned T5-large, per metric
and per dataset?
RQ2. Do the reference-based metric families agree
with one another and with a source-grounded judge
on these LLM generated outputs?
RQ3. What does an LLM judge measure on KG-
to-text outputs, when two different LLM judges are
compared and validated against human annotation?
RQ4. Do fine-tuned and prompted systems make
the same kind of errors?
Our contributions are: 1) a unified evaluation of
five recent model families against fine-tuned T5-
large on three benchmarks with eight reference-
based metrics, showing that compared with earlier
published zero-shot results the gap has narrowed
from tens of BLEU points to a few, with parity
on neural metrics and a remaining lead for fine-
tuning on BLEU, BARTScore and PARENT scores
(RQ1); 2) an analysis of metric agreement show-
ing that the reference-based families agree among
themselves and not very strongly with a source-
grounded judge (RQ2); 3) a two-judge BinEval
(Cho et al., 2026) evaluation of all outputs, val-
idated against 220 human-labelled outputs (two
model outputs on the same 110 graphs) with adjudi-
cated error types, establishing that the LLM judges
reproduce human omission labels triple by triple
and detect contradictions and entity corruptions,
and that they do not detect unsupported elaboration
(RQ3); 4) the finding that, by the human standard,
the fine-tuned and the prompted systems have a
faithfulness problem in a similar share of outputs
but of different kinds: corruption and omission
against elaboration(RQ4).

Related work
The early efforts for verbalizing structured data
relied on recurrent neural network (RNN) architec-
tures, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) models, to en-
code graph-derived sequences and generate natural
language descriptions (Song et al., 2018); (Zhao
et al., 2020); (Guo et al., 2020). Subsequently,
Graph Neural Networks (GNN) based models have
been employed to use a graph encoder to better
capture the structural dependencies and relational
information inherent in graph data; (Li et al., 2021)
and (Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2019). More recent
approaches, however, have increasingly shifted to-
wards the use of large pre-trained language models,
often relying on linearized graph representations
as textual input sequences. Recent work has ex-
plored both pre-trained sequence-to-sequence ar-
chitectures (Ribeiro et al., 2021); (Li et al., 2024)
and open-source generative language models (He
et al., 2025), (Iarosh et al., 2025). The implemen-
tations of Ribeiro et al. (2021) based on T5 and
BART are widely adopted as benchmark baselines
for KG-to-Text evaluation.
We have used WebNLG17, WebNLG20 and DART
as the benchmark datasets. The main reason for
choosing these datasets is that they have negligible
graph-text alignment noise in their training data
i.e. each triple from the graph is described in the
corresponding text and there is no information in
the text which is not grounded in the corresponding
graphs. This property is missing in most of the
other KG-to-Text datasets available. For example,
GraphNarrative (Shi et al., 2023) dataset has on an
average 0.62 hallucinated entities and 0.74 halluci-
nated relations per graph-text pairs, EventNarrative
(Colas et al., 2021) has on an average 0.69 hal-
lucinated entities and 0.70 hallucinated relations,
TEKGEN (Agarwal et al., 2021) has these numbers
as 0.84 hallucinated entities and 0.92 hallucinated
relations by construction (He et al., 2025). An-
other dataset GenWiki (Jin et al., 2020) has 58.1%
unused triplets and 65.1% unguessable text in the
graphs and corresponding text, respectively (Kim
et al., 2026).
While the models finetuned on the respective
datasets have consistently achieved higher perfor-
mance on structured data; Li et al. (2024) and
Yuan and Färber (2023) explored closed-source
generative models for generating text from GPT-
3 in a zero-shot scenario. Their results showed
that the generative models without any fine-tuning
fall significantly behind the fine-tuned sequence-
to-sequence architectures. Recently, Iarosh et al.
(2025) evaluated GPT-4o in zero-shot and chain-
of-thought (CoT) few-shot settings on WebNLG20
and reported significant performance gains when
using detailed prompts. Building on these find-
ings, our work extends the evaluation to a broader
set of recent LLM architectures. Evaluation of
graph-to-text systems has traditionally relied on
reference-based metrics and on human annotation
of dimensions such as triple coverage and hallucina-
tion. Because human annotation is costly to scale,
recent work has turned to LLM-based judges as an
automatic alternative (Liu et al., 2023). Rather than
asking an LLM for a single holistic rating, Check-


Eval (Lee et al., 2025) and BinEval (Cho et al.,
2026) decompose the judgment into a set of binary
questions and aggregate the verdicts. A related
line of work localises data-text mismatches (omit-
ted, extra and incorrect content), but does so with
a fine-tuned verifier rather than LLM prompting
(Efimov-Zhang et al., 2026). Huidrom et al. (2025),
on the other hand, meta-evaluate 29 reference-free
metrics against human semantic-consistency rat-
ings on WebNLG. For this study, we have included
BinEval and GPTScore (Fu et al., 2024) in our
evaluation protocol.

Datasets
Below, we describe these datasets in detail:
WebNLG17. This dataset consists of RDF triples
and their corresponding text descriptions released
as part of the WebNLG challenge, with the goal of
producing a common benchmark to compare “mi-
croplanners” for RDF description (Gardent et al.,
2017). It has become an important benchmark for
KG-to-text generation. The dataset contains graphs
having 1 to 7 triples, which are verbalized into a
few sentences. All the experiments mentioned in
this paper have been performed on the full test sub-
set of the WebNLG17 dataset.
WebNLG20.WebNLG dataset has been revised a
few times since the first challenge (Gardent et al.,
2017).
The WebNLG20 (Ferreira et al., 2020)
dataset has a new category called "company" along
with all the categories present in the WebNLG17
version. Consistent with the previous dataset, all
the experiments on this dataset have been per-
formed on the full test dataset.
DART. DART (Nan et al., 2021) is a large open
domain graph to text dataset constructed using var-
ious sources: human annotation on Wikipedia ta-
bles from WikiSQL (Zhong et al., 2017) and Wik-
iTableQuestions (Pasupat and Liang, 2015); auto-
matic conversion of questions in WikiSQL into
declarative sentences; and inclusion of RDF to text
datasets WebNLG 2017 and E2E (Dušek et al.,
2018). DART thus has triples sourced from diverse
domains and serves as a benchmark dataset for
graph-to-text generation.

Model Architectures
The set of model architectures we selected pro-
vides checkpoints ranging from 270M-parameter
encoder-decoder models to a 550B-parameter
sparse model. The architectural difference among
these models can be stated as: Encoder-decoder vs
decoder-only. We have two models with encoder-
decoder architecture, T5 and T5-Gemma2. How-
ever, the later is an encoder-decoder model which
adapts a pretrained decoder-only Gemma 3 model
into an encoder-decoder, and adds tied word embed-
dings across encoder and decoder and a merged at-
tention module that unifies decoder self- and cross-
attention. We use the 270M-270M, 1B-1B and
4B-4B checkpoints. All other models have decoder-
only architectures. Dense vs Sparse. Granite-
4.2 (with size variants 3B, 8B and 30B) is fully
dense decoder-only model. Gemma-4 has size vari-
ants 4B-it, 26B-A4B and 31B-it; of which 4B-it
and 31B-it are dense decoder-only models whereas
26B-A4B is a mixture-of-experts (Shazeer et al.,
2017) model with around 26 billion total param-
eters and 4 billion active parameters with 128 ex-
perts and uses interleaved local sliding-window and
global attention. The three Nemotron-3 models
are sparse with Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B hav-
ing approximately 3 billion active parameters out
of total 30 billion parameters; Nemotron-3-Super-
120B-A12B having 12 billion parameters out of
120 billion total parameters and Nemotron-3-Ultra-
550B-A55B having 55 billion active parameters
out of the total 550 billion parameters and uses in-
terleaved Mamba-2 state-space (Dao and Gu, 2024)
and MoE layers, with a few self-attention layers.
Open vs closed source models. Out of all the mod-
els that we are using, GPT-5’s architecture is undis-
closed, so it serves as the closed-source reference.
We employed three variants of GPT-5: GPT-5-nano,
GPT-5-mini, and the full GPT-5 model.
5
Experiments
5.1
Fine-tuned models
We adopted Shi et al. (2023)’s pipeline for fine-
tuning T5 for all our T5 experiments. We fine-tuned
the different variants of T5 (Small, Base and Large)
on the three datasets WebNLG17, WebNLG20 and
DART independently, using a learning rate of 3e-5,
a batch size of 8 (for training and validation sets),
for up to 30 epochs with early stopping (patience of
3) based on validation performance. Source and tar-
get sequences are truncated/padded to a maximum
length of 384 tokens for both training and genera-
tion, with beam search decoding with a beam size
of 3 used at inference. The implementation uses
linearized knowledge graphs by representing their
triples as a sequence of subject–predicate–object


elements, concatenated in a fixed order and sepa-
rated by special delimiter tokens "<H>", "<R>" and
"<T>". Training was run on a single A40/A6000
GPU per model.
We fine-tuned T5Gemma2 (4B-4B variant) for
the knowledge-graph-to-text generation task using
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). The input format
has been kept consistent with the T5 model input
setting. We use rank learning rate = 5e-4, r = 32
with scaling factor α = 64, dropout = 0.05, ap-
plied to the q_proj and v_proj modules. This
setting was selected via a hyperparameter sweep
over r ∈{8, 16, 32} (with α = 2r) and learning
rates of {1×10−4, 2×10−4, 3×10−4, 5×10−4},
averaged over 3 random seeds per configuration to
control for initialization variance. Maximum input
and target sequence lengths were set according to
the each dataset’s length distribution.
5.2
Prompted models
For all the decoder-only models and GPT-5, we
have used few-shot prompting. In our experiments
on these models, following the methodology of
Yuan and Färber (2023), each test instance was
treated as a separate request, and the model’s out-
put was recorded as the generated text. Instead
of using the simple prompt, "generate text from
graph," we utilized a more elaborate prompt as
shown in Table 10. The prompt was inspired by the
mistakes noticed when we used the simple prompt,
as mentioned in Appendix A, and it is kept the
same for all the models.
We provide the input graphs to the models as a
text sequence, we have used the same linearization
as used in the case of the T5 and T5-Gemma-2
experiment. Given that most of the open-sourced
LLMs we used are reasoning or thinking models,
we set the thinking or reasoning level to "off" or
"none" and similarly for GPT-5 we kept reason-
ing level to “minimal”. We also set temperature
= 0.0, max tokens = 384 and default top-p value
wherever applicable. We conducted preliminary ex-
periments with GPT-5-mini and Gemma-4-E4B-it
on WebNLG test dataset under different few-shot
settings to determine the most suitable k-shot value
for the remaining experiments. For selecting few-
shot samples, we first use E5-base-V2 (Wang et al.,
2022) to obtain dense embeddings of the graph lin-
earizations. This model provides an effective bal-
ance between retrieval quality and computational
efficiency. Each graph was linearized into a flat tex-
tual sequence by concatenating the head, relation,
and tail tokens of all triples. To mitigate the risk of
data leakage, all samples identical to test instances
were removed from the training set. For deciding
the best sample retrieval strategy, we selected sam-
ples for few-shots based on the cosine similarity
between the linearized graph embeddings of the test
input and the graphs in training set. As (He et al.,
2025) mentioned the effectiveness of Difficulty and
Diversity of the chosen few shots; we tried Maxi-
mal Marginal Relevance (Carbonell and Goldstein,
1998) as a strategy to balance relevance and non-
redundancy in our initial experiments. And as an
alternative, we also tried organising both the train-
ing and test sets into subsets based on graph triple
size. For one-shot retrieval, examples were selected
from the subset containing graphs with the same
number of triples n as the test instance. For two-
shot retrieval, examples were selected from the n
and n + 1 triple subsets, while for three-shot re-
trieval, examples were drawn from the n −1 , n
, and n + 1 triple subsets to introduce structural
diversity among the retrieved examples. For test in-
stances belonging to the largest triple-size category,
where an n + 1 subset was unavailable, retrieval
was restricted to n triple subset. We discuss the
effectiveness of these retrieval strategies in Section
6.1.
5.3
Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate the model outputs from multiple per-
spectives using a diverse set of evaluation metrics.
Based on their underlying methodology, these met-
rics are categorized into four groups. 1) Surface-
Level Metrics. These metrics evaluate the gen-
erated text based on its lexical overlap with the
reference text. The metrics included in this cat-
egory are BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002), ChrF++
(Popovi´c, 2017), METEOR (Banerjee and Lavie,
2005), and ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004).We compute
corpus-level BLEU and chrF++ scores using the
SacreBLEU(Post, 2018) library. METEOR is com-
puted using the implementation provided in NLTK,
while ROUGE-L is computed using the rouge-
score package. 2) Semantic or Neural Metrics.
These metrics assess the semantic similarity and
coherence between the generated text and the ref-
erence text, going beyond exact lexical matching.
The metrics used in this category include BLEURT
(Sellam et al., 2020), BERTScore (Zhang et al.,
2019), and BARTScore (Yuan et al., 2021). We
compute BERTScore using the bert-base-uncased
checkpoint, BLEURT using the BLEURT-20 check-


point, and BARTScore using the facebook/bart-
large-cnn checkpoint. 3) Alignment-Based Met-
rics. When evaluating Large Language Models
for graph-to-text generation, it is essential to deter-
mine whether the generated text remains grounded
in the source graph. The PARENT (Dhingra et al.,
2019) metric addresses this aspect by computing
an F1-based score that measures the alignment of
the generated text not only with the reference text
but also with the source graph.
We adopt a multi-reference evaluation protocol
for all metrics. For corpus-level metrics such as
BLEU and ChrF++, we use SacreBLEU’s native
multi-reference evaluation, where each prediction
is evaluated against all available references jointly.
For sentence-level metrics, including METEOR,
ROUGE-L, BLEURT, BARTScore, and PARENT,
scores are computed against each reference inde-
pendently, and the maximum score across refer-
ences is retained for each instance.
4) LLM as Judge In the task of Graph-to-Text gen-
eration, it is very important to judge the outputs
explicitly for the faithfulness to the source graph,
the coverage of information present in the graph
and the fluency of the text as a whole. We lever-
age LLMs as judges in this task through BinEval
(Cho et al., 2026) and GPTScore (Fu et al., 2024).
BinEval is a training-free, reference-free frame-
work that decomposes each evaluation criterion
into atomic yes/no questions rather than asking
an LLM for one holistic score. A meta-prompt
first generates the atomic questions from the task
prompt, organised by evaluation dimension. An
evaluator LLM then checks the output against the
source for each question, returning a binary ver-
dict and a short natural-language explanation. The
verdicts are aggregated into a score per dimension,
so a low score can be traced to the specific failed
checks. We evaluated Faithfulness to graph, graph
triple Coverage and Fluency of the model outputs
using BinEval framework. We use DeepSeekV-
4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026) and GLM-5.3-Flash
(GLM-5-Team et al., 2026) with thinking disabled
as the judge LLMs. Instead of allowing the BinEval
framework to generate binary questions, we pro-
vided our own questions for each criterion to the
judge LLMs. For each question, the evaluator gives
binary output and the final recorded scores are the
mean of the total satisfied questions per dimension
in the case of Faithfulness and Fluency. The ques-
tions provided to the judges are presented in Tables
12 and 13. For Coverage, the final score is the ratio
of the sum of the binary score for each triple to
the total triples. GPTScore is also a reference-free
metric that scores a generated text by the likeli-
hood a pretrained language model assigns to it,
conditioned on an instruction. We use GPTScore
to get the likelihood of the generated output given
the source graph and task instruction. The official
implementation of GPTScore is tested with Flan-
T5, GPT-2, GPT-3 and OPT family models, how-
ever, we have used DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash in non-
thinking setting as it is a more recent model and
belongs to a different model family compared to the
LLMs we are using for text generation. The imple-
mentation details for both BinEval and GPTScore
are in Appendix B.

Results
6.1
Standing of recent models on reference
based evaluation (RQ1)
The results obtained on all the models are presented
in Tables 1, 2 and 3. 95% Confidence Intervals
(CIs) are computed via paired bootstrap resampling
((Koehn, 2004), N=1,000) for BLEU and ChrF++,
and via Student’s t over per-sample scores for all
other metrics.
For all the models, we observed an increase in per-
formance when moving from smaller variants to
larger variants within the same family so the final
scores presented in this study are on the largest
variants of each model family (T5-large, Gemma-
4-31B, Granite-4.2-30B, Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-
55B and T5-Gemma2-4B-4B). However, the per-
formance gain achieved by GPT-5 full was not
found to be very significant compared to GPT-5-
mini. Considering that GPT-5 full costs more than
GPT-5-mini and nano, we have conducted the re-
ported GPT-5 experiments on GPT-5-mini. In sec-
tion 5.2, we described the different ways of sample
retrieval for few-shot selection. The approach of
using Maximal Marginal Relevance for few-shot
retrieval showed some performance gain against
cosine similarity-based retrieval with λ = 0.7 but
the gain was very small. We also experimented
with sample retrieval based on graph triple size,
but the performance of GPT-5-mini using this re-
trieval strategy did not surpass the simpler cosine
similarity-based retrieval approach. Consequently,
we adopted cosine similarity-based retrieval for all
few-shot experiments reported in this work.
