ABSTRACT
Recent methods for injecting graph structure into LLM prompts include graph
soft prompting, in which a graph neural network (GNN) learns graph-specific
tokens that capture relational information among entities in textual prompts. A
major development in this line of work is G-Retriever, which combines graph
soft prompts with a verbalized description of the same graph to strengthen the
textual prompt signal.
In this paper, we conduct an in-depth analysis of G-
Retriever and uncover a pathological behavior we term token collapse. Across
three benchmarks, three graph encoders, two LLM families, and both frozen and
LoRA-adapted models, G-Retriever’s graph tokens become almost indistinguish-
able across inputs. We provide evidence that the same failure is inherited by
subsequent methods to G-Retriever, even with multi-token pooling. Under the
standard setup, swapping a token with the dataset mean, a token from another ex-
ample, or one derived from a corrupted graph produces no resolved answer-level
effect in accuracy. Moreover, a learned constant prompt that never accesses the
graph matches or surpasses G-Retriever on all three benchmarks. These findings
indicate that, rather than conveying graph content as intended, the collapsed to-
ken functions primarily as a generic soft prompt. We examine conditions linked
to this collapse and propose a two-stage procedure that yields non-collapsed to-
kens while maintaining competitive performance in most cases. However, to-
ken variability alone does not prove that the model is using graph information:
via interventions and decoding probes, we show that depending on the configu-
ration, a token can reflect the question, an answer prior, or graph-derived con-
tent that the LLM may or may not actually leverage. Overall, our results offer
a practical approach to evaluating graph-token alignment rather than inferring
it from task accuracy alone. Code to reproduce our experiments is available at
https://github.com/difra100/Token_Collapse_KGQA.

INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) store knowledge in their parameters, making information outside
or newer than their pre-training data hard to access or update (Lewis et al., 2020). Such external
knowledge may be organised as text-attributed graphs (He et al., 2024). We can incorporate this in-
formation into an LLM either by textualizing the graphs, i.e., constructing verbalized descriptions of
nodes and edges, or through graph soft prompting, i.e., providing a learned representation generated
by a graph neural network (GNN). Verbalisation has two limitations: its token cost grows with the
amount of graph content included, and serialisation imposes a sequence order on facts that have no
intrinsic order. Expressive Graph soft prompting aims to offer an alternative interface for convey-
ing relational information without verbalising every node and edge. G-Retriever (He et al., 2024)
supplies the same underlying graph via both channels. However, this design introduces an identifi-
ability issue: because the graph content is already present in textual form, improvements in answer

G-Retriever interprets its ablations as evidence that the
graph encoder and verbalised graph provide comple-

revisit this interpretation by testing what the graph to-
ken contains and whether the LLM uses it.
This study evaluates the proposed mechanism by
quantifying both the information encoded in graph-
derived tokens and the information that the large lan-
guage model (LLM) causally exploits during infer-
ence.
The experimental protocol integrates token-
space statistical analyses with fixed-weight causal in-
terventions: individual tokens are substituted with (i)
the training-set mean token, (ii) a token sampled from
a different example, or (iii) a token recomputed from
corrupted or different graph inputs. In addition to an-
swer accuracy, we assess gold-answer log-likelihood
to capture subtler shifts in the model’s output distribu-
tion, and we employ decoding-based probes to measure the recoverability of question, answer, and
graph attributes from internal representations. Graph-free control conditions preserve the projector
architecture and the full training pipeline while ablating the graph input itself.
Applying this protocol to G-Retriever uncovers an unanticipated phenomenon that we call token col-
lapse, which also recurs in subsequent graph-prompting methods. Specifically, across several graph
encoders, benchmark datasets, LLM families, and fine-tuning regimes, the token representations
generated by G-Retriever are nearly invariant with respect to the input graph (Figure 1). More-
over, substituting these input-dependent projected tokens with a fixed alternative yields no resolved
answer-level effect under the default configuration; indeed, a learned constant token that never ob-
serves any graph matches or even surpasses the performance of the full model. These results indicate
that the collapsed token operates primarily as a soft prompt rather than as a carrier of graph-specific
information. Although likelihood-based analyses show that perturbing the token can modulate the
output distribution, such sensitivity alone is insufficient to demonstrate input- or graph-conditioned
utilization.
We associate collapse with two factors: a projector that suppresses variation in the encoder repre-
sentations and an answer objective that does not require this variation to be preserved. Our ablations
implicate both factors, although they do not isolate either as the sole cause. We address them with
a two-stage pipeline that first aligns and freezes the graph encoder, then trains separate graph-token
and prompt-token pathways. The resulting tokens no longer collapse, but non-collapse alone guar-
antees neither graph use nor improved accuracy. Depending on the benchmark and training con-
figuration, they may encode the question, an answer prior, graph information made redundant by
the text, or graph information that the LLM uses to answer. This distinction matters because task
improvements may otherwise be attributed to graph–language alignment even when they arise from
the verbalised graph or a graph-independent soft prompt. By separating token variation, graph con-
tent, and downstream use, our protocol makes alignment empirically testable and provides concrete
criteria for developing graph–language models that preserve and use task-relevant graph information
beyond what is already supplied by the textual channel.
Contributions.
We identify token collapse across G-Retriever benchmarks, graph encoders, LLM
families, and tuning regimes, and find the same failure in recent graph-prompting methods. Using
matched graph-free controls and fixed-weight interventions, we show that the collapsed token acts
primarily as a soft prompt rather than as a graph representation used to answer. We associate collapse
with the projector and end-to-end answer training, and propose a two-stage pipeline that mitigates
it across the tested settings. Finally, we show that a non-collapsed token may encode question or
answer priors, unused graph information, or graph information that changes the answer. Together,

RELATED WORK
Graph soft prompting. Soft prompting conditions an LLM with learned continuous vectors (Lester
et al., 2021; Li & Liang, 2021); graph soft prompting instead projects graph-encoder outputs into one
or more such tokens. GraphToken (Fatemi et al., 2024), GraphLLM (Chai et al., 2026), GNP (Tian
et al., 2024), GraphGPT (Tang et al., 2024), and G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) differ in their en-
coders, token counts, retrieval, and LLM adaptation. In this paper, we test whether G-Retriever and
subsequent methods preserve and use graph-specific information in their projected tokens.
Evaluating graph–language models.
Recent work questions whether existing objectives and
benchmarks require graph–language integration. Zhang et al. (2026) show that autoregressive train-
ing can favour a text-dominant solution, while Petkar et al. (2026) find that single-modality and raw
soft-prompt baselines remain competitive and introduce CLEGR to test compositional graph–text
reasoning. We complement these studies by auditing the graph token– LLM interface of graph soft
prompting. Extended related work discussion is in Appendix S.

3.1
PRELIMINARIES: GRAPH SOFT PROMPTING IN G-RETRIEVER
A textual graph G = (V, E, {xv}v∈V, {xe}e∈E) associates every node v with a text attribute xv and
every edge e with a text attribute xe. Each example i consists of a graph, a question qi, and an
answer yi.
Retrieval. Node, edge, and question texts are embedded by a frozen sentence encoder LM(·) into
Rd (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). The respective representations are zv = LM(xv), ze = LM(xe),
and qi = LM(qi).
When the complete graph exceeds the LLM’s context length or a predefined budget, only a relevant
subgraph can be supplied to the model. Following G-Retriever, similarities between qi and the node
and edge embeddings define retrieval relevance, and a prize-collecting Steiner tree is used to select
a compact, connected subgraph (Bienstock et al., 1993). We denote the resulting question-specific
subgraph by Gi. When the complete graph fits within the budget, no retrieval is needed and Gi = G.
Graph token. A graph encoder fϕ processes Gi using node features zv, edge features ze, and
optionally the question representation qi. G-Retriever uses a graph transformer by default (Shi
et al., 2020); we also evaluate GAT (Veliˇckovi´c et al., 2018) and GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017). The
node representations are mean-pooled into hi ∈Rdg, and a projector Pψ maps the result into the
LLM embedding space:

3.2
THE GRAPH TOKEN COLLAPSE PHENOMENON
Task accuracy conflates three distinct questions: Does the token vary across inputs? Does the LLM
use the graph token? What information does it contain? We address them with complementary mea-
surements of token geometry, fixed-weight interventions, gold-answer likelihood, and decodability.
Does the token vary across inputs? We measure inter-sample cosine, the input-specific residual
relative to the mean token, and token norm relative to ordinary word embeddings. Collapse appears
as cosine near one and a small residual ratio. We also compute inter-sample cosine before projection,
on the pooled encoder representations hi, to determine where collapse arises. Formal definitions are
given in Appendix D.
Does the LLM use graph-specific token variation? At fixed weights, REAL uses the original to-
ken, MEAN replaces it with the training-set mean, and SWAP uses another example’s token. Our
main graph-specific intervention, GSWAP, recomputes the token from another graph while preserv-
ing the original question conditioning. NODE, EDGE, and REL corrupt individual graph components,
while NOTXT removes the verbalised graph. Retrained no-entity and no-question variants isolate
the information available during training. Because accuracy observes only changes in the decoded
answer, we also measure the paired change in gold-answer negative log-likelihood under teacher
forcing. This detects smaller changes in the output distribution. Full details on our interventions are
in Appendix D.
What does the token contain? Recoverable content and downstream use are not equivalent: a
token may contain information that the LLM ignores. This matters for our pipeline because Stage 1
is conditioned on the question and trained with an answer-related target. A non-collapsed token may
therefore encode the question, an answer prediction or prior, the graph, or a mixture. The answer
can be a supervision target rather than an input, so it cannot be removed through a fixed-weight
intervention; appendix Table 8 maps the information retained by the remaining conditions.
We complement the interventions with a post-hoc probe that maps stored tokens to frozen embed-
dings of the question, answer, or verbalised graph. The map is fitted on half of the test examples;
on the held-out half, we report the median rank of each example’s correct target among all candi-
dates. A constant token provides the control rank. Estimation details and limitations are given in
Appendix D.
Graph-free controls. CONST passes a learned graph-independent vector through G-Retriever’s
projector, while MLPQ replaces the graph encoder with an MLP over the question. PT is the raw
prompt-tuning baseline, the same as that used by He et al. (2024), and does not use the projector.
These controls distinguish the contribution of graph input from that of token parameterisation.

3.3
PREVENTING COLLAPSE: A TWO-STAGE PIPELINE
Our ablations associate collapse with two factors: a Sigmoid projector that suppresses encoder vari-
ation and end-to-end answer training that does not require this variation to be preserved. We address
both while retaining G-Retriever’s data, prompt, answer loss, and optimisation setup (Figure 2).
Stage 1: preserve input-specific variation. Without the LLM, we train the graph encoder with
an InfoNCE objective (van den Oord et al., 2019). Its pooled representation hi is aligned with a
frozen embedding of a sample-specific target and contrasted against negative targets. The question
conditions each node through xv = zv + Wqqi, with Wq initialised at zero. We use the answer as
the target, or the verbalized graph, when the answer space is too small to distinguish samples (e.g.,
answers are Yes/No). The encoder is then frozen.
Stage 2: separate graph representation from prompting. Using the original answer loss, we
jointly train a GELU projector over the frozen encoder and a separate graph-independent prompt
token. Freezing protects the variation learned in Stage 1, while the second pathway gives the shared
prompting function its own token rather than forcing it to compete with input-specific information.
The full objective, targets, and design ablations are reported in Appendix N.

EXPERIMENTS
We investigate four questions. RQ1: Do graph tokens collapse, and how general is this behaviour?
RQ2: What role does the collapsed token play when the same graph is also verbalised in the prompt?
RQ3: What is collapse associated with, and can it be prevented without sacrificing task perfor-
mance? RQ4: What do non-collapsed tokens encode, and which of that information does the LLM
use?
4.1
SETUP
Benchmarks.
We use the three GraphQA benchmarks introduced by He et al. (2024):
ExplaGraphs, SceneGraphs, and WebQSP. Following their official splits, retrieval proce-
dures, and evaluators, we report accuracy on ExplaGraphs and SceneGraphs and Hit@1 on
WebQSP. Dataset statistics and retrieval settings are in Appendix B.
Models. We reproduce G-Retriever with its default graph transformer (GT) and frozen Llama-2-7B
(Touvron et al., 2023). Robustness experiments use GAT, GCN, Qwen2.5-3B (Qwen Team, 2025),
and LoRA adaptation. Architecture, optimisation, and pipeline settings are in Appendix A.
Evaluation stability. Re-evaluating identical WebQSP weights in a separate process changes Hit@1
by 1.04 points. We therefore require an answer-level difference larger than 2 points with p <
.05. Gold-answer likelihood is more stable; we require |∆NLL| > 0.03 and a bootstrap interval
excluding the replicate floor. Calibration results are in Appendix D.
Statistical testing. Accuracy and Hit@1 comparisons are paired with the exact McNemar test (Mc-
Nemar, 1947). We denote p < .10, .05, .01, and .001 by †, *, **, and ***, respectively; unmarked
comparisons have p ≥.10. Reported p-values are unadjusted, so claims rely on the effect-size
criteria above and consistent evidence rather than isolated significant cells.
4.2
RQ1: DO GRAPH TOKENS COLLAPSE, AND HOW GENERAL IS COLLAPSE?
Collapse occurs in every tested configuration. Token cosine ranges from 0.985 to 0.998, while
the input-specific residual is only 4–12% of the shared mean, whose norm is 22–97 times that of an
ordinary word embedding (Appendix E). Figure 1 illustrates this geometry on WebQSP. Models with
similarly collapsed tokens nevertheless obtain substantially different scores, so task performance
does not reveal collapse.
The encoder representations are considerably more variable, with cosines between 0.423 and 0.693.
This makes GNN oversmoothing (Rusch et al., 2023) an insufficient explanation for token collapse:
even if node representations are smoothed within a graph, the pooled outputs might remain distinct
across inputs, and near-identity appears only after projection. Collapse also persists across three

4.3
RQ2: WHAT ROLE DOES THE COLLAPSED TOKEN PLAY?
The input-specific component has no resolved answer-level effect. Across all three benchmarks,
MEAN, SWAP, and GSWAP remain within the two-point threshold (Table 2); corrupting entities,
edges, or relations gives the same result (more evidence in Appendix I). Yet replacing the token with
zero costs 59.64–81.95 points (see Appendix J). The model therefore relies on the token’s shared
value, but not measurably on its graph-specific variation.
The verbalised graph is the dominant graph channel. Removing it costs 19–51 points, whereas
replacing the remaining token with its mean changes performance by at most 1.04 points. Thus,
in the default Llama-2 configuration, task-relevant graph information is supplied primarily through
text.
The shared token acts as a soft prompt. CONST, a graph-independent vector passed through the
same projector, matches or exceeds G-Retriever on every benchmark. Graph input is therefore un-
necessary for the benefit attributed to the collapsed token. Raw prompt tuning, namely the existing
ablation performed by He et al. (2024), performs worse (Appendix K), but changes the parameteri-
sation and is not a controlled graph-versus-no-graph comparison.
Small distributional effects remain: on WebQSP, SWAP and GSWAP increase gold-answer NLL by
0.0183 and 0.0182, both below the 0.03 criterion (Appendix L). These results answer RQ2: the
collapsed token functions primarily as a projector-parameterised soft prompt, while the verbalised
graph is the dominant graph-information channel. RQ3 asks what collapse is associated with and
whether it can be prevented.

4.4
RQ3: WHAT IS COLLAPSE ASSOCIATED WITH, AND CAN IT BE PREVENTED?
Factors associated with collapse. Table 3 identifies two associations. With the aligned Stage 1
encoder frozen, its representations are highly distinct (cosine 0.024), yet the Sigmoid projector
produces nearly identical tokens before and after training (0.9870 and 0.9918). GELU instead yields
cosine 0.0910 before training and 0.3229 afterwards. Untrained-projector experiments reproduce
this contrast on random inputs (Appendix H), linking the Sigmoid parameterisation to suppression
of encoder variation.
Changing the projector alone is insufficient: under end-to-end answer training, GELU still pro-
duces highly similar tokens (cosine 0.9139). Conversely, combining the aligned frozen encoder
with GELU prevents collapse, but the single-token model falls to approximately majority-class per-
formance (57.76 versus 58.8). Thus, both factors are associated with collapse, but this experiment
does not identify either as the sole cause. The cross-method audit supports this qualification (Ap-
pendix F). GRAG (Hu et al., 2025) and GRETPool (Grover et al., 2026) inherit the same Sigmoid
projector and also collapse, while GraSP (Wang et al., 2026) collapses despite using the hyperbolic
tangent.
Preventing collapse while retaining performance. Table 4 shows that the complete two-token
pipeline mitigates collapse in every tested setting. For the standard pipeline, token cosine falls from
above 0.99 to 0.099–0.142 with Llama-2 and to 0.103–0.200 with Qwen2.5-3B.
With Llama-2,
the pipeline retains performance on WebQSP (69.90 versus 70.33) and
ExplaGraphs (85.74 versus 85.56), with both differences below our resolution threshold. On
ExplaGraphs, the separate prompt pathway recovers the performance lost by the non-collapsed
single-token model, raising its score from 57.76 to 85.74. On SceneGraphs, however, GSWAP
produces a resolved −5.30-point effect while the score falls from 81.35 to 72.50.
The Qwen2.5-3B replication further separates performance from graph use. The Qwen rows in Ta-
ble 4 use a matched training budget and are distinct from the robustness checkpoint in Table 1. On
WebQSP, our pipeline improves the matched baseline from 57.13 to 65.79 (p = 4.5×10−20), while
GSWAP produces a resolved −2.09-point effect (p = .0027). On ExplaGraphs, it improves from
74.37 to 83.94 (p = 1.5×10−5), but GSWAP has no effect (+0.36). Conversely, on SceneGraphs,
it produces the strongest graph-replacement effect in the study (−6.50, p < .001) while scoring 3.40
points below G-Retriever. Qwen G-Retriever on ExplaGraphs provides the opposite exception:
its tokens remain collapsed, yet GSWAP causes a resolved −4.51-point effect. Near-collapse there-
fore does not imply complete insensitivity to the remaining variation.
These results answer RQ3. We found that collapse is associated with both the projector and end-to-
end answer training, without either being isolated as its sole cause. The two-stage pipeline mitigates
collapse across both LLM families and can retain or improve performance. However, geometric
collapse, graph-specific token use, and task performance remain distinct properties. RQ4 takes a
step forward and examines what the tokens encode and which information the LLM uses.

uses those differences. Because Stage 1 receives the question and an answer-related target, the token
may encode the question, an answer prior, the graph, or a mixture.

The retrieval probe maps each token to the corresponding question, answer, or verbalised graph
among held-out candidates. We report the median rank of the correct target: lower is better, and a
constant token provides the control rank. Recoverability does not imply use. A low answer rank,
for example, means that the answer is predictable from the token, not that it was computed from the
graph or used by the LLM.
We use five diagnoses. USED means that GSWAP changes the task score by more than two points
with p < .05. MASKED means that this effect resolves only after removing the verbalised graph.
PRIOR identifies a no-entity model whose token carries question and answer information and is
used under MEAN or SWAP, without resolved graph-specific use. DECODABLE means that graph in-
formation is recoverable without a resolved answer-level effect. SOFT PROMPT denotes a collapsed
baseline without resolved graph-specific use; any information recoverable from its residual variation
is reported separately by the retrieval ranks.
WebQSP: the token’s role depends on training. The standard token makes the question and an-
swer strongly recoverable, at ranks 65 and 60 against the control of 407. With the verbalised graph
present, GSWAP affects likelihood but not the decoded score; after removing the text, it causes a re-
solved −2.27-point effect (Tables 5 and 70). Removing entity features further strengthens question
and answer recovery and improves Hit@1 without resolved graph use, supporting the PRIOR diag-
nosis. Conversely, removing question conditioning produces resolved graph use. Thus, the same
architecture can favour priors or graph information depending on its training inputs.
