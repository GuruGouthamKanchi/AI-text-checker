Abstract—Counterfactual explanation methods have been in-
troduced for unimodal settings, with dedicated frameworks for
tabular, timeseries, text, and image data, yet no post-hoc method
has been designed for classifiers that learn from multimodal
inputs. Existing explainability methods for multimodal classifiers
apply modality-specific attribution independently to each input,
which fails to yield coherent explanations and does not transfer to
arbitrary pre-trained multimodal architectures. To address these
shortcomings, we present CRANE (Counterfactual Retrieval via
Agnostic Nearest-neighbour Explanations), the first post-hoc,
model-agnostic framework for nearest-neighbour multimodal
counterfactual generation that retrieves real training examples
from the target class as candidate explanations, ensuring plausi-
bility by construction. Across four heterogeneous datasets and
three fusion strategies, we show that Multimodal Consensus
Retrieval (MC-R), which intersects per-modality neighbour sets
to identify instances jointly close to the query in every modality,
emerges as the strongest multimodal strategy, achieving the best
or joint-best outcome on three of four datasets while grounding
every explanation in the joint training distribution. Unimodal
strategies remain competitive when a single modality dominates
the decision boundary.
Index Terms—Explainable artificial intelligence, Counterfac-

I. INTRODUCTION
Data science pipelines increasingly operate on heteroge-
neous inputs, combining structured records, free text, time-
series, and images within a single predictive model. Clinical
decision support systems fuse vital signs with structured
patient demographics to predict outcomes, such as sepsis mor-
tality [1] and cancer metastasis [2]–[4]; content-moderation
systems pair images and their captions to detect hateful
memes [5]; and fraud detectors combine free-text job postings
with structured posting metadata to flag deceptive listings [6].
As these multimodal architectures become standard practice,
the need to explain their predictions grows. This need has
driven a substantial body of work on explainable Artificial
Intelligence (XAI) [7], [8].
Counterfactual explanations are among the most natural and
actionable forms of post-hoc justification: a counterfactual x′
reconfigures a sample x so as to flip its prediction while
staying close to x and consistent with the data manifold.
Falling within the example-based XAI methods, they answer
the intuitive, often directly actionable question “what would
need to change in x for the model to decide otherwise?”.

Well-performing counterfactual generators exist for individ-
ual modalities: tabular [9], [10], timeseries [11], [12], text [13],
and image [14]. These generators, however, assume a single,
homogeneous input space. When a classifier consumes several
modalities at once, applying these generators independently
per modality fails to yield coherent explanations, as illustrated
in Fig. 1. For a multimodal mortality classifier over static de-
mographics and laboratory timeseries, a tabular counterfactual
generator may return a female survivor of the same age, while
a timeseries generator may return signals that reflect decreas-
ing N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP),
a cardiac stress marker, but might not change the increasing
trend in Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA), which is a clinical
impossibility, as biological females do not produce PSA. This
process would result in a chimeric counterfactual that would be
locally plausible within each modality, yet globally incoherent
and clinically meaningless when considered as a whole.
Retrieval-based methods avoid this problem by construction:
rather than synthesising a new sample, they return a real
training example from the target class. The nearest-unlike-
neighbour (NUN) literature exploits this in unimodal set-
tings [11], [12], [15], but it has not been extended to the
multimodal case.
In addition, when using a retrieval-based approach, different
choices regarding the search space itself deserve consideration.
One could search within a single modality’s feature space,
concatenate all modality embeddings and search jointly, or

search in the multimodal model’s latent representation. Each
choice encodes a different notion of similarity, and how their
relative merits compare in terms of the standard counterfactual
quality objectives (prediction-flip rate, proximity, sparsity, and
plausibility) [16], and of the diversity of the generated set [10]
remains to be explored.
We address these open questions with CRANE (Counterfac-
tual Retrieval via Agnostic Nearest-neighbour Explanations),
a post-hoc, model-agnostic framework for nearest-neighbour
counterfactual generation for multimodal classifiers. CRANE1
supports arbitrary combinations of tabular, timeseries, text,
and image modalities through retrieval strategies spanning
from unimodal search to multimodal fusion, and extends
the standard counterfactual quality objectives (i.e., outcome,
proximity, sparsity, and plausibility) to heterogeneous input
spaces. Among the different strategies, Multimodal Consensus
Retrieval (MC-R), which retrieves training instances jointly
close to the query in each modality, emerges as the strongest
multimodal strategy in four datasets spanning social media,
recruitment, content moderation, and clinical prediction, while
unimodal retrieval remains competitive when a single modality
dominates the decision boundary.
II. RELATED WORK
Explainability methods for machine learning (ML) span a
broad landscape, from feature-attribution methods [17], [18],
to saliency maps [19], attention-based explanations [20], and
example-based approaches, like counterfactuals, yet the vast
majority assume a single, homogeneous input space. This lim-
itation is sharpest for counterfactuals, where the explanation
must simultaneously specify what would change across all
input spaces, not merely within one.
Counterfactual explanations were formalised in the context
of ML by Wachter et al. [9], and have since grown into
a rich research area. For example, DiCE [10] generates a
diverse set of counterfactuals by adding a determinantal point
process term to the objective, so that different explanations
suggest different actionable paths, while Dandl et al. [21]
proposed a multi-objective formulation that simultaneously
optimises proximity, sparsity, plausibility, and actionability
without collapsing them into a single weighted sum.
A distinct thread within the counterfactual literature avoids
optimisation altogether by framing explanation as retrieval,
where a real training example from the target class, the NUN,
is used directly as the explanation or as a seed for further
refinement. Brughmans and Martens [15] formalise this ap-
proach in NICE, which exploits the NUN to construct tabular
counterfactuals that are guaranteed to exist in the observed
data distribution. The retrieval strategy carries an important
structural guarantee: since the returned sample was observed
in the training data, it is plausible by construction, without
any explicit plausibility penalty in an objective function.
However, the methods reviewed above are designed exclu-
sively for flat tabular or embedding-space inputs; the question

A. Problem Formulation
We introduce a general formulation for multimodal coun-
terfactual generation that is independent of any particular data
type, classifier architecture, or application domain.
Multimodal instance space. Let M ≥1 be the number of
modalities present in a dataset S. For each modality m ∈
{1, . . . , M}, let X m be the corresponding modality space,
encoding the representation format and domain constraints of
that modality. The multimodal input space is
X := X 1 × · · · × X M.
(1)
A multimodal instance is a tuple x = (x1, . . . , xM) ∈X,
where xm ∈X m is the component of x in modality m.
In principle, X m may represent any data type for which a
suitable distance function dm and plausibility estimator pm can
be defined. Without loss of generality, in this work we explore
four modality types commonly encountered in heterogeneous
data pipelines: static feature vectors (tabular), multivariate
timeseries, token sequences (text), and two-dimensional pixel
arrays (image). Adding additional modalities requires only
specifying appropriate dm and pm functions for each one.
Multimodal classifier. Let Y be a finite label set. A multi-
modal classifier is a function f : X →[0, 1]|Y|, where fy(x) is
the score assigned to label y ∈Y for instance x. The predicted
label is ˆy := arg maxy∈Y fy(x). CRANE treats f as a black
box: only the prediction function is required, and no access to
model internals, gradients, or training procedure is assumed.

On the plausibility of retrieved counterfactuals. A key
property of retrieval-based methods is that whenever k = 1,
the returned counterfactual is a real training instance from the
target class and is therefore plausible by construction: it lies
on the empirical data manifold, and all its modalities were
observed together in a single real-world instance, preserving
cross-modal coherence. When k > 1, CRANE always returns
the single nearest neighbour as its first candidate; the j-th
subsequent candidate (j = 2, . . . , k) is the component-wise
median over the j nearest neighbours, yielding a sequence
of increasingly smoothed prototypes. In general, none of
these median candidates corresponds to any observed train-
ing instance. The plausibility objective in Eq. (7) quantifies
plausibility for all candidates uniformly.
B. CRANE Retrieval Strategies
CRANE constructs counterfactual candidates by searching
the training set D for the k instances closest to x among
those labelled y⋆, and assembling a candidate from those
neighbours. The key design choice is in which space the
nearest-neighbour search is performed. CRANE provides a
family of retrieval strategies, ranging from unimodal search to
multimodal fusion. All strategies share a common interface:
they take (x, y⋆, k) as input and return an ordered list of
candidate counterfactuals. All treat the classifier f as a black
box. The different strategies are described below:

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets
We evaluate CRANE on four publicly available datasets
spanning diverse application domains, modality combinations,
and class structures. A summary is given in Table I.
Sepsis (in-hospital mortality). The Sepsis dataset is extracted
from MIMIC-III [22] following the procedure described in [1],
which identifies ICU admissions with a confirmed sepsis di-
agnosis. Each admission is represented as 24 hourly timesteps
over 100 features, partitioned into a timeseries modality of 53
time-varying clinical variables (vital signs and laboratory val-
ues) and a tabular modality of 47 static features (demographic
and admission data extracted from the first timestep). The
classification task is binary in-hospital mortality prediction.
The dataset is strongly imbalanced, with a mortality rate of
approximately 7.4%. Evaluation follows 5-fold stratified cross-
validation; results are pooled across folds and reported as F1
score as the primary metric, and AUC.
Long COVID Tweets (emotion classification). This dataset 2
consists of Italian tweets discussing long COVID, annotated
with four emotion labels drawn from the FEEL-IT taxon-
omy [23]: fear, anger, sadness, and joy. Each instance com-
prises a raw tweet text field and a tabular modality encoding
engagement metadata (likes, retweets, replies, quote counts)
and structural tweet features (hashtag and URL counts). The
class distribution is imbalanced, with fear (41.6%) and anger
(32.9%) dominating and joy (8.8%) forming the minority class.
Hateful Memes (hate speech detection). The Hateful Memes
dataset [24] is a binary classification benchmark for detecting
hate speech in internet memes. Each instance consists of a text
caption and a 224×224 RGB image. The class distribution is
moderately imbalanced, with 63.7% non-hateful and 36.3%
hateful instances.
Real or Fake Jobs (fraud detection). This dataset [25]
contains job postings labelled as real or fraudulent. Each
instance combines a tabular modality (structured fields such
as salary range, employment type, and required experience)
with three independent text fields: job description, company

B. Experimental Setup
1) Classifier Families and Fusion Strategies: For each
dataset, we train classifiers under three fusion strategies: early
fusion, intermediate fusion, and late fusion, as defined in
Section III-B. For each fusion strategy, we compare two clas-
sifier families: a deep family based on multi-layer perceptrons
(MLP) with task-trained branches, and a non-deep family
using tree-based ensembles (Random Forest and Gradient
Boosting) or, for timeseries branches, the ROCKET transform
with Logistic Regression [26]. This design yields a range
of classifier types that stress-tests CRANE’s model-agnostic
claim: the IF-NN retrieval strategy requires a deep neural
classifier with accessible penultimate activations and is there-
fore evaluated only on deep models, while all other CRANE
strategies are evaluated uniformly across both families.
Text is encoded using pre-trained transformer models:
XLM-RoBERTa for Long COVID Tweets, DistilBERT for
Real or Fake Jobs, and BERT or MiniLM for Hateful Memes.
Images in Hateful Memes are encoded using ResNet-50,
EfficientNet-B0, or ViT-B/16. For early-fusion classifiers, all
encoders are frozen (no fine-tuning); for intermediate-fusion
classifiers, the transformer backbone is fine-tuned end-to-end.
Class imbalance is addressed via inverse-frequency weighting
for all classifiers. For training we use a 15% hold-out valida-
tion split for early stopping and hyperparameter tuning.
2) CRANE Configuration: Each CRANE retrieval strategy
is configured with k = 20, returning 20 candidates per
query. For MC-R, the per-modality search pool is set to
ksearch = 200 neighbours per modality prior to intersection,
ensuring that the consensus set is non-empty for all queries
in our datasets. For each dataset we run a systematic ablation
over all valid combinations of encoder and distance metric
per modality (as defined in Section III-B), yielding 6, 22, 16,
and 84 combinations for Sepsis, Long COVID Tweets, Real or
Fake Jobs, and Hateful Memes, respectively. The results are
reported as averages across all ablation combinations.
3) CRANE Strategies per Dataset: Not all retrieval strate-
gies are applicable to every dataset and classifier combination.
IF-NN is evaluated only on deep classifiers, as described in
Section IV-B; for datasets where a modality is absent (e.g., no
image modality in Sepsis, Tweets, or Jobs), the corresponding
unimodal strategy is not evaluated.
