Abstract
Masked diffusion language models generate to-
kens in parallel, but their efficiency depends on
which predictions are committed. Learned un-
masking policies improve the speed–accuracy
trade-off, yet differ in supervision, inputs, and
capacity, obscuring which choices matter. We
view supervised policies as distilling a decod-
ing procedure through a teacher and examine
independently what the teacher labels as com-
mittable, on which states the policy imitates it,
what the policy observes, and how much the
number of function evaluations (NFE) must de-
crease to offset policy overhead. We find that
labeling predictions matching the final output
is overly permissive at low NFE, and hidden
states are weak alone but can complement confi-
dence. About a 10% NFE reduction suffices to
offset policy overhead for policies up to 7.5M
parameters across tested settings. We therefore
train a policy on trajectory-contraction targets,
which accept only commitments staying on a
reference trajectory, using confidence and hid-
den states. On LLaDA-8B-Instruct, the result-
ing 856K-parameter policy achieves the best
accuracy–NFE frontier and highest throughput
on GSM8K, is competitive at moderate targets
on MATH-500 and HumanEval, and largely
transfers to Dream-7B-Instruct.

Introduction
Building on discrete diffusion models (Hoogeboom
et al., 2021; Austin et al., 2021a; Campbell et al.,
2022; Lou et al., 2024), masked diffusion language
models (dLLMs) (Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al.,
2024; Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Sakurai
et al., 2026) provide an alternative to the left-to-
right generation of autoregressive models (Brown
et al., 2020; Grattafiori et al., 2024; Qwen et al.,
†Corresponding author.
2025) by iteratively refining partially masked se-
quences and committing multiple tokens in paral-
lel. Their efficiency, however, depends strongly on
the unmasking policy, which decides which posi-
tions to commit at each step. Conservative policies
require many denoising evaluations, whereas un-
masking many tokens at once can produce mutually
inconsistent tokens, as they are predicted indepen-
dently given the same state. Such errors persist,
since committed tokens are not revised later (Wu
et al., 2026; Jazbec et al., 2026; Bao et al., 2026;
Xu et al., 2026). Recent work therefore adapts the
number of committed tokens to the decoding state.
Training-free methods do so using confidence (Wu
et al., 2026) or entropy-based dependence control
(Ben-Hamu et al., 2025), while learned policies are
trained from data (Jazbec et al., 2026; Bao et al.,
2026; Xu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026).
Despite this progress, learned unmasking poli-
cies differ in several design choices at once: which
positions are labeled as safe to commit, which de-
coding states are used for training, and what in-
formation the policy observes, often together with
policy capacity. For example, whether backbone
hidden states help cannot be judged, since the poli-
cies that use them also differ in size or supervision.
Prior work reports the number of function evalua-
tions (NFE) and throughput, but the NFE reduction
needed to offset policy overhead across policy sizes,
caching, and prompt lengths remains unclear.
We therefore revisit learned unmasking policies
from a distillation perspective. Existing supervised
policies (Bao et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026) can
be viewed as distilling a fixed decoding procedure:
the procedure is run on training prompts to obtain
reference trajectories, a teacher derived from them
labels which positions may be committed, and the
policy imitates this teacher. This view separates
what the teacher labels, on which states the policy
imitates it, and what the policy observes; we vary
one at a time, comparing choices from prior work

with new candidates. First, existing teachers label
a prediction as committable whenever it matches
the final output. A correct token, however, is not
necessarily safe to commit early, since its commit-
ment changes the input to subsequent steps. We
therefore introduce trajectory contraction, which
labels a commitment as valid only when it directly
reaches a later state of the reference trajectory, and
compare it jointly with the choice of training states:
reference trajectories or those induced by the la-
bels. Second, as policy inputs, we compare confi-
dence, probability margins, hidden representations,
and confidence combined with hidden representa-
tions. Third, we measure both NFE and end-to-end
throughput in tokens per second (TPS), and profile
the policy overhead.
Our study shows that matching the final output is
an overly permissive label in the low-NFE regime,
with 28.7% of such positives leaving the reference
trajectory when committed early, and that hidden
representations are weak alone but can complement
confidence. It also shows that, across the tested set-
tings, a policy of up to 7.5M parameters pays off
in throughput once it reduces NFE by more than
about 10%. Based on these findings, we train a pol-
icy with trajectory-contraction targets on reference
trajectories, taking confidence and hidden represen-
tations as input; it has only 856K parameters, about
0.01% of the backbone.
On LLaDA-8B-Instruct (Nie et al., 2025), our
policy achieves the best accuracy–NFE frontier
on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), where it also
attains the highest TPS at every accuracy target.
On MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman
et al., 2024) and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), it
reaches moderate accuracy targets with the fewest
NFE and the highest TPS, whereas training-free
methods are more efficient at the highest targets,
and on MBPP (Austin et al., 2021b) it performs
comparably to the strongest baselines. Its advan-
tage largely carries over to Dream-7B-Instruct (Ye
et al., 2025) and, on mathematical reasoning, to
unseen generation lengths.
Our contributions are:
• We show that matching the final output is an
overly permissive commitment label in the low-
NFE regime, and introduce trajectory contrac-
tion, which improves the accuracy–NFE fron-
tier, with a further out-of-domain gain from
supervision on reference trajectories.
• We show that backbone hidden states alone
are a weak input, but can improve the frontier
at high accuracy targets when combined with
token confidence, and build on this an 856K-
parameter policy.
• By profiling per-step latency, we show that
policies of up to 7.5M parameters pay off in
throughput once they reduce NFE by more than
about 10% in the tested settings, and evaluate
our policy across tasks, backbones, and genera-
tion lengths.

Related Work
Efficient unmasking. The efficiency and quality
of dLLMs depend strongly on the order and de-
gree of parallelism used for unmasking (Kim et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2026). Training-free approaches
exploit predictive uncertainty, decoding dynamics,
or dependencies among candidate positions to de-
termine which tokens can be committed in parallel
(Ben-Hamu et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026; Luxem-
bourg et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026; Kim et al.,
2026). Fast-dLLM is particularly relevant to our
setting: it uses confidence-thresholded parallel de-
coding to reduce denoising steps while keeping the
underlying dLLM frozen (Wu et al., 2026). De-
spite substantial progress, these methods prescribe
the commitment rule at inference time rather than
learning it from decoding experience.
Learning unmasking policies. Recent work in-
stead learns where or when to commit tokens, using
reinforcement learning, supervised oracle signals,
ranking objectives, or learned dependency models
(Hong et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Asano et al.,
2026; Ringel et al., 2026). LUP learns a lightweight
Transformer policy from confidence-based features
using reinforcement learning (Jazbec et al., 2026).
More directly related to our supervision setting,
Learn2PD trains a lightweight filter from oracle
token-correctness signals, whereas TraceLock de-
rives commitment labels retrospectively from com-
pleted decoding traces and exploits richer hidden-
state representations (Bao et al., 2026; Sun et al.,
2026). We study how to construct supervised com-
mitment targets directly from Fast-dLLM trajec-
tories and how to incorporate richer token-level
information without sacrificing the efficiency of
lightweight confidence-based policies.

Revisiting Unmasking Policy Learning
3.1
Problem Formulation
A masked dLLM generates a sequence x
=
(x1, . . . , xL) through partially masked states τ =
(s0, . . . , sT ). At each step t, the model produces to-
ken distributions pθ(· | st) for the masked positions
M(st), and an unmasking policy selects the posi-
tions to commit, At = πϕ(zt) ⊆M(st), where zt
is the information available to the policy. The de-
coder then transitions to st+1 = C(st, At), where
C fills the selected positions with their greedy pre-
dictions, so the trajectory is deterministic given the
policy decisions. Committing more positions per
step reduces NFE, but premature commitments may
degrade generation quality; a good policy achieves
a favorable trade-off between the two.
3.2
Design Space and Protocol
We study supervised policies trained offline on a
frozen backbone, such as Learn2PD (Bao et al.,
2026) and TraceLock (Sun et al., 2026). These
policies distill a fixed decoding procedure: the pro-
cedure is run on training prompts to obtain refer-
ence trajectories, from which each masked position
at each state is labeled with a binary commitment
target indicating whether it may be committed, and
the policy is trained to predict these targets. Build-
ing such a policy requires deciding what counts
as a correct commitment (the target), on which
states the policy learns it (the training states; Sec-
tion 3.3), and what the policy observes (the input;
Section 3.4). Moreover, the policy is evaluated at
every step, so its cost may offset the reduction in
NFE (Section 3.5).
Existing methods vary simultaneously across
multiple design axes, making it difficult to iso-
late the effect of individual design choices from
their reported results. For example, LUP-hidden
underperforms LUP-conf despite roughly 1000×
more parameters (Jazbec et al., 2026) (Table 1). We
therefore compare existing and newly introduced
candidates for each design axis while holding the
others fixed. We evaluate the commitment target
and training states jointly in a 2 × 2 design to ac-
count for their interaction during teacher rollouts.
Setup. We use LLaDA-8B-Instruct (Nie et al.,
2025) as the frozen backbone. Following Jazbec
et al. (2026), all policies are trained on a mixture
of the GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH
(Hendrycks et al., 2021) training sets, with ref-
erence trajectories generated by Fast-dLLM (Wu
et al., 2026) at confidence threshold 0.9. Unless
otherwise specified, we use generation length L =
256 and block length 32, and evaluate zero-shot.
We take the accuracy of one-by-one confidence-
based decoding (Nie et al., 2025) as the reference
and define target accuracy levels at 98%, 95%, and
90% of it. For each method, we sweep its com-
mitment threshold over a fixed grid (Section C.4)
and compare the resulting accuracy–NFE trade-off
against these targets. In this section, we evaluate on
GSM8K (in-domain) and MBPP (out-of-domain)
(Austin et al., 2021b), and all candidates share the
same architecture, optimizer, and training schedule
(Sections B.1 and C.3).

3.4
What Information Should the Policy Use?
Input Candidates. Existing policies use token con-
fidence (Learn2PD, LUP-conf) or backbone hid-
den representations (TraceLock, LUP-hidden). Be-
yond confidence, uncertainty statistics such as the
top-two probability margin (Kim et al., 2025) and
entropy (Xu et al., 2026) are also used to decide
which tokens to unmask. For each masked posi-
tion i at step t, we consider the token confidence
ct,i = maxv pθ(xi = v | st), the top-two probabil-
ity margin ∆t,i defined as the difference between
the largest and second-largest token probabilities,
and the backbone hidden representation ht,i. We
compare four input configurations:
zt,i ∈{ct,i, ht,i, [ct,i, ∆t,i], [ct,i, ht,i]} .
(2)
Each input is projected to a fixed hidden dimension
and processed by the policy network fϕ, which pre-
dicts a commitment score for each masked position.
To isolate the effect of the input from policy capac-
ity (Section 3.2), we keep the policy architecture
fixed across configurations, varying only the input
projection.
Comparison. As shown in Figure 3, confidence
provides a strong low-dimensional signal, whereas
hidden states alone require substantially more
NFEs to reach the same accuracy on both tasks.
Adding the margin yields no consistent gain. Com-
bining confidence with hidden states reaches the
98% and 95% targets with the fewest NFE on both
tasks, e.g., 42.1 versus 52.9 NFE for confidence
alone at the 98% target on GSM8K. At the 90%
target, however, confidence alone is comparable
on GSM8K and slightly more efficient on MBPP.
On MATH-500 and HumanEval, the benefit is less
consistent, and adding the margin is competitive or
better at the highest targets (Section B.6). These
results suggest that hidden states can complement
confidence; we hypothesize that their high dimen-
sionality makes it difficult for a lightweight policy
to exploit them alone. This gain comes at a cost:
projecting the 4096-dimensional hidden state in-
creases the policy from 331K to 856K parameters.
Finding 2. Hidden representations underper-
form confidence when used alone but can com-
plement it, mainly reducing the NFE needed
to reach high accuracy targets; the gain varies
across tasks.
3.5
Do NFE Gains Translate to Speedups?
The comparisons above measure decoding effi-
ciency by NFE. While NFE captures the number of
backbone evaluations, it does not account for the
computational overhead of the policy itself. This
trade-off depends on policy size (Table 1), includ-
ing the 331K-to-856K increase from [c, h], and on
backbone latency, which varies with prompt length
and caching (Wu et al., 2026). We therefore profile
per-step latency across these settings to determine
how much NFE reduction is needed to improve
throughput (Table 2; details in Section B.5). Be-
sides our 331K and 856K policies, we include ran-
domly initialized policies of 7.5M and 300M pa-
rameters, approximately matching TraceLock and
LUP-hidden.
The key result is that the break-even NFE reduc-
tion remains small for lightweight policies. Up to
7.5M parameters, the policy adds a nearly constant
cost of about 1.5 ms per step, at most 10% across

5.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and backbones.
We follow the
setup of Section 3.2 and extend the evaluation
to MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman
et al., 2024) and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), so
that GSM8K and MATH-500 evaluate in-domain
generalization and HumanEval and MBPP evaluate
out-of-domain generalization to code. We addition-
ally evaluate on Dream-7B-Instruct (Ye et al., 2025)
(Section 5.4); implementation details are provided
in Section C.
Baselines. We compare against two training-free
methods, Fast-dLLM (Wu et al., 2026), which com-
mits positions whose confidence exceeds a thresh-
old, and EB-Sampler (Ben-Hamu et al., 2025),
which commits positions within an entropy budget,
and against Learn2PD (Bao et al., 2026), a learned
policy for which we use the official checkpoint. We
do not include LUP (Jazbec et al., 2026) or Trace-
Lock (Sun et al., 2026), as no trained checkpoints
are publicly available for either method.
Evaluation protocol.
We report accuracy on
GSM8K and MATH-500 and Pass@1 on Hu-
manEval and MBPP, together with NFE and TPS,
with all throughput measured on the same hardware
and configuration (Section C.5).
