SyRA-CS: Synthetic-to-Real Adaptation for Code-Switched ASR with
Meta-Learning
Fadel Thior, Ahmer Tabassum, Haania Siddiqui, Hanan Aldarmaki, Thamar Solorio
Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificially Intelligence
fadel.thior@mbzuai.ac.ae
Abstract
Code-switched (CS) automatic speech recogni-
tion (ASR) remains difficult because real CS
speech is scarce and synthetic speech does
not fully match it.
We investigate whether
synthetic CS speech is more useful for learn-
ing an adaptation-friendly initialization than
for ordinary continual fine-tuning. We pro-
pose SyRA-CS, a pair-specific synthetic-to-
real adaptation framework based on Reptile
meta-learning.
For each language pair and
pretrained model, we meta-train on the cor-
responding synthetic CS-FLEURS subset and
then adapt using one hour of labeled real target
speech. We evaluate four architectural settings:
Encoder–CTC, FastConformer–RNN-T, Atten-
tion Encoder–Decoder (AED) and Speech-
LLM. Experiments cover on Arabic-, French-,
German-, and Hindi-English, against synthetic-
only transfer, real-only fine-tuning, and con-
tinual fine-tuning. SyRA-CS improves over
continual fine-tuning on WER in all 16 model–
pair comparisons and on CER in 15 of 16, with
relative macro WER reductions of 17.7% for
XLS-R and 5.3–5.8% for the other three mod-
els. The gains hold across the tested models
but vary in size across architectures, language
pairs and metrics.

Introduction
Code-switching (CS)—the alternation of two or
more languages within a conversation or utterance—
is a common phenomenon in multilingual commu-
nities, but presents additional challenges for Au-
tomatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems. The
challenge is both linguistic and data-centric: real
CS corpora are expensive to collect and transcribe,
and switching patterns vary with speaker commu-
nity, accent, discourse function, topic, and ortho-
graphic convention.
As a result, ASR models
trained for one CS condition often degrade under
another (Sitaram et al., 2019; Lyu et al., 2010; Li
and Fung, 2012).
Synthetic CS speech offers an attractive way to
cheaply expand supervision data (Yan et al., 2025;
Dhawan et al., 2023), but synthetic speech does not
perfectly match real speech. Synthetic utterances
may have cleaner acoustics, less speaker and ac-
cent variability. Therefore, simply fine-tuning on
synthetic CS speech may overfit to synthetic regu-
larities instead of generalizing to real CS recogni-
tion. An alternative approach is meta-learning: a
method that trains a model to become adaptable,
by repeatedly simulating small adaptation episodes
and updating the parameters so that a few gradi-
ent steps on a new, data-scarce task lead to good
performance (Finn et al., 2017). The output is not
a final recognizer but an initialization from which
fine-tuning on a small labeled set is fast and sta-
ble. This paper asks a focused question: under a
limited real data budget, can a meta-learned ini-
tialization from synthetic CS speech adapt better
to real CS speech compared to standard training
strategies? We use Reptile (Nichol et al., 2018)
because it is a first-order meta-learning algorithm
that repeatedly simulates adaptation episodes and
moves the initialization toward parameters that are
easy to reach after a few gradient steps: our con-
tribution is not a new meta-learning optimizer or
a benchmark of all meta-learning algorithms (Sec-
tion 4.1). Instead, we test whether a lightweight
meta-initializer is useful for synthetic-to-real CS
ASR under severe target scarcity. We train one Rep-
tile initialization for each combination of language
pair and pretrained model. Our experiments cover
four architectural settings: XLS-R 300M with a
CTC prediction head (Babu et al., 2021), Nemotron
3.5 with a FastConformer–RNN-T architecture
(NVIDIA, 2026a), Whisper-small with an atten-
tion encoder–decoder architecture (OpenAI, 2026),
and Nemotron-Labs-Audex-2B as a Speech-LLM
(NVIDIA, 2026b). Within each model–pair combi-
nation, the training regimes start from the same pre-

Our contributions are:
• We formulate pair-specific synthetic-to-real code-

real target speech
• We investigate Reptile across four architectural

attention encoder–decoder, and Speech-LLM, us-
ing comparisons against synthetic-only transfer,
real-only fine-tuning, and continual fine-tuning
within each pretrained model.
• We characterize the code-switching profile of
each real target set (switching ratio) and assess
the robustness of the gains to the two smallest
evaluation sets.

Related Work
2.1
Multilingual self-supervised
Self-supervised speech learning has become the
dominant paradigm for low-resource ASR. Baevski
et al. (2020) introduced wav2vec 2.0, which learns
contextualized acoustic representations from raw
unlabeled audio and can then be fine-tuned with
limited transcribed speech. HuBERT improved
masked prediction with cluster-based targets (Hsu
et al., 2021), and XLS-R scaled the wav2vec 2.0
recipe to nearly half a million hours of multi-
lingual audio across 128 languages (Babu et al.,
2021). Large multilingual speech resources such
as FLEURS (Conneau et al., 2022), Common
Voice (Ardila et al., 2020), VoxPopuli (Wang et al.,
2021), and MMS (Pratap et al., 2023) have further
strengthened multilingual transfer.
2.2
Code-switched ASR and synthetic data
A recent systematic review of end-to-end CS-ASR
studies notes that, although data augmentation is
widely used, it remains less explored than many
model-centric approaches (Agro et al., 2025). In
response, recent studies have used synthetic text
and speech to improve low-resource code-switched
ASR. Several works explore how synthetic code-
switched data can be created or leveraged when
real code-switched speech is scarce. Nguyen and
Tran (2025b) show that ASR systems can be trained
with synthetic phrase-level code-switching derived
from monolingual resources, while Nguyen and
Tran (2025a) propose AsyncSwitch, which adapts
models on large amounts of code-switched text be-
fore aligning this knowledge with limited paired
speech. More broadly, Hamed et al. (2025) find that
the value of synthetic code-switched data depends
strongly on its quality and the downstream task. At
the same time, recent work has introduced stronger
benchmarks and architectures for code-switched
ASR. Yan et al. (2025) present CS-FLEURS, a
multilingual benchmark covering 113 unique code-
switched language pairs, and show that synthetic
training data improves performance on both seen
and unseen pairs. Ugan et al. (2024) show that
bilingual ASR systems still struggle on realistic
code-switching benchmarks, while Ye et al. (2024)
propose a mixture-of-experts architecture to bet-
ter handle language switching in streaming and
non-streaming settings. Earlier work also explored
semi-supervised and unified approaches (Tazakka
et al., 2024; Dhawan et al., 2023). Overall, these
works show that synthetic data is becoming an
important tool for code-switched ASR, especially
in low-resource settings where real code-switched
speech is scarce. They also suggest that the main
challenge is not only generating more data, but gen-
erating data that is linguistically and acoustically
useful for adaptation.
2.3
Meta-learning for ASR
Meta-learning is well suited to low-resource ASR
because it explicitly learns parameters that can
adapt quickly to new conditions with limited su-
pervision. Early work applied MAML-style meta-
learning to end-to-end ASR, learning initializations
from high-resource languages that adapt quickly to
low-resource targets (Hsu et al., 2020), and Winata
et al. (2020) extended this idea to code-switched
speech with meta-transfer learning, which draws
episodes from monolingual corpora so that the ini-
tialization transfers to limited real CS data. Our
work differs in that episodes are sampled from syn-
thetic CS speech of the same language pair and
the meta-learner is first-order Reptile rather than
MAML. Recent ASR work has strengthened this di-

3.2
Source tasks: CS-FLEURS data
Our synthetic source data are drawn from CS-
FLEURS (Yan et al., 2025), a corpus of syn-
thetic code-switched speech spanning multiple lan-
guage pairs. We use four code-switched subsets
from XTTS version as it contains training data for
our meta learning experiments: Hindi-English (6
hours), French-English (10.28h), Arabic-English
(4.51 hours) and German-English (9.37 hours),
where each subset corresponds to one language
pair and the reported durations denote the total
amount of synthetic training audio available for
that pair. For a model a and target pair p, synthetic
episodes are sampled only from the corresponding
source subset Sp. Thus, each meta-training run
learns a model-specific, pair-specific initialization
rather than an initialization trained jointly across
language pairs.
3.3
Target tasks: MUCS and Switchlingua
data
We evaluate adaptation under real-world code-
switched conditions using all four pairs as target
tasks. Real-world Hindi-English speech data is
from MUCS (Diwan et al., 2021), French-English
(2.64 hours), Arabic-English (1.43 hours) and
German-English (1.40 hours) are from the Switch-
Lingua dataset (Xie et al., 2025).
SwitchLin-
gua provides real audio recordings from bilingual
speakers, but the spoken prompts are generated
code-switched text samples. Thus, the target au-
dio is human speech, while the linguistic content
follows a synthetic-script collection protocol. Un-
like SwitchLingua, MUCS Hindi-English is ex-
tracted from human spoken tutorials. The speech
is therefore real instructional audio, and the code-
switching primarily arises from technical lecture
content rather than from generated code-switched
text. For each target pair, we randomly select a
1-hour labeled subset for adaptation and evaluate
final performance on the corresponding real code-
switched test set. As CS speech data is scarce, this
setup is intentionally low-resource and is designed
to measure how effectively a model trained on syn-
thetic code-switched speech can transfer to real
speech with only a small amount of target-domain
supervision.

Reptile is particularly suitable for our setting be-

This overhead is particularly relevant when repeat-

the inner loop uses each model’s transcription ob-

on synthetic CS episodes produces an initializa-
tion that transfers more effectively to limited real
target speech than ordinary synthetic-domain fine-
tuning. This hypothesis is tested through within-
model comparisons against CFT. Reptile’s first-
order formulation reduces the derivative overhead
associated with standard second-order MAML, but
does not remove the cost of repeated inner-loop
training.
