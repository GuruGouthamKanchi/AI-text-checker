Fine-Grained Analysis of Catastrophic Forgetting in
Continual Learning via Algorithmic Stability
Guodong Zheng1, Yan Sun2, Peng Wang1, Enneng Yang1, Jiafei Lyu3, Xiaochun Cao1, Dacheng Tao2, Li Shen1
Abstract—Understanding catastrophic forgetting, in which
learning new tasks degrades performance on previously learned
tasks, remains a fundamental challenge in continual learning
(CL). Existing theoretical analyses often rely on specific model
or task structures and offer limited insight into how iterative
optimization affects forgetting. To address this gap, we develop
an algorithmic stability framework for expected population
forgetting under SGD without prescribing a particular model
architecture. Specifically, we decompose forgetting into intra-
task and cross-task generalization gaps and empirical forgetting.
To bound the generalization gaps, we track how a sample
perturbation affects updates within a task and propagates through
subsequent tasks. We consider constant, intra-task decaying, and
continuously decaying learning-rate schedules. Comparing the
two decaying schedules allows us to characterize how restarting
learning-rate decay at task boundaries affects the propagation
of historical sample perturbations. For empirical forgetting, we
derive bounds for all three schedules by retaining cumulative
gradient alignment along the optimization trajectory, without
requiring convexity. Combining these results yields upper bounds
on expected forgetting that capture the effects of training set size,
the number of updates per task, the total number of tasks, learning-
rate schedules, and cumulative gradient alignment. Finally, we
conduct experiments on Split CIFAR-100 and Split MiniImageNet
to examine the empirical implications of our theoretical findings.
Index Terms—Catastrophic forgetting, algorithmic stability,
intra-task generalization gap, cross-task generalization gap,
empirical forgetting.
I. INTRODUCTION
Continual learning (CL) aims to learn from a sequence of
non-stationary data streams and tasks over time. However, this
classical training paradigm remains vulnerable to a fundamental
challenge, known as catastrophic forgetting [1, 2], in which
the model exhibits a substantial degradation in performance on
previously learned tasks as it adapts to newly introduced tasks.
To mitigate catastrophic forgetting, several empirical methods
have been proposed and have achieved promising results [3].
However, the theoretical understanding remains much less
developed. Existing analyses have largely focused on the
linear regime [4–8] or the neural tangent kernel (NTK)
regime [9–11]. While linear models yield explicit forgetting
bounds, the resulting guarantees depend on the assumed linear

networks, but typically rely on infinite-width limits. Moreover,
most of these studies analyze forgetting through closed-form
solutions to the optimization problem, abstracting away the
fine-grained dynamics of iterative updates and yielding coarse
bounds. Recently, Zheng et al. [11] analyze forgetting dynamics
through NTK gradient flow, which captures more detailed
trajectories than conventional static analyses. Taheri et al. [12]
characterize forgetting in two-layer quadratic neural networks
in the kernel regime, deriving explicit empirical forgetting
bounds for orthogonal XOR-cluster tasks and using algorithmic
stability to control delayed generalization. These studies provide
insights into how forgetting evolves during training, while
their guarantees remain tied to specific kernel approximations,
network architectures, or task structures. This motivates an
analysis of training dynamics, without prescribing a particular
network architecture, that tracks both within-task updates and
the propagation of their effects across tasks.
In this work, we develop an algorithmic stability analysis
of expected forgetting in CL. We quantify forgetting as the
expected increase in population risk on historical tasks and
decompose it into intra-task generalization gaps (IGG), cross-
task generalization gaps (CGG), and empirical forgetting (EF).
To bound the generalization terms, we characterize stability
under a sample perturbation in either the current task or a
historical task. We follow SGD trajectories to track how the
perturbation affects updates within that task and propagates
through subsequent tasks. This analysis yields generalization
bounds for constant, intra-task decaying, and continuously
decaying learning rate schedules in the convex setting, and for
the two decaying schedules in the non-convex setting. Under
intra-task decay, the learning rate decreases from the same
initial value within each task, whereas under continuous decay,
it continues decreasing across task boundaries. Comparing
these schedules allows us to characterize how resetting the
learning rate at task boundaries affects the propagation of
historical sample perturbations. For EF, we analyze changes
in empirical risk on historical tasks along the optimization
trajectory, retaining the cumulative inner products between
gradients of historical tasks and those of the current task. The
EF analysis covers all three learning rate schedules without
requiring convexity. Combining these results yields bounds on
expected population forgetting that explicitly account for both
generalization gaps and empirical risk changes.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We derive explicit IGG and CGG bounds for continual
SGD by tracking sample perturbations within a task and
through subsequent tasks. These bounds characterize the
effects of sample size, local training length, the total
number of tasks, and learning-rate schedules.
• We derive an EF bound in terms of cumulative gradient
alignment and an explicit second-order term involving
squared learning rates. The bound reveals how positive
cumulative gradient alignment contributes to controlling
empirical forgetting.
• We derive upper bounds on expected forgetting through
combining the IGG, CGG, and EF bounds, linking
generalization with changes in empirical risk along the
continual learning trajectory.
II. RELATED WORK
Empirical studies in CL. Continual learning has made
substantial progress in empirical methods for mitigating catas-
trophic forgetting. We divide these methods into three main
families: (1) replay-based methods, which either store data
from previous tasks and replay it during training on the current
task [13–15], or store gradient or feature information from prior
tasks and constrain the current-task gradient to be orthogonal
to the stored information [16–20]; (2) regularization-based
methods, which aim to restrain updates of parameters that are
important for previous tasks [21, 22], or maintain consistency of
intermediate features by adding a regularization term to the loss
[23]; and (3) architecture-based methods, which dynamically
expand or adapt the model architecture to preserve knowledge
from previous tasks [24–26].
Theoretical analysis of CL. Recent theoretical studies
on CL have mainly focused on catastrophic forgetting and
generalization. Broadly speaking, the existing literature can
be divided into two main lines of work: linear model analysis
and NTK-based analysis. Under the linear regime, Evron et al.
[4], Lin et al. [5] investigate forgetting and generalization under
the assumptions of overparameterized linear models. Ding et al.
[6] study forgetting in both overparameterized and underpa-
rameterized regimes while relaxing the data assumptions to
fourth-moment conditions. Moreover, several works use linear
models to analyze concrete methods for mitigating forgetting,
including replay-based methods [8, 27, 28], regularization-
based methods [7, 29–31], and mixture-of-experts methods
[32]. The other main line of theoretical work relies on the NTK
regime. Specifically, Bennani et al. [33] establish generalization
error bounds for CL based on the NTK and Rademacher

complexity. Doan et al. [9] analyze forgetting by introducing
the NTK overlap matrix as a task similarity metric. Karakida
and Akaho [10] study forgetting and generalization error
through learning-curve analysis between two tasks. Zheng
et al. [11] further characterize the training-time dynamics of
forgetting and generalization in the general CL setting. Taheri
et al. [12] analyze forgetting in two-layer quadratic neural
networks in the kernel regime, deriving explicit empirical
forgetting bounds for orthogonal XOR-cluster tasks and using
algorithmic stability to control delayed generalization. Liu et al.
[34] derive generalization bounds for parameter-efficient fine-
tuning in CL. However, these theoretical analyses have several
limitations: (1) they rely on restrictive model assumptions,
such as linear models, infinite-width models, or infinitesimal
learning rates, which make the results less general; and (2)
the upper bounds derived in the NTK setting often involve
complicated kernel matrices, making them difficult to interpret
and generalize. In contrast, we analyze forgetting through a
fine-grained characterization of the training trajectory without
restrictive model assumptions. Moreover, our bounds depend
only on a few readily interpretable quantities, such as the
training set size, local training length, learning-rate schedule,
and gradient inner products between tasks.
The work most closely related to ours is that of Taheri et al.
[12], who analyze forgetting using algorithmic stability. Our
analysis differs in three aspects: (1) Their theoretical results are
developed for two-layer neural networks with quadratic activa-
tions and fixed output-layer weights, whereas our analysis does
not prescribe a particular network architecture. (2) Their explicit
empirical forgetting guarantees rely on orthogonal XOR-cluster
tasks and the specified network model. Our EF bound requires
only that each sample loss be Lipschitz continuous and smooth
with respect to the model parameters, without imposing these
structural assumptions. Their delayed generalization analysis
also uses Lipschitz and smoothness assumptions on the loss.
(3) Their main forgetting and generalization theorems adopt
constant learning rates, whereas we examine constant, intra-task
decaying, and continuously decaying learning rates.

A. Continual Learning Setting
We consider the standard CL setting with T ≥2 sequential
training tasks. For any positive integer n, let [n] := {1, . . . , n}.
For each task τ ∈[T], let Dτ denote the data distribution.
To simplify the analysis, we assume that each task dataset
contains n samples, and write Sτ = (zτ,1, . . . , zτ,n) for the
corresponding training dataset drawn i.i.d. from Dτ. The
datasets are independent across tasks. We denote the task
sequence by S1:T := {S1, . . . , ST }, which collects the datasets
from tasks 1 to T. Let f denote a differentiable loss function

B. Algorithmic Stability

concerns perturbations in the current training data only. In CL,
by contrast, the model is exposed to a sequence of tasks,
so perturbations at different tasks can propagate differently
through the subsequent training trajectory. To reflect this
sequential structure, we propose intra-task uniform stability
for perturbations on the current task and cross-task uniform
stability for perturbations on a historical task.

∥∇f(u; z) −∇f(v; z)∥≤β∥u −v∥.
Assumption 3 (Convex). For every z ∈Z, the function f(·; z) :
Ω→R is convex, that is, for all u, v ∈Ω,
f(u; z) ≥f(v; z) + ⟨∇f(v; z), u −v⟩.

Based on the decomposition in Equation (4), we analyze the
forgetting measure FT from the following three aspects.
(1) Intra-Task Generalization Gap (Section IV-A). This term
captures the generalization behavior of the trained model on
the current task. A smaller absolute intra-task generalization
gap indicates closer agreement between the empirical risk and
the population risk on the current task.
(2) Cross-Task Generalization Gap (Section IV-B). This
term reflects the generalization behavior of the model trained
on later tasks when evaluated on earlier task distributions. A
smaller absolute cross-task generalization gap indicates closer
agreement between the empirical risk and the population risk

(3) Empirical Forgetting (Section IV-C). This term captures
how much performance on the training set of task i deteriorates
after learning T tasks. A smaller empirical forgetting indicates
that the model forgets less of the knowledge from the training
set of a previous task.

Remark 6. Theorem 5 surprisingly shows that the bound for
the CGG coincides with that for the IGG under convex loss
functions. This indicates that, under convexity, the algorithmic
stability of SGD is preserved throughout the entire sequence of
tasks. Even as the model parameters are continuously updated
using data from new tasks, the resulting bound on the expected
generalization gap on historical tasks does not grow. This
conclusion relies on the non-expansive property of convex and
smooth loss functions, as established in Lemma 1. Therefore,
the discussion of IGG bound in Remark 3 remains valid for
CGG as well.
