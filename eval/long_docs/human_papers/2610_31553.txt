Abstract
Industrial organizations hold operational knowledge in structured records, such as telemetry, point
lists, and transactions, and in unstructured sources, such as manuals, service notes, communications, and
inspection reports. Governance programs often manage these sources separately. Classifying, linking, and
documenting them depends on a small group of employees with deep domain knowledge. Agents built on
large language models can propose metadata, cite documents, and flag quality issues, but unreviewed
proposals can spread errors into downstream systems. A perfunctory approval leaves no record of what a
reviewer checked. We present contestable governance: specialist agents produce scored, cited arguments
for disputed metadata decisions; a steward can accept, reject, modify, or add arguments before commit;
and a governance record retains the evidence, interventions, and outcome. A versioned domain glossary
supplies model-specific terms and acronym definitions to the agents. We describe the workflow, a triage
path that reserves full argumentation for uncertain proposals while preserving review before commit, the
scoring and record models, and a gate that controls which verified decisions become precedent. Two
HVAC use cases illustrate the pattern: point classification using Brick Schema class labels, supported by
an open reference implementation, and an illustrative warranty failure-mode decision linking claim fields
and telemetry to service notes and revisioned bulletins.
Keywords: data governance, metadata management, AI agents, contestable AI, computational argumen-
tation, data lineage, domain vocabulary, Brick Schema, structured and unstructured data

Introduction
Manufacturers of commercial HVAC equipment depend on data in two forms. Connected chillers, rooftop
units, and air handlers stream structured telemetry from hundreds of points per unit, while installation,
operation, and maintenance manuals define those points in prose that varies by model and firmware revision.
Factory end-of-line tests produce structured pass and fail results, while PDF specifications and change notices
explain the test limits and engineering deviations. Warranty and service systems hold claim codes and
part numbers, while technicians describe failures in free-text notes. Governance programs typically assign
these sources to different tools and owners, leaving employees to remember the links between fields and the
documents that define them.
That arrangement breaks down as the installed base grows. Every new product line, firmware update, and
connected site generates data that must be classified and linked to documentation before remote monitoring

steward reviews a new model, its points may remain unclassified or carry incorrect classes. If a mixed-air
temperature sensor is labeled as discharge air, for example, a remote monitoring system could interpret
normal mixing as a failed heating coil and send a technician to the site with an incorrect diagnosis.
AI agents built on large language models change the economics of this work. Foundation models can
match schemas, clean entries, and infer the meaning of cryptic field names with little task-specific training
(Narayan et al., 2022). Agents that combine a model with retrieval and extraction tools can read a manual,
compare a field with prior decisions, and propose a classification (Wang et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2020). They
also introduce a governance problem. An agent that writes metadata directly to a catalog can propagate an
error to every report and application that consumes it. A wrong class assigned to one air handler can spread
across a portfolio when its mapping is copied to other units of the same model. Reviewers who approve
output with a single click risk rubber-stamping it, a documented pattern in which people accept automated
recommendations with little scrutiny (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010; Buçinca et al., 2021). The resulting
record says that a person approved the decision without showing what they checked. When a reviewer
overrules an agent, for example by reclassifying a supplier’s part number, the correction may stay in an email
or ticket that the agent never sees. The agent then repeats the mistake. Months later, an engineer investigating
a fault rule that fires on the wrong point cannot tell whether its classification came from a model, a reviewer,
or a bulk import.
Closing this gap requires seven properties, which we use as requirements throughout the paper: proposals
must cite the structured fields and documents they rely on (R1); a reviewer must be able to contest them
before commit (R2); every decision must be attributable to an agent, a model version, and a named reviewer,
as applicable (R3); history must be append-only (R4); the full lineage of a decision must be retrievable in one
query (R5); verified decisions must pass a gate before becoming precedent (R6); and reviewer interventions
must be stored as measurable data (R7).
Here, lineage means the trace from source evidence through agent proposals and human edits to committed
metadata. We use PROV, the World Wide Web Consortium’s provenance data model, to describe that trace: its
entities include documents and decision records, its activities include generating and reviewing arguments, and
its agents are the models and people responsible (Moreau and Missier, 2013). PROV provides a conceptual
mapping in this paper; the reference implementation does not store records in a PROV serialization.
This paper argues that contestability belongs in the data layer of agent-assisted governance. A contestable
system lets a qualified person challenge the reasoning before an output is committed and retains that
intervention in the decision record (Hirsch et al., 2017; Alfrink et al., 2023). In our proposed pattern, agents
attach cited evidence to each decision, specialist agents argue disputed cases, and a steward can contest a
proposal before commit. A governance record shows who made each decision and how the steward intervened
(Section 3). We discuss two HVAC use cases (Section 4). The first is point classification, supported by an
open reference implementation. The second is an illustrative warranty failure-mode classification that shows
how a steward could resolve conflicting evidence across structured service records and revisioned documents.

Background and Related Work
Khatri and Brown (2010) frame data governance as a set of decision domains covering data principles, data
quality, metadata, data access, and data lifecycle, with explicit assignment of who holds decision rights
in each. Weber et al. (2009) show that the right governance structure depends on organizational context,
and Otto (2011) documents how large firms organize stewardship roles in practice. Abraham et al. (2019)
consolidate this literature into a framework of structural, procedural, and relational mechanisms, and the
DAMA body of knowledge sets out the operational practices stewards follow (DAMA International, 2017).
Across this work, stewardship is a human activity performed alongside operations, and its outputs, such as
2


approved definitions and classifications, are stored separately from the reasoning that produced them.
Large data catalogs show what happens when documentation depends entirely on engineers: Google’s
Goods system infers metadata for billions of datasets after creation because requiring documentation up
front did not scale (Halevy et al., 2016). Provenance research distinguishes why a result was produced from
where its data came from (Buneman et al., 2001). PROV formalizes this account through relationships among
entities, activities, and agents (Moreau and Missier, 2013). Other work describes what metadata should
enable. The FAIR principles call for data to be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (Wilkinson
et al., 2016), while datasheets ask producers to document how datasets were collected and processed (Gebru
et al., 2021). Data quality provides a way to judge the result across dimensions that depend on fitness for use
(Wang and Strong, 1996). Together, these works motivate recording evidence and responsibility close to the
decision that creates metadata.
Point naming in building automation makes the metadata problem concrete. A controller exposes
thousands of points, and one vendor may label an air handler’s supply-air temperature sensor AHU3_SA_T,
while another uses SupplyAirTemp_03. Brick defines a shared ontology of building equipment, points,
and their relationships (Balaji et al., 2016). Vendors still name points differently, so someone must map raw
names to Brick classes before energy reports and fault detection rules can use them consistently. Automated
approaches infer mappings from names, for example by learning parsing rules from a few expert examples
(Bhattacharya et al., 2015) or transferring learned mappings across buildings through an intermediate
representation (Koh et al., 2018). A name alone can mislead when vendor conventions differ, and relevant
manuals may resolve the ambiguity. Foundation models can read those manuals (Narayan et al., 2022); the
question is how to use both kinds of evidence while keeping a reviewer in control of what is committed.
Freeman et al. (2026) compared four sequential single-task agents with one multi-task agent on a fixed
ERP incident, then added a reconciler to inspect contradictions. They also tested glossary injection on
a separate HVAC troubleshooting query. Their judge-based results motivate role separation and explicit
vocabulary here, but do not establish that either measure improves the factual correctness of metadata
decisions in this workflow.
Contestability has been studied as a design goal for machine learning systems whose users need to
question outputs as part of their professional practice (Hirsch et al., 2017). Later work examined how
people understand challenges to algorithmic decisions (Lyons et al., 2021) and proposed design practices for
contestable AI (Alfrink et al., 2023). These studies describe what a person should be able to do; computational
argumentation offers one way to represent what they can challenge. Abstract argumentation frameworks
model arguments and their attacks on one another (Dung, 1995), while bipolar frameworks add support
relations (Cayrol and Lagasquie-Schiex, 2005). Quantitative bipolar argumentation frameworks (QBAFs)
assign base scores and compute how support and attack affect argument strength, as in DF-QuAD (Rago
et al., 2016). An argument graph divides a decision into claims that a person can challenge individually. Such
graphs have been proposed for contestable AI (Leofante et al., 2024), and recent work constructs them from
language-model-generated arguments (Freedman et al., 2024). We apply this approach to data governance
and add a storage model that preserves each reviewer’s edits alongside the arguments they changed.
3
Our Approach
3.1
Workflow
The full contestation path has four phases. In data analysis, an analyzer reads structured inputs, such as a
point list or schema, gathers relevant documents and glossary entries, and retrieves similar verified decisions
from a precedent library. In multi-agent argumentation, specialist agents submit supporting or challenging
arguments only when their evidence bears on a candidate; each argument has a base score and a source
reference (R1). Lack of evidence for one candidate does not, by itself, support another. The agents have
3


distinct evidence roles: one might inspect a point name, another the equipment manual, and a third the
applicability of a document revision. Their disagreements show a reviewer where the evidence diverges. In
human contestation, a steward can accept, reject, modify, or add arguments. The system rescores after each
change, and no decision is committed before review ends (R2). In selection and storage, the scoring engine
selects the highest-scoring candidate, a finalizer explains the outcome and propagation steps, and the system
stores the evidence, arguments, interventions, and decision in a governance record (R3 to R5). The finalizer
does not choose the class; an auditor can recompute it from the stored arguments without rerunning a model.

The human steward may accept, reject, modify, or add arguments during review. The
scoring engine selects the highest-scoring candidate. The finalizer explains the result and propagation steps
but does not choose the class. The auditor can recompute the class from the stored arguments without
rerunning a model.
3.2
Domain vocabulary
Acronyms and equipment terms often change meaning across vendors, product families, and revisions. Before
the agents evaluate a case, the analyzer can supply a controlled glossary containing only terms present in its
evidence packet. Each entry needs an expansion, a definition, its equipment and revision scope, the source
4


passage that authorizes it, a version, and a steward owner. For example, DISCH may identify the discharge
side of a mixing box on one air handler but a post-coil location on another. A universal expansion would
conceal that difference. Selecting relevant entries also avoids filling each agent’s context with unrelated
definitions, a tradeoff explored in the glossary experiments of Freeman et al. (2026).
The glossary is governed metadata, not an unquestionable prompt instruction. Agents should cite both
an applicable glossary entry and its source document when a term affects a proposal. A reviewer who finds
an obsolete or ambiguous definition can challenge it, record the correction, and prevent the unverified entry
from becoming precedent. The proposed decision record would store the glossary version used so that later
changes can identify decisions needing review.
3.3
Triage
Running every decision through full argumentation requires model calls and reviewer time, even though
many points on a new unit are unambiguous. A typed decision model such as Jev, which returns a bounded
choice with a probability instead of generated text (TypeSafe AI, 2026), could classify points first and send
only low-confidence proposals through multi-agent argumentation. In a separate controlled benchmark of
100 agent tasks, a similar routing design reduced calls to a strong language model by 72.7% while reaching
95% task success (Wu and Lim, 2026). Those figures do not measure this governance workflow.
High-confidence proposals still go to a steward with their source evidence, model version, and probability.
Each remains contestable before an explicit review decision and catalog commit (R2). Its governance record
stores the question, evaluated state, reviewer action, and outcome even when no argument graph was generated
(R3). The threshold that selects the review path is itself a governance decision: it needs a named owner
and must be stored with every record it affects. A reviewer can escalate any proposal to full argumentation
because a probability alone gives no specific reasoning to challenge.
3.4
Argument scoring
Each decision on the full contestation path uses a quantitative bipolar argumentation framework (QBAF)
⟨A,R+,R−,τ⟩, drawing on bipolar and quantitative argumentation (Cayrol and Lagasquie-Schiex, 2005; Rago
et al., 2016). “Bipolar” means that arguments can support or attack one another; “quantitative” means that
they have numerical strengths. The set A contains the active arguments. For an argument x, R+(x) contains its
supporters, R−(x) contains its attackers, and τ(x) ∈[0,1] is its unitless base score. The framework specifies
the range of τ(x) but does not determine its value: an assessor must assign that input before the graph is
scored. Within a candidate, supporting arguments support one another, as do challenging arguments. Every
supporting argument attacks every challenging argument, and vice versa. Let f t(x) denote the computed
strength of argument x after iteration t, with f 0(x) = τ(x).
We use the signed-sum logistic update from the open reference implementation as an illustrative QBAF
scoring rule. Its support and attack contributions have explicit signs, its inputs can be stored for independent
recomputation, and the logistic function bounds strengths in (0,1). This choice preserves the reference
implementation’s arithmetic for the worked examples; it is not evidence that this rule is preferable. DF-QuAD
(Rago et al., 2016) and other modular weighted semantics for bipolar graphs (Potyka, 2019) offer alternative
ways to aggregate and propagate support and attack. The unitless coefficient α scales every support and
attack contribution before the logistic transform. We fix α = 0.3 as an illustrative uniform edge weight, not
a parameter fitted to outcomes. These strengths are comparison scores, not calibrated probabilities. For a
positive integer n, we update all strengths simultaneously for n iterations:

Here, sig(u) = 1/(1 + e−u) is the logistic function. The summation variable y ranges over arguments

The first update uses each neighbor’s base score f 0 = τ, before that neighbor’s own supporters and
attackers have changed its strength. Later updates propagate those changes through the graph, including its
reciprocal links, until the strengths approach a fixed point. Iteration seeks a self-consistent graph score; it
does not add evidence or change τ.
For the worked warranty example, we set n = 10, matching the reference implementation’s default1
and serving as a case-specific numerical stopping count. S1 moves from f 1 = 0.665 to f 10 = 0.683.
In the largest candidate graph of that example (Section 4.2), each of five arguments has at most four
incoming relations. Since the logistic derivative is at most 1/4, Equation 1 has a sup-norm contraction
bound q ≤(1/4)(0.3)(4) = 0.3. The saved reproducibility script finds a maximum maxx |f 10(x) −f 9(x)|
of 3.20×10−7 across the candidate graphs before and after review. The contraction-mapping error bound
(Rudin, 1976) multiplies this difference by q/(1−q), making the remaining error at most 1.37×10−7 per
argument and 6.85×10−7 per raw margin, below the three-decimal precision used to compare candidates.
A local sensitivity check at α = 0.2 and 0.4 preserves Sensor as the pre-review winner and Linkage as the
post-review winner, although the margins change. These checks establish numerical stability for this example,
not decision validity, calibration, or general robustness; larger graphs or different weights require a new
convergence check.
The reference implementation normalizes candidate margins for display; the worked examples report
raw margins. Reviewer operations change the active graph: a rejected argument is removed before rescoring
but retained in the record; an added argument enters with a base score of 0.85; a modified argument retains
its original text; and an accepted argument is marked as reviewed. With no incoming support or attack, the
logistic transform maps base scores in [0,1] to strengths between 0.50 and 0.73, rather than preserving an
isolated argument’s base score. This behavior should be assessed against published axioms for weighted
bipolar argumentation (Amgoud and Ben-Naim, 2018). The presence or absence of an argument can therefore
move a candidate’s score more than a modest change in its base score, making rejection a strong review
action. In the reference implementation, the agent that writes an argument also assigns its base score. For the
synthetic warranty example, we selected the base scores in Table 1 to illustrate the scoring and review steps;
they were not derived from a rubric, empirical calibration, or a rating study. In a deployment, a versioned
rubric would first check whether the cited source applies to the item and revision: an inapplicable source
should not contribute a scored argument. For each eligible argument, the rubric could assess the directness
and specificity of the evidence and record the assessor, rationale, and rubric version. Independent adjudication
would then be needed to test whether these assignments are repeatable and improve decisions. A separate
decision model could score arguments, separating authorship from assessment.
3.5
Governance record
The governance record connects a decision with its evidence and review history, so an auditor can retrieve
its lineage without manually reconciling logs, tickets, and catalogs. The reference implementation uses
MongoDB documents for arguments and audit entries, time series collections for sensor data, and vector
search over embedded documents. In the proposed cross-system schema, evidence sits in a context document;
each argument has its own document with base and final scores and edit fields; and the decision document
contains the outcome, scores before and after review, and an entry for every human edit. A shared session
identifier connects these documents, allowing one aggregation pipeline to return the decision’s lineage (R5):

This structure can be expressed in PROV terms (Moreau and Missier, 2013): source documents and

actors. The decision record is the resulting entity. Storing interventions as data also supports operational
questions: which agent’s arguments are rejected most often, how long review takes for a new equipment
model, and how many points the triage threshold routes to full argumentation (R7). To meet the append-only
requirement in a deployment, edits would need a sequenced event log and database roles that allow inserts
but prohibit updates and deletes (R4). Schema validation should require a named reviewer, model and prompt
versions, and a rationale for each intervention (R3).
3.6
Promotion gate
A verified record is useful both as a decision and as precedent for a similar case. Its embedding could enter
the vector index searched during data analysis, allowing the next unit of the same equipment model to retrieve
the reviewer’s corrections. An incorrect precedent could spread across a portfolio. The proposed promotion
gate therefore admits only verified records (R6), stores each promotion as an attributable event, and lets a
reviewer demote a record. Demotion would flag decisions that relied on the record for review.

Use Cases
4.1
Use case 1: HVAC point classification
Each connected HVAC unit exposes hundreds of points with vendor-specific names. Mapping them to
standard classes lets building management systems and analytics platforms interpret them consistently. The
open reference implementation https://github.com/mongodb-industry-solutions/iom
t-contestable-ai demonstrates contestable governance for this task on synthetic data using CrewAI
agents, GPT-4o, Voyage AI embeddings, and MongoDB (Figure 1).
The synthetic site contains 247 points, 18 of them flagged as ambiguous. Under the proposed triage
path, 229 points would receive a shorter evidence-based steward review, while the 18 ambiguous points
would receive full argumentation and review. None would be committed before review. The reference
implementation runs the full workflow on one point: AHU-1-DISCH-T, on an air handler from a fictional
EQ-7700 series. The analyzer retrieves precedents showing why this point needs review: DISCH-T maps to
mixed air on EQ-7700 air handlers and supply air on EQ-7700 fan coil units, while a similar name on another
vendor’s equipment refers to post-coil discharge air. Two agents argue over three option labels: brick:
Supply_Air_Temperature_Sensor, brick:Discharge_Air_Temperature_Sensor, and
brick:Mixed_Air_Temperature_Sensor. Brick 1.4.2 declares the supply-air and discharge-air
sensor classes equivalent and marks the latter as an alias of the former (Brick Consortium, Inc., 2024). The
three labels therefore represent two distinct ontology choices; the reference implementation nevertheless
scores the labels separately. The String Matcher sees only the point name, the evidence available to name-
based methods (Bhattacharya et al., 2015; Koh et al., 2018), and favors discharge air. The Documentation
Reader sees manual fragments and cites section 4.2, which places the sensor in the mixed-air plenum, and
Appendix A, which explains that DISCH refers to the discharge side of the mixing box.

display the near tie above as 1.00 and 0.99, which a reviewer might mistake for high confidence; raw margins
should appear alongside them. Base scores are assigned by the model itself, remain uncalibrated, and can vary
between runs. The reference implementation overwrites an argument on a second edit, seeds its precedent
library without writing verified decisions back, and has no triage step. Append-only history, the promotion
gate, and triage remain proposed extensions.
