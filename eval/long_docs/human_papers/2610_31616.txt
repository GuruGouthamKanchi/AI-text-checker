Qi Li

National University of Singapore

Abstract
On-policy distillation has been widely adopted
for alleviating the distribution mismatch of off-
policy distillation. Self-distillation further re-
moves the need for an external teacher by us-
ing the student’s own outputs, enhanced with
extra context or demonstrations, as supervi-
sion. This teacher-free design makes on-policy
self-distillation practical, yet its privacy risks
remain largely unexplored. In particular, us-
ing two coupled views of the same training
example may strengthen its memorization. Mo-
tivated by this, we present the first study of
membership inference attacks against on-policy
self-distillation. We investigate both gray-box
and black-box threat models and propose a uni-
fied attack framework, named OP-Print. OP-
Print builds on our finding that the supervision
imprint in on-policy self-distillation is sparse
and structured, concentrating on uncertainty-
sensitive correction sites rather than across the
entire rollout. Exploiting this property, we con-
struct attack signals tailored to the two access
levels: in the gray-box setting, OP-Print di-
rectly aggregates the privileged teacher-student
residual correction along the same rollout; in
the black-box setting, it measures restart-based
output-basin stability as an observable surro-
gate for the hidden correction process. Across
diverse models and distillation schemes, all
baseline attacks remain essentially at random
guessing even under gray-box access, whereas
OP-Print is broadly validated to be effective in
both gray-box and black-box settings.

Introduction
On-policy distillation has recently become an im-
portant paradigm for improving model perfor-
mance, as it alleviates the distribution mismatch of
off-policy distillation (Guo et al., 2025; Song and
Zheng, 2026; Ye et al., 2026b). Its self-distillation
variants go one step further by removing the need
*Corresponding Author
for an external teacher: the distillation signal is
instead derived from the student model itself, aug-
mented with additional context or demonstrations
that bootstrap its own capabilities (Zhao et al.,
2026a; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Sang et al., 2026;
He et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026a). This teacher-
free property makes on-policy self-distillation es-
pecially attractive in practice. In particular, the
model is exposed to two coupled views of the same
example: a standard version without privileged in-
formation and a privileged version augmented with
guidance such as a reference solution. While this
design improves reasoning performance, its privacy
implications have remained largely unexamined.
Intuitively, processing both views of the same sam-
ple may produce a stronger and more distinctive
training imprint, potentially making that sample
easier to memorize than under ordinary training.
Motivated by this concern, in this work, we in-
vestigate the data privacy risks of on-policy self-
distillation through the lens of membership infer-
ence attacks (Shi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Song
et al., 2019; Li et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2025).
Membership inference asks whether an adversary
can determine if a given sample was used in train-
ing (Shokri et al., 2017a; Carlini et al., 2022a;
Yeom et al., 2018). We explore this problem under
a unified gray-box and black-box threat model (Hu
et al., 2022; Wu and Cao, 2025; Bai et al., 2024).
We found this problem fundamentally nontrivial:
the supervision signal in on-policy self-distillation
is not globally visible, nor is it well captured by
coarse output-level heuristics (Zhang et al., 2024;
Hu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b). Instead, it is
sparse, structured, and tied to the model’s internal
correction process. More importantly, our analy-
sis shows that this signal is organized primarily
by token difficulty rather than absolute decoding
position. In other words, privileged correction con-
centrates on uncertain reasoning steps, while po-
sition alone provides only weak evidence. This

To address this problem, we propose a unified
framework, named OP-Print, that targets the same
hidden supervision imprint at two levels of ad-
versary observability. Our key insight is that on-
policy self-distillation leaves a sparse but structured
correction imprint concentrated on uncertainty-
sensitive reasoning tokens. In the gray-box setting,
this signature can be measured directly by com-
paring student-conditioned and privileged teacher-
conditioned behaviors along the same rollout. In
the black-box setting, the correction process itself
is hidden, but it still leaves observable traces in the
geometry of the output basin: when generation is
restarted from partial prefixes, member and non-
member examples differ in how stably their contin-
uations remain within a local solution basin. Based
on this observation, OP-Print instantiates two com-
plementary attacks. Its gray-box variant aggre-
gates teacher-student residual discrepancy along
self-rollouts into a direct membership score, while
its black-box variant probes restart-based output-
basin stability as a natural surrogate for the inac-
cessible correction signal. Together, they offer a
unified view of privacy leakage: one captures the
hidden correction process directly, while the other
reveals its behavioral effects from outputs alone.
A gray-box example is shown in Figure 1. The
gap between members and non-members is observ-
able but with magnitude vary across decoding po-
sitions. OP-Print makes use of such signal and
outperforms the baselines. Across diverse models
and distillation schemes, we found that all baseline
attacks, consistent with observations from prior
works (Meeus et al., 2025a; Das et al., 2025; Duan
et al., 2024), remain essentially at random guessing
even under gray-box access, whereas OP-Print is
broadly effective in both gray-box and black-box
settings. These results reveal an overlooked pri-
vacy risk of on-policy self-distillation and show
that its leakage surface differs from conventional
distillation. Our contributions are as follows:
• We uncover the location of the membership
signal in on-policy self-distillation: rather
than being globally visible, it resides in a
sparse and structured correction imprint con-
centrated on uncertainty-sensitive tokens.
• Building on this observation, we propose OP-
Print, a unified attack framework that targets
this hidden signal under both threat models:
in the gray-box setting, it exploits privileged
residual discrepancy directly, while in the
black-box setting, it captures its restart-based
output-basin traces, thereby enabling reliable
membership inference.
• Extensive experiments across diverse LLMs
consistently demonstrate the superiority of
OP-Print. For example, on Qwen3-8B (Yang
et al., 2025) under the black-box setting, OP-
Print improves AUC by 7.7% and TPR@5%
FPR by 26.5% over the strongest baseline.

Related Work
On-Policy Self-Distillation. On-policy distilla-
tion (OPD) trains the student on trajectories sam-
pled from its own evolving policy, reducing the
training-inference mismatch of off-policy distilla-
tion (Song and Zheng, 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a).
Existing approaches include logit matching (Agar-
wal et al., 2024a; Ko et al., 2024; Jung et al.,
2025; Wu et al., 2025b), outcome supervision (Ye
et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2026;
Singh et al., 2025; Mendes et al., 2026), and self-
distillation (Zhao et al., 2026a; Penaloza et al.,
2026; Ye et al., 2026b,a; Dat et al., 2026). In self-
distillation, the same model acts as both teacher
and student under asymmetric conditioning, elim-
inating the need for an external teacher. Early
methods such as SPIN (Chen et al., 2024) rely
on reference data, whereas recent approaches in-
ject privileged information into the teacher context.
A representative example is OPSD (Zhao et al.,
2026a), where the teacher receives additional su-
pervision while the student sees only the original in-
put, and training minimizes token-level divergence
on the student’s own rollouts. Recent extensions

Membership Inference Attack. Membership In-
ference Attacks (MIAs) (Shokri et al., 2017a; Car-
lini et al., 2022a; Li et al., 2026a; Yin et al., 2026b)
determine whether a sample was used to train a
target model, posing risks to sensitive training
data. Existing MIAs mainly fall into metric-based
and shadow model-based methods (Carlini et al.,
2022a; Li et al., 2024). Metric-based attacks in-
fer membership from statistics derived from model
outputs (Yeom et al., 2018; Salem et al., 2018;
Song and Mittal, 2021; Li et al., 2024), whereas
shadow model-based attacks train auxiliary models
to mimic the target (Shokri et al., 2017a; Zarifzadeh
et al., 2023). Since the latter is computationally ex-
pensive and often impractical for LLMs (Li et al.,
2024, 2026b), this work focuses exclusively on
metric-based methods.
MIAs were first studied for classification mod-
els (Shokri et al., 2017a) and later extended to gen-
erative (Chen et al., 2020; Hayes et al., 2017) and
embedding models (Mahloujifar et al., 2021; Song
and Raghunathan, 2020). Recent work has further
investigated MIAs against LLMs (Shi et al., 2023;
Li et al., 2024), covering both pretraining (Shi et al.,
2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Changpinyo et al., 2021)
and post-training data (Li et al., 2024; Wu and Cao,
2025; Hu et al., 2025). The on-policy distillation
process is commonly conducted during the post-
training process. This highlights the unique im-
portance of this problem: The capability of LLMs
to effectively interact with humans fundamentally
depends on the post-training stage, as the strength
of this capability is directly tied to the quality of
the post-training dataset. Furthermore, develop-
ers often construct their own task-specific datasets
for this stage (Song and Zheng, 2026; Zhao et al.,
2026a), which introduces additional privacy risks.
Motivated by these factors, we focus on the post-
training stage MIA of LLMs.

Method
Challenges.
Figure 2 clarifies both the diffi-
culty and the feasibility of MIA on on-policy self-
distillation. The key observation is that the su-
pervision imprint is not organized primarily by
token position, but by token difficulty. As shown in
Figure 2a, teacher-student divergence varies only
moderately across relative decoding positions, so
position alone provides at most a weak signal. In
contrast, Figure 2b shows a much sharper depen-
dence on student uncertainty: the divergence rises
substantially in higher-uncertainty regions, indicat-
ing that privileged correction is concentrated on
difficult reasoning tokens rather than uniformly dis-
tributed throughout the rollout. Figure 2c further
shows that this signal occupies only a restricted
region of the reliability map, confirming that the
resulting imprint is sparse and structured instead
of globally exposed. This structure directly ex-
plains the gap between gray-box and black-box
attacks. In the gray-box setting, adversaries can
access the teacher-conditioned residual correction
signal along the same rollout, and directly aggre-
gates this privileged discrepancy. In the black-box
setting, however, the most informative variable is
fundamentally hidden, and the adversary only ob-
serves final output tokens. Since position is only
weakly informative, this makes black-box MIA
substantially harder.

A Unified View: Hidden Correction Imprint.
The analysis in Figure 2 suggests that on-policy
self-distillation leaves a structured correction im-
print: membership-relevant signal is sparse, con-
centrated on uncertainty-sensitive reasoning tokens,
and only weakly aligned with absolute decoding
position. This motivates a unified view of both at-
tacks. We posit a latent quantity measuring how
much privileged correction the model would still
require on a rollout:
∆t(x, y) = KL(pθ(· | pt(q, r), y<t)
∥pθ(· | ps(q), y<t)) .
(1)
Intuitively, ∆t(x, y) is large when the student-
conditioned model still disagrees substantially with
its privileged counterpart at token t, and small when
the correction has already been internalized. Mem-
bers should therefore exhibit smaller privileged
correction on average, because self-distillation has
already adapted the model on those examples. Our
gray-box attack estimates this quantity directly,
while our black-box attack targets its observable
consequence in the output basin.

Datasets and Models. A long-standing and highly
debated issue in MIA against LLMs is data con-
struction (Meeus et al., 2025a; Das et al., 2025;
Duan et al., 2024). In prior work, the attack results
are often rendered unreliable by severe distribution
mismatch between the member and non-member
sets, leading to overclaimed performance (Meeus
et al., 2025a). On the other side, prior work has
found that training a model too much on-policy self-
distillation can suffer from mode collapse (Song
and Zheng, 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a). This also
motivates controlling the data scale in evaluation.
To avoid the above problems and to ensure a correct
and fair evaluation of attack performance, we care-
fully select and construct the dataset and train the
target models under a protocol that strictly follows
the requirements of membership inference evalu-
ation. For the dataset, e use the mathematical rea-
soning subset of OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025),
sampling 1k problem-solution pairs with chain-of-
thought reasoning, splitting them evenly into 500
members and 500 non-members. For the target
models, we fine-tune three Qwen3 variants (Yang
et al., 2025) (1.7B, 4B, 8B) on the sampled member
set under the distillation variants described above.
Training details are in Appenfix A.
Baselines. We consider both the general gray-box
baselines and on-policy self-distillation specified
black-box baselines. For gray-box baselines, we in-
clude Min-K% (Zhang et al., 2024) and MaxRényi-
K% (Li et al., 2024). We report the best results
among different variants of these methods. For
black-box baselines, we construct rollout-derived
metrics including solution length (S-Len), rollout
correct-rate (RC-Rate), rollout majority-answer (R-
Maj), average rollout length (Avg. R-Len), rollout-
length standard deviation (R-Std), and formatted-
answer rate (F-Rate). These metrics capture output
length, correctness, answer consistency, length sta-
bility, and formatting reliability, respectively.
Evaluation metric.
As a binary classification
problem, the performance can be evaluated with
the AUC score (Carlini et al., 2021). We define
the members as "positive" and the non-members
as "negative". We also report True Positive Rate
(TPR) at low False Positive Rate (FPR) (Carlini
et al., 2022a), which is an important metric in MIAs
and measures detection rate at a meaningful thresh-
old. We set the threshold as 5% and evaluate all the
methods under TPR@5% FPR. We also report the
best classification accuracy achievable by sweep-
ing over all possible thresholds on the attack scores.
