Bilingual Language Models Capture Cross-Linguistic Influence in
Second-Language Reading
Abderrahmane Issam1, Irene E. Winther2, Stefan L. Frank2
1Maastricht University, 2Radboud University
abderrahmane.issam@maastrichtuniversity.nl
{irene.winther, stefan.frank}@ru.nl
Abstract
While monolingual language model (LM) sur-
prisal is widely established as a predictor of
reading times, it cannot capture the non-native
(L2) reading dynamics that are shaped by the
reader’s first language (L1). In this work, we
investigate whether the cross-lingual transfer
within bilingual models aligns with human L1-
driven biases during L2 reading. We evaluate
bilingual models on eye-tracking data of read-
ers from 12 diverse L1 backgrounds reading
English texts. To isolate L1-specific transfer,
we compare bilingual models trained on an L1
that matches the readers’ L1 background to
both a monolingual English baseline and bilin-
gual models trained on other L1s. We demon-
strate that matching the readers’ L1 improves
the prediction of L2 reading times. This pre-
dictive advantage is concentrated in late-stage
eye-tracking measures, modulated by L1-L2
script overlap, and peaks among intermediate-
proficiency readers. These findings provide
evidence that bilingual LMs capture aspects of
cross-linguistic influence, and that such influ-
ence is present in L2 reading behavior.

Introduction
Language Models (LMs) are increasingly being in-
vestigated for their alignment with human cognitive
processing (Armeni et al., 2017; Karamolegkou
et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2026; Futrell and Mahowald,
2026). Within this domain, a prominent line of
work evaluates LMs on eye-tracking data (Dem-
berg and Keller, 2008; Frank, 2009; Goodkind and
Bicknell, 2018; Oh and Schuler, 2023; Shain et al.,
2024; Wilcox et al., 2023), resting on the well-
established assumption that fixation durations re-
flect cognitive effort during sentence processing
(Rayner, 1998; Schotter and Dillon, 2025). Un-
der surprisal theory (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008), the
cognitive effort of processing a word is tied its
predictability in context (surprisal). While sub-
stantial work shows that LM surprisal successfully
predicts native-language (L1) reading times (Smith
and Levy, 2013; Goodkind and Bicknell, 2018;
Wilcox et al., 2020; Shain et al., 2024), much less
is known regarding the ability of bilingual LMs to
predict reading behavior in a non-native language
(L2).
One candidate source of difference between
native and non-native reading is cross-linguistic
influence from the reader’s L1 (Kim, 2025;
Alemán Bañón and Martin, 2021; Odlin, 1989;
Tokowicz and MacWhinney, 2005; Tokowicz et al.,
2024; Foucart, 2021). If such influence actively
shapes L2 reading, a monolingual LM trained
solely on the target language cannot, by definition,
capture it.
In parallel, research in NLP demonstrates that
multilingual models learn shared cross-lingual rep-
resentations (Pires et al., 2019; Libovický et al.,
2020; de Varda and Marelli, 2023), enabling zero-
shot performance transfer across languages (Wu
and Dredze, 2019; Conneau et al., 2020b,a). How-
ever, it remains an open question whether these
representations translate into predictive biases that
align with human L2 reading behavior.
In this work, we investigate whether bilingual
LMs’ cross-lingual transfer aligns with human
cross-linguistic influence during L2 reading. We
evaluate bilingual GPT-2 models on the Multilin-
gual Eye Movement Corpus (MECO L2) (Kuper-
man et al., 2025), using eye-tracking recordings
from participants across 12 diverse L1 backgrounds
reading English texts. To examine whether the du-
ration of L1 training affects L2 prediction in the
models, we train bilingual models across multiple
Step of Exposure (SoE) stages, varying the mo-
ment during L1 learning at which L2 English is
introduced. Hence, we manipulate the duration of
initial L1 training and the amount of L2 exposure.
We evaluate model surprisal across four reading-
time metrics (first fixation duration, gaze duration,
go-past time, and total fixation duration) to distin-
arXiv:2610.31620v1  [cs.CL]  22 Oct 2026


guish early lexical access from late-stage structural
reanalysis and text integration (Rayner, 1998; Liv-
ersedge and Findlay, 2000; Inhoff, 1984).
Our investigation addresses two primary ques-
tions. First, we establish whether bilingual mod-
els’ surprisal explains variance in L2 reading
times that a monolingual baseline cannot. Second,
we compare L1-matched bilingual models (where
the model’s initial training language matches the
reader’s L1) to L1-mismatched controls (where
the model is trained on a different L1) to deter-
mine whether this predictive advantage is driven
by language-specific transfer rather than a generic
multilingual effect.
We find a bilingual predictive advantage concen-
trated in late-stage reading measures, with little
to no advantage in early ones. Furthermore, L1-
matched models outperform mismatched models,
indicating that the explained variance in L2 reading
times is L1-specific. Finally, we observe that this
advantage is modulated by L1-L2 script overlap
and peaks for L2 readers with intermediate pro-
ficiency. In summary, these findings provide evi-
dence that bilingual LMs are capable of modeling
cross-linguistic influence, and that such influence
is present in human L2 reading behavior.

Related Works
2.1
Bilingual Models for L2 Acquisition
The success of multilingual language models has
prompted research into whether their internal cross-
lingual transfer mechanisms align with human
cross-linguistic influence (Mueller et al., 2020; Pa-
padimitriou and Jurafsky, 2020; Oba et al., 2023;
Aoyama and Schneider, 2024; Yadavalli et al.,
2023; Issam et al., 2026; Salhan et al., 2026). To
simulate the dynamics of L2 acquisition, previ-
ous studies have modeled bilingual exposure by
training on unbalanced L1 and L2 data (Dhar and
Bisazza, 2021; Winther et al., 2021), introducing
the L2 sequentially (Winther et al., 2021; Constan-
tinescu et al., 2025; Arnett et al., 2025; Issam et al.,
2026), freezing L1 representations during L2 expo-
sure (Papadimitriou and Jurafsky, 2020; Yadavalli
et al., 2023; Aoyama and Schneider, 2024), or
using specialized training objectives (Oba et al.,
2023). Previous research on bilingual LMs has
investigated various effects of bilingual exposure,
including syntactic transfer (Oba et al., 2023; Ya-
davalli et al., 2023; Issam et al., 2026), cross-
linguistic priming (Frank, 2021; Michaelov et al.,
2023; Arnett et al., 2025), and L1 influence on L2
reading times (Aoyama and Schneider, 2024; Sal-
han et al., 2026). Building on this, we adopt the
sequential training strategy of Issam et al. (2026) to
train bilingual models for several L1s, and evaluate
their ability to predict human L2 reading times.
2.2
Evaluating LMs on L2 Reading Times
While most work evaluating language models on
eye-tracking data focuses on L1 reading (Dem-
berg and Keller, 2008; Frank, 2009; Goodkind
and Bicknell, 2018; Oh and Schuler, 2023; Shain
et al., 2024; Wilcox et al., 2023), the release of
bilingual speaker corpora such as CELER (Berzak
et al., 2022) and MECO L2 (Kuperman et al., 2025)
has enabled the study of non-native processing
(de Varda and Marelli, 2022; Berzak and Levy,
2023; Aoyama and Schneider, 2024; Salhan et al.,
2026). Most of this work, however, still relies on
surprisal from monolingual models for the predic-
tion of L2 reading times.
A smaller body of work has explored bilingual
models for this purpose, but critical gaps remain.
Salhan et al. (2026) lacked monolingual baselines
and L1-mismatched controls, making it impossible
to isolate L1-specific transfer. While Aoyama and
Schneider (2024) included a monolingual baseline,
they relied on frozen L1 representations and com-
pared independent model fits rather than testing for
additional variance explained. Furthermore, both
studies omitted L1 control groups and restricted
their evaluations primarily to early reading metrics
like gaze duration. Our approach systematically
addresses these limitations. First, we train fully un-
frozen models, allowing bilingual representations
to dynamically interact. Second, we measure each
bilingual model’s variance explained over a mono-
lingual baseline and test whether L1-matched sur-
prisal explains variance beyond L1-mismatched
surprisal within the same regression analysis. Em-
pirically, we broaden the scope by evaluating L1
and L2 readers across four eye-tracking measures
and 12 L1s, further examining how this predictive
advantage is modulated by reader proficiency and
script overlap.
3
Methods
3.1
Bilingual LM Training
We follow the training approach of Issam et al.
(2026) to train bilingual GPT-2 LMs for each L1-
L2 language pair. We acquire multilingual data


from FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024), using the 10-
billion token sample (sample-10BT) for English
and the language-specific splits from FineWeb-v2
(Penedo et al., 2025) for the L1s. We follow the
approach of Issam et al. (2026) to minimize code-
switching by filtering out sentences using sentence-
and word-level language detection. While com-
pletely mitigating code-switching remains an open
challenge in NLP (Burchell et al., 2024), this fil-
tering step is crucial. Because code-switching is
known to enhance cross-lingual transfer (Qin et al.,
2020; Wang et al., 2025), leaving it unfiltered could
inflate the observed L1 influence and contaminate
the monolingual baseline.
We vary the Step of Exposure (SoE) as the step
at which we introduce the L2 to study how ex-
tended L1 training and delayed L2 exposure affect
the prediction of L2 reading data. Increasing the
SoE results in longer initial L1 training and less
L2 training. Since we train all models for the same
total amount of training steps, this mimics a com-
parison between bilingual speakers of the same age
but with different ages of first exposure to the L2.
Once the L2 is introduced, we continue training on
the L1 data by interleaving L1 and L2 sequences.
This prevents catastrophic forgetting and maintains
the L1 influence on the L2 (Constantinescu et al.,
2025; Arnett et al., 2025). Furthermore, it mirrors a
realistic scenario where non-native speakers main-
tain strong knowledge of their L1, either due to
critical period effects (Lenneberg, 1967; Bylund,
2019) or persistent L1 use during L2 learning.
We train a SentencePiece tokenizer (Kudo and
Richardson, 2018) for each L1-L2 pair using a bal-
anced subset of 10 million lines (5 million lines per
language). Once trained, this tokenizer is used to
process the full corpus of each language indepen-
dently, packing the text into shuffled sequences of
256 tokens. Finally, from these fully tokenized cor-
pora, we sample one billion tokens per language for
LM training and one million tokens for evaluation.
We train all models for a total of 64k train-
ing steps. During monolingual training, batches
consists of monolingual sequences, while bilin-
gual training consists of interleaved L1 and L2
sequences in each batch. We train 4 SoE models
for each L1-L2 pair by initiating bilingual training
at training step 0, 16k, 32k and 48k. We use a fixed
learning rate with 5K warmup similarly to Issam
et al. (2026) to avoid confounding our variables,
because a linear learning rate would lead to later
SoE models being trained on L2 with a lower learn-
ing rate. Finally, besides the bilingual models, we
train a monolingual baseline using an identical tok-
enizer to each bilingual pair exclusively on English
data. In total, 55 GPT-2 models were trained in this
study (1 monolingual and 4 bilingual SoE models
for each of the 11 L1-L2 pairs). Detailed training
hyperparameters are provided in Appendix B.
3.2
Evaluation Framework
We use MECO L2 (Kuperman et al., 2025), which
provides reading data of English as a second lan-
guage. The dataset includes eye-tracking data of
participants from 13 L1 language backgrounds col-
lected across 16 institutions. Rather than aggregat-
ing participants by L1, we keep each institution as
a distinct subset. We include all L1 groups except
the Basque subset, which we exclude because of
insufficient data representation in FineWeb-v2. We
also include the native English (En-UK) subset to
serve as a native control group. Participants across
all subsets read the same 12 English texts sourced
from standardized reading assessments. See Ap-
pendix A for further details on the subsets.
We evaluate model predictions against four eye-
tracking measures:
1. First Fixation Duration: The duration of the
first fixation on a word.
2. Gaze Duration: The sum of all fixation dura-
tions landing on a word before the eye leaves
it for the first time.
3. Go-Past Time: The total time elapsed from
the initial fixation on a word until the reader’s
gaze moves to the right of that word. This
includes all time spent regressing to earlier
parts of the text.
4. Total Fixation Duration: The sum of all fix-
ation durations on a word, including subse-
quent fixations if the eye returns to it.
These measures are thought to reflect progressively
later stages of cognitive processing (Rayner, 1998;
Liversedge and Findlay, 2000). Early measures,
such as first fixation duration and gaze duration,
are assumed to capture initial lexical access, includ-
ing word form recognition and meaning retrieval.
Late measures, such as go-past time and total fix-
ation duration, are thought to reflect post-lexical
processing, such as structural reanalysis and syntac-
tic or semantic integration. Separating these met-
rics allows us to distinguish context-invariant word


recognition from higher-level, context-dependent
processing (Inhoff, 1984; Berzak and Levy, 2023).
We derive surprisal estimates from every LM
trained for this study: 1 monolingual and 4 SoE
bilingual LMs for each of the 11 L1-L2 language
pairs (excluding native English and Basque sub-
sets). We calculate the surprisal of each word as
its negative log-probability. For words segmented
into multiple subwords by the tokenizer, we sum
the surprisals of the constituent tokens.
To evaluate the predictions, we fit linear mixed-
effects models (LMMs) with crossed random in-
tercepts for participants and words. The baseline
fixed effects include log-frequency, word length,
and word position in the sentence. We also include
the log-frequency and length of the preceding word
(wi−1) to account for spillover effects. Word fre-
quencies are extracted using the wordfreq library
(Speer, 2022).
3.2.1
Model Comparisons
We test whether bilingual models capture cross-
linguistic cognitive processing through two main
comparisons.
Bilingual vs. Monolingual
First, we compare
each bilingual model against the monolingual En-
glish baseline. Our hypothesis is that if bilingual
models capture cross-linguistic influence, their sur-
prisal estimates will yield better predictions of non-
native reading times than a strictly monolingual
model’s. To account for inherent differences in
baseline predictability across the different language
subsets, we use a pseudo-R2 metric:

Matched vs.
Mismatched Bilingual Models
Second, we compare matched bilingual LMs to mis-
matched bilingual LMs. A bilingual LM is consid-
ered matched if its L1 matches the native language
of the evaluated MECO subset; all other bilingual
LMs are considered mismatched. If bilingual LMs
develop L1-specific transfer effects similar to those
of human readers, matched LMs should predict
reading times more accurately than mismatched
LMs. To quantify this difference, we calculate
the difference in log-likelihood (∆LL) between
two non-nested LMMs: one using surprisal from a
matched bilingual LM and the other using surprisal
from a mismatched LM.
To compute formal p-values, we also conduct
nested regression model comparisons. We fit an
encompassing model (mboth) containing both the
matched and mismatched surprisal predictors. We
then compare this encompassing model against re-
stricted models containing either the matched or
the mismatched predictor alone. All three mod-
els use identical baseline covariates and random
effects structures, differing solely in their surprisal
terms. The unique explanatory variance added by
matched surprisal is calculated as LL(mboth) −
LL(mmismatch). Because mmismatch is nested within
mboth, we evaluate statistical significance of this
added variance using a likelihood ratio test.

regression inspired by Berzak and Levy (2023).

prisal sensitivity across eye-tracking measures by
fitting individual participant regressions that con-
trol for baseline features. For each reader, we com-

monolingual). In the second stage, we

Results and Analysis
4.1
Are L2 Models Better at Predicting L2
Data than Monolingual Models?
Figure 1 shows the difference in explained vari-
ance (∆R2
pseudo) between the matched bilingual
models and the monolingual baselines across four
SoE stages. The results show a difference between
early and late reading measures.
For first fixation duration, the bilingual surprisal
provides no predictive advantage for L2 readers.
The mean ∆R2
pseudo for L2 readers starts near zero
and drops slightly below zero at the SoE=48k. In
contrast, for the later reading measures, the bilin-
gual model yields a clear advantage. In both gaze
duration and go-past time, the L2 mean is strictly
positive and increases consistently with SoE. In
total fixation time, the L2 mean remains positive
and stable across all SoEs.
Crucially, across gaze duration, go-past time,
and total fixation duration, the native English con-
trol group stays close to zero and consistently be-
low the L2 mean. This gap confirms that the bilin-
gual models capture variance specific to non-native
reading behavior, rather than offering a generic
predictive improvement for all readers.
4.2
Are Matched Models Better Than
Mismatched Models?
Figure 2 illustrates the non-nested per-word ∆LL
between LMMs using matched and mismatched
bilingual surprisals. To control for evaluation bi-
ases or tokenization artifacts, we include a monolin-
gual baseline derived by treating each L1 monolin-
gual LM as matched and comparing it against the
other monolingual LMs. If the matched advantage
is genuinely driven by bilingual cross-linguistic
transfer, it should exceed this strictly monolingual
baseline. Except for first fixation duration, the
matched bilingual models consistently outperform
their mismatched counterparts, and this advantage
strengthens in later processing measures. Notably,
go-past time is the only metric exhibiting a clear up-
ward trend in matched model advantage as SoE in-
creases. Although there is variance among individ-
ual subset traces (representing the mean per-word
∆LL between matched and mismatched models
for each L1 subset), the matched bilingual models
generally exceed the monolingual control.
The nested model results in Table 1 show
whether this matched advantage provides unique
explanatory variance. By evaluating both predic-
tors simultaneously, we can assess which model
contributes a greater share of unique information
based on their mean ∆LL and significance ratios.
These nested comparisons perfectly align with our
non-nested findings. During early lexical access
(first fixation duration), matched models offer no
L1-specific predictive advantage, contributing less
unique variance than mismatched models. In con-
trast, across all later reading measures (gaze du-
ration, go-past time, and total fixation duration),
the matched models contribute substantially more
unique log-likelihood and achieve statistical signif-
icance at much higher rates than their mismatched
counterparts.

4.3
The Role of Script Overlap
A primary structural dimension separating the eval-
uated subsets is writing system overlap with En-
glish. We examine this effect by partitioning the
results from Figure 2 by script. The Latin script
group consists of 8 MECO subsets across 7 L1s,
while the Non-Latin group consists of 6 subsets
across 4 L1s (with the exception of go-past time,
where the ch_s subset is missing). See Appendix
A for details on the L1 subsets and their scripts.
This analysis is motivated by prior NLP research
showing that shared scripts facilitate cross-lingual
transfer in LMs (Dhamecha et al., 2021; Moosa
et al., 2023), as well as psycholinguistic studies ex-
amining the processing dynamics of reading across
different writing systems (Miwa et al., 2013; Taylor
and Mukai, 2023; Arab, 2026).
Figure 3 illustrates the resulting per-word ∆LL.
In first fixation duration, neither script group dis-
plays a bilingual advantage. Latin bilingual models
hover near zero close to the monolingual baseline,
while Non-Latin bilingual models decline sharply
below zero as SoE increases, dropping well be-
low their within-group baseline. In gaze duration,
the groups diverge. Latin bilingual models remain
consistently positive and comfortably above the
monolingual baseline, whereas Non-Latin models
hover near zero and below the baseline across all
SoEs. For later measures (go-past time and total fix-
ation duration), however, bilingual models in both
groups perform comparably to or better than the
monolingual baseline. Non-Latin subsets also dis-
play a pronounced, steady upward trend in go-past
time as SoE increases.
4.4
Effect of Reader Proficiency
Previous work demonstrates that L2 proficiency
non-monotonically modulates the predictive power
of monolingual surprisal (Berzak and Levy, 2023).
Inspired by this finding, we ask a novel question:
how does the added predictive power of bilingual
surprisal change with reader proficiency? We hy-
pothesize an inverted-U relationship for this bilin-
gual predictive advantage over the monolingual
baseline. This advantage should be minimal at
both extremes of the proficiency spectrum: begin-
ners rely primarily on shallow, context-independent
features such as word frequency (Diependaele
et al., 2013; Berzak and Levy, 2023), while ad-
vanced readers no longer depend on L1 transfer
as their reading behavior approaches that of a na-
tive speaker (Chen et al., 2026). Consequently, the
bilingual advantage should peak for intermediate
readers, who possess enough fluency to engage in
contextual prediction but still rely heavily on L1

Our hypothesis of an inverted-U trajectory is

To contextualize this result, Appendix D.1 vi-
sualizes the absolute explanatory power (R2) of
each model independently. As expected, both mod-
els exhibit an inverted-U trend across all metrics,
corroborating Berzak and Levy (2023) by show-

intermediate learners. Crucially, however, Figure 4

Discussion and Conclusion
In this work, we investigated whether bilingual
LMs align with human cross-linguistic influence
during L2 reading. Our findings demonstrate that
bilingual LMs successfully capture aspects of L1-
specific effects on L2 processing. Crucially, our
two distinct evaluation frameworks—comparing
bilingual LMs against a monolingual baseline
(§4.1), and contrasting L1-matched against L1-
mismatched bilingual LMs (§4.2)—yield consis-
tent results.
Across both comparisons, we observed a pro-
gressive shift in predictive performance from early
to late reading measures. The predictive advan-
tage of bilingual surprisals is absent in first fixation
duration, begins to emerge in gaze duration, and
becomes most pronounced in late-stage measures
