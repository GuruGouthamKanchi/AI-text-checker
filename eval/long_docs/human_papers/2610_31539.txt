Abstract
Machine learning has permeated material optimization, providing high value. Nevertheless, its re-
liance on a high number of samples limits its applicability to relatively explored material families. In
this paper we extend the applicability of machine learning to novel and understudied materials through
the PYTHIA (Probabilistic Yet Transferable Hierarchical Interaction Architecture) framework. Pythia
utilizes two of the most powerful machine learning concepts, foundational models and transfer learning.
We employ a complex foundational model trained on a large dataset of chemically similar molecules,
on a target that is physically close to our prediction target. Then, we freeze the weights and perform
subspace alignment with the use of a transformer layer and add uncertainty quantification with gaussian
processes. These two layers can be trained on a limited dataset yet allow us to leverage the gained knowl-
edge and the studied materials and complex architectures. The resulting model is used as a surrogate
for a Bayesian Optimization scheme. To showcase the robustness of the framework, we applied it to the
investigation of adsorption and defect optimization of two distinct systems. Not only were we able to
correctly and efficiently identify the optimal adsorption sites of even complex adsorbates, the algorithm
showed remarkable accuracy even for systems not present in its training set.

Introduction
Adsorption plays an important role in the interaction of nanostructured materials with molecules and ions.
These interactions depend on the types of atoms present and their local arrangement. Identifying stable
adsorption structures also helps explain electrochemical reactions at metal surfaces [1]. For materials with
different compositions or defects, this search becomes more difficult. Changes in the local atomic environment
can affect both the preferred adsorption position and the strength of binding. To investigate this, Density
functional theory (DFT) is widely used. Mapping the potential energy surface (PES) requires calculations
for many molecular positions and orientations [2], a search that can be automated by various tools which
help generate, screen, and relax these structures [3, 4]. Other methods examine different parts of the energy
landscape.
Minima hopping and evolutionary algorithms search for structures with low energies [5, 6].
The nudged elastic band (NEB) method finds minimum energy paths between two energy states [7], while
metadynamics explores free energy landscapes [8, 9]. These methods provide useful information, but their
computational cost can remain high when each energy evaluation requires a DFT calculation. The cost
increases further when several substrate compositions and defect structures are studied.
Machine learning can reduce the number of expensive calculations. It has been applied to many problems
in materials research [10, 11, 12]. In atomistic simulations, machine learning models can describe surface

reaction PESs [13] and predict adsorption energies on nanoclusters [14]. Graph neural networks have also
been used to predict molecular adsorption energies on metals [15], and learned interatomic potentials can
speed up adsorption searches [16]. However, these models need suitable training data. For a new adsorption
system, generating enough DFT data can account for much of the total computational cost, often times
providing a full system investigation before the model is even trained.
Active learning (AL) and Bayesian optimization (BO) help address this problem by using the available
data to choose new calculations. Bayesian models can learn atomic interactions during simulations [17, 18],
and uncertainty estimation is also an active area of research for molecular models [19]. Related approaches
have been used for crystal structure prediction [20], the optimization of nanoporous materials [21], materials
discovery with graph models [22], and catalyst screening through adsorption calculations [23].
Machine
learning can also accelerate searches for saddle points and reaction paths [24, 25], as well as stable adsorbate
geometries [26].
The choice of structural representation affects how well these methods work.
BO has been used to
search for molecular conformers by varying torsion angles [27]. The Bayesian Optimization Structure Search
(BOSS) framework uses structural coordinates to explore energy landscapes [28]. Its applications include
molecular adsorption on surfaces [29] and the search for molecular conformations on metal clusters [30].
These coordinates are useful when the main degrees of freedom can be identified in advance. However, a
representation designed for one system may need to be changed when the molecular composition, substrate,
or defects change. Learning a representation directly from atomic structures offers a way to handle these
differences.
In our previous work, we developed AUGUR to identify adsorption sites by combining a graph neural
network, Gaussian process (GP) regression, and BO [31]. AUGUR learns a structural representation through
a graph neural network (GNN) and uses it to predict energies and estimate uncertainty via a GP, without
requiring manually designed molecular descriptors. A very relevant question however is, how this approach
can benefit from knowledge learned on other atomic systems. Such knowledge could reduce the amount
of data needed for a new adsorption problem. This is particularly useful when the search includes several
compositions or defect types, each with only a limited number of reference calculations.
Pretrained atomic models provide a starting point for this transfer. The MACE architecture [32] has been
used to develop foundation models trained on many different structures [33]. The atomic features learned
by these models can then be used in new applications. However, pretraining does not guarantee accurate
adsorption energies for every system, especially when its chemical environments or reference calculations
differ from those used in training. Previous work has shown that adapting a pretrained potential while
keeping some parameters fixed can reduce the amount of training data required [34] and even extrapolate
to new properties [35]. For adsorption searches, we also need a model that adapts to the available data and
estimates uncertainty to guide the choice of new calculations.
To address the above concerns we introduce PYTHIA, which combines a pretrained MACE model, a
Transformer, GP regression, and BO. The pretrained MACE provides atomic features that contain relevant
descriptors for an adjacent system and property. A Transformer [36] processes these features and produces
a representation that has translated the features to the current system. This vector is then used as input for
the GP that predicts the adsorption energy and its corresponding uncertainty. During training, the MACE
parameters remain fixed, while the Transformer and GP are trained on data from the systems being studied.
This allows the model to reuse features learned during pretraining and adapt them to the target energy
prediction problem. BO then uses these predictions to select candidate defects and adsorption positions for
further electronic structure calculations. This pipeline provides all the benefits of large and complex models,
without the usual necessity of large datasets.
We verify PYTHIA on two highly relevant cases, optimizing both the defects of the cluster and the position
of the adsorbate simultaneously. The first is the Zn adsorption on a defective Chini cluster. This system is of
high interest due to its role in the chemistry of metal carbonyl clusters [37] and their encapsulation properties.
The second examines adsorption of formaldehyde on a buckled silicon layer [38, 39]. Formaldehyde is a
harmful chemical and silicene is a periodic and complex structure. Therefore the optimization of this system
provides both a high environmental impact as well as a heightened complexity.
These systems provide
different local chemical environments for deep testing of the model. We compare PYTHIA with two baseline
models, AUGUR and a fully trained MACE with a GP as a final layer and was found to outperform both
by a significant margin showing that it has higher complexity than AUGUR and lower data demand then
2


the full MACE. We also examine the predictions for defect groups not present in the training data and
found our model highly accurate. To summarise, we show that PYTHIA combines the high accuracy of
complex models at a fraction of the data cost. Additional to the predictions, PYTHIA also provides highly
tailored optimization, identifying sites with many times better adsorption energy than present in the training
set. With novel materials emerging fast, this data-efficient transferable framework can significantly expedite
chemical investigation alleviating the need for time consuming data acquisition.

2.2
Bayesian Optimization
Bayesian Optimization (BO) is an optimization algorithm that has proven itself to be both data efficient
and accurate even in non-convex/concave optimization problems.
It is especially useful in optimization
problems where there is a costly underlying evaluation function, such as time consuming experiments or
simulations. Data-driven models substitute these procedures with a comparatively inexpensive surrogate,
that, however, requires data to be trained. Oftentimes, this data demand is high enough as to invalidate the
eventual benefits. Therefore an efficient and data-sparse dataset is necessary to leverage the full advantage
of machine learning. Bayesian optimization gives answers to this bottleneck, simultaneously optimizing both
the underlying problem as well as the sampling one. This is done by combining two components, a stochastic
predictor, typically a GP regressor and a function that weighs each hopeful optimization point based on its
optimality and amount of new information it brings into the model. This function is known as the acquisition
function and can take many forms depending on the individual case needs. For the remainder of the chapter,
we will describe a minimization process, but everything can be applied without loss of generality to any
optimization problem. In the present work, we seek to find the position of adsorption and set defects that will
result in the lowest possible energy. To this end, we can generate a number of random positions and evaluate
their respective energies using the uncertainty-aware predictor. The prediction’s uncertainty is reflected in
its standard deviation. Initially, we choose to evaluate points predicted to have high standard deviation,
regardless of their predicted energy. In this way, we are giving the model information about compositions
and sites far removed from the ones already in the training set. Once this process has sufficiently trained the
model, the focus can shift on determining the systems that exhibit good adsorption energies, thus fulfilling
our original optimization goal. These stages are called exploration and exploitation respectively. Note that
there are many ways to optimize the acquisition functions. The high number of physical constraints however
made most of them not trivially applicable.
To this end, we have chosen to generate 10.000 individual
systems and predict them. Though this is slower than traditional gradient based approaches, it allows for
the strict control of the evaluated system’s physical feasibility. Further, PYTHIA is efficient enough for the
evaluation to still be performed in negligible time frames. More information about how the points were
generated can be found in Section 2.3.
2.3
Point generation
The convergence of Bayesian Optimization can be significantly sped up if we introduce a layer of physical
understanding of the problem. The simplest way is to generate a number of points (in our case, 10.000) that
adhere to a set of constraints. A random number of defects and a corresponding number of random atoms of
the cluster is chosen. These atoms are permuted, each by a unique, randomly selected element from the valid
pre-defined defect elements. This has created our defective adsorbant. Subsequently, one random atom of
the adsorbant and one random atom of the adsorbate is chosen. The chosen adsorbate atom is placed on the
surface of a sphere with a radius determined by the physical limits of the interaction distance between the
two atom types and centered on the chosen adsorbant atom. A random rotation matrix is defined and the
adsorbate is rotated thus. The resulting adsorbate is tested to ensure that none of its atoms pass through
or lie in the adsorbant. This is done by performing a Delaunay tessellation on the cluster coordinates and
determining the location of the adsorbent with respect to the resultant convex hull coordinate comparison.
If this constraint is violated, the system is discarded and the process restarts. If the system passes this
check, the distance between the adsorbate and remaining adsorbant atoms is calculated. If through the
rotations any atom is placed closer than physically possibly the system is discarded. Otherwise, the new
system is added to the sample set and evaluated through the pipeline. The sample generation process can
be summarized as follows:
1. Choose a random number of cluster atoms and exchange them for a set of random elements that are
capable of producing valid, defective structures.
2. Choose a random atom of the cluster, provided adsorption is physically possible on it.
3. Place a random adsorbent on the surface of a sphere with a radius determined by physical knowledge
of the maximum and minimum interaction distance of these two atoms. The functionality to determine
a specific adsorbent atom for placing on the sphere is also provided.
4


4. Rotate the adsorbent around its center of mass by a random set of angles.
5. Test if the adsorbent either lies in or passes through the cluster.
6. If the above constraint is not violated, test if the adsorbent is closer than a distance of tolerance to
any cluster atoms.
7. If the above constraint is not violated, the sample is admitted into the list of viable samples.
2.4
Gaussian Processes
In the current project, the uncertainty quantifying model chosen was Gaussian processes as it combines
robustness to overfitting and the well-documented accuracy of global kernel methods. It is a data-driven
stochastic algorithm that models predictions as the posterior of the Bayes formula.
The prior and the
likelihood are modeled as Gaussian distributions whose parameters are optimized based on the already
measured samples. In this way, the algorithm elegantly provides an inherent uncertainty quantification,
which in data sets of small size can be crucial. The fundamental principle of the method is that the true
underlying physical process y can be modeled by a model f, tuned on a set of points X using a multivariate
Gaussian distribution. This is the prior distribution, and without loss of generality, we can assume it is
centered around zero. This assumption can be made more robust by standardizing the data so that their
targets also fall within a zero mean Gaussian distribution. The covariance matrix can be defined by the
use of a kernel function. A kernel is a distance-like user-chosen function that encodes the correlation of two
outputs given the distance of their respective inputs. The prior would then take the mathematical form

of a million samples with very limited approximations.

2.5
Foundational Model
Gaussian processes are a versatile family of algorithms. Nevertheless, they suffer significantly with feature
spaces of large dimensionality. To this end, we have elected to process the structure using a Graph Neural
Network (GNN). The complexity and sheer size of the chemical space needed to be investigated however
makes the use of a simple GNN impractical. Therefore, we used an established and powerful foundational
model, MACE [32]. This is done without loss of generality and showcases the power of such architectures.
Other architectures, such as NequIP [44] or potentially newer models can be seamlessly integrated.
2.6
Translation Layer
MACE is a powerful model, but its accuracy comes at a cost of high complexity and large parameter space.
The typical size of datasets for adsorption application, however, does not suffice for the training of such
a large model. Therefore, we are forced to utilize pre-trained models on adjacent properties and chemical
species. Novel systems, however, differ significantly from the existing databases and models derived from
these databases are not of-the-box applicable. For the model to be transferable, there is need for a translation
layer that adapts the information extracted to the current case.
The final output of the model however is too specific to use as all the general chemical information have
been condensed to a target specific representation. To this end, we extract a deeper MACE layer, containing
a rich molecular representation, to be used as input to the translation layer.
The translation layer we have chosen is in the form of transformers. Transformers are a class of deep
learning architectures designed to model complex dependencies in sequential or structured data using a
mechanism known as self-attention. Introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017) [36], the transformer architecture
replaced traditional recurrent and convolutional models in many applications by enabling parallel processing
of input data and capturing long-range relationships more effectively.
At the core of the transformer is the multi-head self-attention mechanism, which computes contextualized
representations of each input token by attending to all other tokens in the sequence. Each attention head
learns to focus on different types of relationships by projecting the input into query, key, and value vectors.
The attention weights are calculated as scaled dot-products between queries and keys, and these weights are
used to combine the value vectors, allowing the model to integrate information from relevant positions in
the sequence.
Therefore, their ease of training on sparse data and their natural translation interpretation makes them
suitable for transferring the knowledge of the foundational model on our very specific task and dataset.
We do not need to retrain the MACE, a feat both computationally expensive and data inefficient. Instead
we leverage its knowledge and only train the translation layer. The pipeline so far however lacks the element
of uncertainty quantification. To add this, we use the translated vector as input to a Gaussian Processes
model which provides the final prediction and the uncertainty quantification of this prediction. This allows
us to leverage said predictions for Bayesian Optimization.
3
Results
To evaluate PYTHIA we deployed it on two distinct cases, that of the Zn adsorption on the chini cluster family
and the adsorption of formaldehyde (CH2O) on the silicene. In both cases we optimize simultaneously for
optimal adsorption position on adsorbent as well as for number and type of defects in the chemical systems.
We compare this approach with both the AUGUR framework as well as deploying the foundational model
to be fully trained solely on the acquired dataset.
3.1
Zn adsorption on Chini Clusters
Chini clusters are platinum (Pt) carbonyl clusters with a distinct chemical formula [Pt3(CO)6]2−
n , built
from n stacked layers of triangular Pt units. These are widely used as precursors for synthesising atom-
precise Pt nanoclusters (Pt-APNCs) which are obtained by encapsulating them inside a Zeolitic-imidazolate
framework (ZIF-8) through a bottle-around ship approach [37]. ZIF-8 assembles from Zn2+ nodes and 2-
methylimidazolate linkers, so the chini cluster interacts with the Zn ions while the framework forms around
6


it. We therefore used Zn adsorption energy as an optimization target, where more negative values denote
stronger binding. The detailed methodology for computing energies is given in SI Section 2.1.
To generate APNCs with higher density of catalytically active sites, experimentalists have tried to sub-
stitute Pt with various metal atoms in the chini cluster geometry and have been successful [45]. Therefore,
substitution of the metal atoms in the chini cluster architecture is a relevant design variable. We used Ni and
Pd as substituents as both belong to group 10 and are isovalent with Pt. Substitution therefore preserves
the valence electron count of the cluster and is expected to retain the chini framework, while changing the
electronic structure that controls Zn binding. We considered clusters with n = 1 and 2 and allowed any
number of Pt atoms, up to all of them, to be substituted. Each candidate structure was thus defined by its
dopant configuration together with the Zn position, and PYTHIA optimises both jointly.
We assessed and compared three models for adsorption energy prediction: the AUGUR baseline, MACE
model trained fully with a GP regressor as a final layer, and PYTHIA, which combines a frozen pretrained
MACE model with Transformers and Gaussian process.

3.2
Formaldehyde adsorption on Silicene
The chini cluster provided a good baseline but the limited range of defects does not fully showcase the
strength of PYTHIA. To this end, we apply our pipeline to a second system, that of formaldehyde adsorbed
by defective silicene. This system allows for more diverse and higher number of defects on the cluster, with
an added layer of complexity due to its periodic nature.. Additionally, the adsorbent has multiple atoms
and needs to be optimized rotationally as well as positionally.
Specifically, silicene was selected as the model substrate because its low-buckled structure and mixed
sp2/sp3 bonding make it more chemically reactive than graphene [46]. This renders its adsorption properties
highly sensitive to local structural and electronic modifications, driving considerable interest in silicene for
chemical sensing and surface functionalization [47]. Formaldehyde (CH2O) was chosen as the target adsor-
bate due to its importance as a hazardous volatile organic compound, for which sensitive room-temperature
detection remains an active area of research [48]. To generate a chemically diverse optimization problem, we
considered substitutional dopants spanning multiple classes with distinct electronic effects: isovalent Group
IV elements (C, Ge), donor-type Group V elements (N, P, As), acceptor-type Group III elements (B, Al, Ga,
In), and transition metals with partially filled d states. Main-group dopants have been extensively investi-
gated as a means of tailoring silicene’s electronic structure, band gap, charge distribution, and adsorption
behavior [49, 50, 51], while transition-metal dopants introduce localized d-derived states that can give rise
to magnetism, topological phases, and enhanced adsorbate interactions [52, 53, 54]. Together, these dopant
classes span a broad range of chemically distinct electronic environments, providing a diverse optimization
landscape for the active-learning framework. Finally, multiple simultaneous substitutions were included to
further increase the complexity of the search space, allowing the framework to explore cooperative effects
between different dopant species rather than being restricted to isolated substitutional defects.
The comparison between the baseline AUGUR model, fully trained MACE and PYTHIA can be seen
in Fig. 4. Unlike the chini case, silicene has a much richer defect space and the adsorbate CH2O more
complex placement options.

4
Discussion
In this work, we introduced PYTHIA (Probabilistic Yet Transferable Hierarchical Interaction Architecture),
a data-efficient transferable machine learning framework for identifying optimal molecular interaction sites
and defect choices for a variety of systems. By combining a pre-trained, frozen foundational model (MACE)
with a lightweight transformer-based translation layer and a Gaussian Process head, PYTHIA is able to
achieve high accuracy with limited data, while simultaneously providing a robust uncertainty estimate. This
makes PYTHIA suitable for a Bayesian Optimization framework, where efficient information acquisition is
equally important to final accuracy. We have tested our pipeline in two highly relevant cases, namely Zn
adsorption on the doped chini cluster family and CH2O adsorption on doped silicene. Across both cases,
PYTHIA outperformed all benchmarks leading to high improvement in the defect-site dual optimization
problem, as well as resulting in much more accurate final models. It must be noted that the prediction
accuracy of PYTHIA was high, even in defects not present in the dataset, thus paving the way to a full
chemical space investigation, a luxury rarely afforded with conventional approaches.
This modularity, combined with the demonstrated transferability to systems and defect configurations
not explicitly represented in the training set, suggests that PYTHIA is broadly applicable to other classes of
interaction-site optimization problems in computational materials discovery — including catalyst design, gas
sensing, and surface functionalization, where DFT data is scarce but adjacent, richly-labeled datasets exist
to transfer from. Lastly, PYTHIA is not limited by the case study or optimization objective. It can just
as seamlessly be applied to determining ideal solvents as well as defects and adsorption sites. Additionally,
