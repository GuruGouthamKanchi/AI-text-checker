Abstract
When a user says “Keep it short”, how short should the LLM’s response be?
More generally, users and applications require different levels of instruction ad-
herence, but prompting does not reliably provide fine-grained control over them.
We therefore study “steerable language models” that provide fine-grained numer-
ical control over the degree to which models follow general instructions specified
in text. However, existing approaches do not jointly offer fine-grained control,
efficiency, and scalability across diverse instructions. To address this gap, we in-
troduce DIAL (Distilled Instruction-Adherence Levels) to achieve all three prop-
erties. For fine-grained control, we perform classifier-free guidance (CFG) with a
frozen LLM to provide training signals for on-policy distillation of instruction ad-
herence at different levels. For efficiency, we design a lightweight steering adapter
that uses a classifier over the LLM’s hidden states to compute a closed-form steer-
ing edit. The classifier adaptively sets the steering magnitude at each token based
on the target adherence level, while accounting for the geometry of the LLM’s
hidden states to preserve response coherence. For scalability, the classifier is
text-conditioned, allowing a shared adapter to support new instructions without
per-instruction fitting. Training requires only instruction and request text, with-
out labeled responses, external generators, or judges. Evaluation across multiple
LLM families shows that DIAL achieves a competitive balance between concept
expression and response fluency, with concept scores increasing monotonically
across adherence levels. We introduce SteerIF, an instruction-only dataset of
98, 625 concept, persona, verifiable, and descriptive instructions. When trained
on SteerIF, DIAL provides fine-grained control over personas and, when com-
bined with prompting, improves verifiable instruction following. In vLLM, the
LLM steered by DIAL retains 96–99% of the base LLM throughput, with per-
token inference latency approximately matching that of the base LLM.

the desired length. More generally, fine-grained adherence control for different instructions would
support a range of applications (Figure 1(b)). For personalization, a user could select the adherence
level for writing-style or persona instructions according to their preferences [2, 3]. For parental con-

A company could configure the adherence level for product-policy instructions to reflect its speci-
fications [5, 6]. Motivated by these applications, we study “steerable language models” [7, 8] that
provide fine-grained numerical control over adherence levels for general text instructions.
Existing approaches for controlling adherence levels in LLMs are not jointly fine-grained, efficient,
and scalable across general instructions. The most straightforward approach is prompting, but it
does not reliably provide fine-grained control over the adherence level [9, 10]. In contrast, classifier-
free guidance (CFG) [11] enables fine-grained control by computing two next-token distributions:
one conditioned on the instruction and one unconditioned. It then combines their log probabili-
ties to achieve the desired adherence level [12, 13]. However, this requires two forward passes per
generated token, increasing inference cost. Activation steering provides a more efficient approach
by editing the hidden states of a frozen LLM within a single inference pass [14–17]. Adjustable
steering strength offers a promising way to adapt an LLM at inference time [18]. However, conven-
tional steering methods are not fine-grained because they are fitted to labeled examples that indicate
whether an instruction is followed, without supervision at intermediate adherence levels. Increasing
steering strength therefore does not reliably increase adherence and can reduce fluency and coher-
ence [19]. Moreover, these methods require fitting for each instruction, limiting scalability. Text-
conditioned steering methods learn to steer an LLM from instruction text and support instructions
unseen during training [20–22]. However, they are trained on labeled responses without supervision
at intermediate adherence levels, and flow-based variants add per-token inference overhead.

three key properties: fine-grained control, efficiency, and scalability. For fine-grained control, we
perform CFG on a frozen LLM to provide training signals for on-policy distillation of instruction ad-
herence at different levels (Section 3.2). For efficiency, we design a lightweight steering adapter [23]
that uses a classifier over the LLM’s hidden states to compute a closed-form steering edit. The clas-

while accounting for the geometry of the LLM’s hidden states to preserve the base LLM’s response

Related Work
We review three lines of work on steering LLMs. Guided inference is fine-grained but incurs sub-
stantial inference overhead; activation steering is efficient, but neither fine-grained nor scalable;
and text-conditioned control generalizes to unseen instructions, but lacks fine-grained control and
requires labeled responses, limiting scalability. See Appendix A for details.
Guided Inference. In diffusion models [26–28], guided inference controls how strongly a condition,
such as a text prompt, influences the generated image. Given the prompt “a cat in the style of Van
Gogh,” a low guidance level gives an image that only hints at the style, and a high level gives one
that is unmistakably Van Gogh. Classifier guidance pushes each sampling step along the gradient of
a separately trained classifier [29], while classifier-free guidance (CFG) removes this classifier by
using the diffusion model itself as an implicit classifier [11]. In LLMs, CFG combines the next-token
predictions with and without the instruction [12, 13]. A low guidance (i.e., adherence) level weakens
the instruction relative to prompting, and a high level strengthens it. Related contrastive inference
methods for LLMs improve faithfulness, instruction following, and open-ended generation [30–33].
Because every guidance level defines a next-token distribution for any instruction, guided inference
is fine-grained. However, CFG is not efficient, because every generated token needs two forward
passes. This motivates guidance distillation in diffusion models, which trains a single-pass student
conditioned on the guidance level, thereby recovering CFG at multiple levels [34]. In autoregressive
models, CFG has been distilled into the weights only at a single level [35]. Unlike a diffusion
model, an LLM takes only tokens and has no natural input for the guidance level. DIAL serves as a
lightweight steering adapter that conditions the LLM on the adherence level, approximating CFG at
multiple levels in a single pass without changing the LLM’s weights or prompt.
Activation Steering. Activation steering offers a promising way to adapt an LLM cheaply at multi-
ple levels [18]. It edits the hidden states of a frozen LLM in a single forward pass while leaving the
weights unchanged, allowing the steering level to be set for each input at inference time. A com-
mon intervention is to use a steering vector obtained from contrastive hidden states [14, 15], which
has been used to control truthfulness [16], refusal [17], and instruction following [36]. Classifier-
guided steering methods project the hidden state onto the half-space of a trained classifier in closed
form [37–39], or onto the constraint set given by a first-order condition on a learned nonlinear safety
score [40]. Geometry-aware methods rotate the state rather than translate it, preserving the hidden
state’s norm [41–43]. These interventions are efficient, and their steering level can be varied contin-
uously. However, they are fitted to labeled examples that either follow the instruction or do not, with
no training at intermediate levels of adherence, and are therefore not fine-grained. Increasing the
steering level therefore does not reliably yield a corresponding increase in adherence, and high levels
often reduce the output fluency and coherence [19]. Moreover, each intervention is fitted to a single
instruction, such as one concept or behavior, so each new instruction requires a new intervention.

Text-Conditioned Control. To avoid refitting for every instruction, text-conditioned methods train a
single network that maps a text description to an intervention. HyperSteer maps a concept descrip-
tion to a steering vector [20], FLAS integrates a description-conditioned flow [21], and UniSteer
trains a text-conditioned flow-matching network that also covers personas and multi-instruction fol-
lowing [22]. Their training, however, suffers from the same two problems as activation steering.
They are fitted to labeled examples, here concept-aligned completions written by an external LLM
or labeled datasets for each behavior, so scaling to a new kind of instruction requires collecting new
data. These examples also either follow the instruction or do not, so the methods are never trained
for intermediate levels of adherence and are not fine-grained. Persona Dosing partly addresses
this for personas by fine-tuning FLAS on persona-labeled data and calibrating its flow time against
measured persona expression [44], which requires measuring each persona, whereas DIAL learns its
levels during training and needs no such measurement for a new instruction. Moreover, flow-based
methods integrate a flow over several steps per token, which adds latency and limits practicality.
To address these limitations, DIAL distills the frozen LLM’s own CFG distributions at multiple
adherence levels into a single steering adapter. Since the teacher requires only instruction and request
text, training can scale to new types of instructions without collecting labeled responses. The adapter
learns to approximate the CFG-guided distribution at each target level, enabling fine-grained control
while keeping inference cost close to that of the base LLM. DIAL thus provides a fine-grained,
efficient, and scalable way to condition an LLM on general instructions and adherence levels.
3
Method: DIAL (Distilled Instruction-Adherence Levels)
DIAL uses a text-conditioned steering adapter to control instruction adherence in a frozen LLM at
different levels. We first define adherence levels via CFG (Section 3.1), and then describe how on-
policy distillation trains the adapter at different adherence levels (Section 3.2). Finally, we introduce
the adapter’s lightweight classifier-based architecture and closed-form steering edit (Section 3.3).
3.1
Instruction-Adherence Levels via Classifier-Free Guidance
Let x denote a request (e.g., “How do vaccines work?”), c an instruction (e.g., “Keep it short”),
and α > 0 an adherence level. Given a response prefix y<t, the frozen LLM yields two next-
token distributions: πbase, conditioned on the request alone, and πprompted, conditioned on both the
request and the instruction. For brevity, we write π·(· | y<t) and leave the conditioning on x and c
implicit. Following CFG [12, 13], we define the teacher distribution at level α as
πT
α(· | y<t) ∝πbase(· | y<t)1−α πprompted(· | y<t)α.
(1)
For α = 1, the teacher recovers πprompted. For 0 < α < 1, it interpolates toward πbase, weakening
the instruction’s influence. For α > 1, it extrapolates beyond πprompted, strengthening the instruc-
tion’s influence. Thus, larger α corresponds to stronger instruction adherence, and CFG provides a
next-token distribution at each adherence level for any instruction text, without labeled responses,
external generators, or judges. The prompt format for each instruction type is given in Appendix B.4.

linear classifier with half-space {h : ⟨ut, h⟩≥τt(α)}. Unlike classifiers fitted separately for each
instruction, DIAL’s classifier is generated from instruction and memory information at each token.

Experiments
We evaluate DIAL when the instruction is to express a concept, adopt a persona, or satisfy a verifi-
able requirement. We also compare its training and inference efficiency against baselines.
Base LLMs. We train DIAL’s adapters for instruction-tuned LLMs: Gemma-2-9B [25], Qwen2.5-
7B [47], Llama-3.1-8B [48], Phi-4-14B [49], LFM2.5-1.2B [50], and Ministral-8B [51]. These
cover six LLM families with 1.2–14B parameters, including Transformer and hybrid architectures.
Training Setup. We train DIAL on 14, 880 concept descriptions from Concept16k [19]. We also
construct SteerIF, an instruction-only dataset of 98, 625 instructions covering concepts, personas,
and verifiable and descriptive requirements, given as single or composite instructions. We train one
shared adapter per LLM on SteerIF, denoted DIAL-IF. For both datasets, we remove exact and
near matches to held-out evaluation instructions to assess generalization. We select a steering layer
for each LLM and train its adapter at that layer. See Appendix B.3 for more details on dataset
construction and to Appendix B.4 for the steered layer chosen for each LLM.
4.1
Controlling Concept Expression Levels
Training and Baselines.
We train DIAL for one epoch on 14,880 concept descriptions from
Concept16k [19]. We use FLAS’s released Gemma-2-9B checkpoint, trained on Concept16k with
LLM-generated labeled responses [21], whereas DIAL uses only concept descriptions. We com-
pare with prompting, per-concept methods (LoReFT, LoRA, ReFT-r1, DiffMean, and SAE [19], SV
+ RePS [52], and S-PSR [53]), and text-conditioned methods (HyperSteer and FLAS). We also in-
clude CFG [12, 13] for fine-grained control over concept expression, and prompting w/ level [54],
which uses the same prompt as prompting but also asks the LLM to follow it at adherence level α,
on a scale from 0 (ignore) to 2 (follow strongly). See Appendix C.1 for implementation details.
Evaluation Protocol. We evaluate on AxBench’s Concept500 (Held-In) and FLAS’s 100 held-
out concepts (Held-Out). HyperSteer’s published Held-Out score uses its own held-out concepts.
Neither Concept500 nor the held-out concepts appear in DIAL’s training pool. Following the
AxBench protocol, we limit responses to 128 tokens and steer DIAL at layer 20 for Gemma-2-9B.

level α across concepts. FLAS controls concept expression through its flow time T, with larger T
