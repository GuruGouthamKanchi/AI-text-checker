Abstract
Large language models have shown outstanding perfor-
mance on text-first tasks such as coding, math, and writ-
ing. Recent efforts are shifting models’ training to include
scientific problems that often live in highly specialized do-
mains beyond text, such as protein structure and biological
images. However, in these scenarios, their general-purpose
accuracy compared with specialized algorithms remains in
question. One such domain is cell tracking, which requires
spatiotemporal awareness of cell dynamics.
To evaluate
this, we created a new benchmark, CellTrack-Bench, which
assesses Vision-Language Models’ (VLMs) cell-tracking-
related performance against specialized trackers. The full
benchmark draws from 15 standard cell-tracking datasets
across four imaging modalities and comprises 1,537 prob-
lems corresponding to 9,041 cell-linking decisions. We se-
lected a hard subset requiring greater reasoning effort by
identifying failures shared across distinct algorithmic track-
ers.
With this proposed dataset, we: (i) evaluated dif-
ferent environment harnesses for VLMs; (ii) benchmarked
9 frontier closed-source models and 13 open-weight mod-
els; and (iii) provided a detailed analysis of their failure
modes.
Code, the stored model outputs, and a pipeline
that regenerates the benchmark from the source datasets
are available at https://github.com/royerlab/
celltrack-bench.
1. Introduction
Cells exhibit complex behaviors, including proliferation,
differentiation, and migration, throughout their life, result-
ing in complex biological patterns such as embryonic de-
velopment [24, 30], tissue mechanics [20], and immune re-
sponse [25]. To quantify this dynamic cellular behavior at

the single-cell level, it is necessary to identify individual
cells and to track them over time [31].
Cell tracking shares many similarities with Multi-Object
Tracking (MOT) [12] in the traditional natural imag-
ing domain, as both involve identifying and tracking ob-
jects. They’re commonly solved through the paradigm of
tracking-by-detection, where an algorithm segments or de-
tects the objects and then links them over time. However,
cell tracking benefits from specialized solutions [7, 28, 45]
because objects are almost identical, can be divided, and
quickly undergo morphological changes, unlike objects in
natural images (e.g., cars, pedestrians).
Here, we take a different approach. Rather than dividing
the problem from the algorithmic point of view, we view
the operations in two different regimes: (i) a highly me-
chanical scenario where it’s easy to design an algorithm
to automatically link the objects, for example, with opti-
mal matching [13, 21]; and (ii) a highly cognitive scenario
where one must reason about what is happening within the
image, considering cell state, if undergoing division, if near
the boundary of the field of view, and many other experi-
mental conditions, see Figure 1 for a practical example.
This motivates the development of a system where ex-
isting cell tracking algorithms perform the mechanical as-
pect of tracking, while VLMs perform the highly cognitive
correction of mistakes made by these approaches. This is
analogous to a scenario where an annotator is fixing the
tracking mistakes. To validate this novel paradigm for cell
tracking, we built CellTrack-Bench from 15 cell-tracking
datasets across four imaging modalities. Thirteen datasets
contribute 1,537 problems, amounting to 9,041 linking in-
stances.
For CellTrack-Bench, we define three tasks that progres-
sively change the role of the VLM from directly construct-
ing associations, to correcting an existing tracking solution,
to identifying errors for review: (i) cell linking; (ii) track
repair; and (iii) track inspection.
In cell linking, the model directly predicts the correspon-
dence between cells in two consecutive frames. In track

We apply these three tasks to difficult cell tracking cases
that are local rather than to complete time-lapse sequences.
In a normal cell-tracking problem, an algorithm handles a
complete time-lapse series of microscopy images; in our ap-
proach, each problem is concentrated on the more difficult
cell links (see Figure 2). This is necessary since, in most
datasets having sufficient temporal resolution, cells do not
move much until a cell-state change takes place, at which
point their visual appearance may change and they can ex-
hibit erratic movement and displacement. Although such
events are rare and more prone to error, the overall tracking
performance can mask them. We identify the difficult cases
algorithmically by looking at failures that are common to
multiple trackers.
After having chosen the difficult cases and defined the
tasks, assessing the VLMs also required controlling the way
in which these problems are presented to the model. For
this reason, we conducted a study looking at various envi-
ronment strategies, recently known as harnesses, such as
the method of presenting cells, the way in which coordi-
nate information is given, the use of multi-turn settings,
and visual hints like segmentation outlines. For our study
we carried out a validation-only investigation into environ-
mental changes with respect to the VLM and kept the best-
performing configuration for the test evaluation. We also
introduce proximity-debiased metrics which separately as-
sess associations that are not already accounted for by a
nearest-neighbor rule, together with division-specific and
graph-level measures. Our protocol also takes into account
the composition of the dataset and the output-token budget.
In summary, our contributions are:
• A benchmark comprising 1,537 hard-mined adjacent-
frame problems, with 9,041 linking associations spanning
15 datasets and four imaging modalities, structured as a
series of tasks concerning cell linking, track repair, and
track inspection.
• A controlled evaluation protocol for VLM-based cell
tracking, including validation-only harness selection,
proximity-debiased, and division-specific metrics, a Cell
Tracking Challenge (CTC) comparable graph score,
dataset-stratified evaluation, and inference budget.
• A study evaluating 9 frontier closed-source and 13 open-
weight VLMs by means of a fixed interface, together with
geometric and specialized tracking references, looking at
proximity shortcuts, interface sensitivity, and token bud-
get effects, and task relationships.

tions our work within the literature. In Section 3, we explain
how the benchmark is created and how we define the evalu-

over the results of closed-source and open-weight models,
followed by our final considerations in Section 5.
2. Related Works
Cell-tracking methods and benchmarks.
With recent
advancements in instance segmentation models [18, 40, 41],
cell-tracking methods have almost exclusively relied on
the tracking-by-detection paradigm, focusing on association
(edge) prediction, as TrackMate [21, 43] and btrack [44] on
the algorithmic side. On the deep learning front, Track-
astra [14] predicts pairwise cell detection associations us-
ing a transformer over temporal windows, while HOCT [8]

The most widely regarded
benchmark is the Cell Tracking Challenge (CTC), which
standardized performance evaluation using lineage-aware
graph-edit metrics [28, 45] and includes a hidden test eval-
uated by the organizers. Our proposed benchmark uses a
similar methodology, assuming cell segmentation is given,
and evaluating performance based on local associations be-
tween consecutive frames.
Because even very effective trackers still produce a few
mistakes, human-in-the-loop review is often necessary. Pop
et al. [37] framed the verification as a multi-armed bandit
problem.
OrganoidTracker flags suspicious links for re-
view [23]. Meanwhile, its successor, OrganoidTracker 2,
computes probabilities for each link [6]. These interactive
approaches motivate our track repair and track inspection
tasks, in which a VLM serves as an external assistant that
either corrects or prioritizes links for revision. Because the
difficulty of tracking individual cells is heavily skewed to-
wards easy cells, existing metrics fail to assess the quality of
hard instances; therefore, CellTrack-Bench extends evalua-
tion from tracker-specific metrics to a more specific assess-
ment of the model’s ability to link, repair, and inspect hard
instances across multiple trackers and imaging modalities.
Biomedical VLM benchmarks.
The µ-Bench [27]
attempts to measure perception and cognition with re-
spect to light, fluorescence, and electron microscopy. Mi-
croVQA [9] focuses on expert scientific reasoning using
single- and multi-image questions and answers. OmniMed-
VQA [19] evaluates medical image understanding on a
large scale.
Both MedFrameQA [47] and general video
benchmarks like MVBench [26] also look into temporal
reasoning. These benchmarks primarily consist of classi-
fication, localization, or question-answering tasks. In con-
trast, CellTrack-Bench evaluates cell-identity correspon-
dence, including continuations, divisions, appearances, and
disappearances, as well as explicit changes to lineage
graphs.
Shortcut-resistant and controlled evaluation. Bench-
mark performance might overestimate a model’s visual un-
derstanding if the model makes use of linguistic or visual
shortcuts [10, 15]. In cell tracking, the main shortcut is spa-
tial proximity: a nearest-neighbor rule accounts for much
of the association distribution. CellTrack-Bench therefore
uses sequence-disjoint validation/test splits, implements a
ranked tracker-error mining, per-dataset sampling caps, and
uses debiased metrics with respect to proximity or division
events.
VLM performance can also depend on how the
problem is presented and the models’ harness [48]. There-
fore, the evaluation protocol includes the renderer, the coor-
dinate exploration interface, the prompt, and the inference
budget. We selected these components based on validation
data and kept them fixed during testing. The result is a con-
trolled diagnostic benchmark for VLM-assisted cell linking
and track inspection, not a new tracker or training method.
3. CellTrack-Bench
In this section, we explain how we constructed CellTrack-
Bench. First, in Section 3.1 we describe the standard pro-
tocol used in the Cell Tracking Challenge (CTC) [28, 45],
and in Section 3.2 we document the data we gathered. Then,
in Section 3.3, we explain how we use state-of-the-art and

3.1. Preliminaries
The tracking problem is formulated as constructing a bi-
nary forest (i.e., each node can have at most two children)
from a candidate graph, GC = (VC, EC), which contains
a superset of valid tracking solutions; throughout the text,
we will refer to the predicted graph as a solution graph
Gpred = (Vpred, Epred) and the ground-truth graph com-
puted from the annotations as Ggt = (Vgt, Egt).
The nodes V· from all graphs come from each frame t
of the segmentation sequence (video), such that segments
from a single frame are V t ⊂VC.
CellTrack-Bench retains the images, segmentations, and
lineage annotations from existing tracking datasets but
changes the unit of evaluation from a complete sequence
to local transitions, eij ∈Egt ∪Epred between frames t
and t + 1 where i ∈V t and j ∈V t+1, allowing errors to
be isolated and diagnosed at the frame level. Each instance
contains a compact group of source cells that includes the
specific error, and the linking, repair, and inspection tasks
share the same local transition (construction detail in Sec-
tion 3.4).
3.2. Source Data
CellTrack-Bench draws from 10 CTC and five bacterial
datasets (35 sequences), encompassing fluorescence, phase-
contrast, differential-interference-contrast, and bright-field
microscopy. Object size, density, morphology, and motion
vary widely. See Figure 3.
We partition sequences before ranking or selecting links
to avoid temporal leakage, so visually adjacent transitions
from the same lineage cannot appear in both partitions. In
CTC datasets, we assign sequence 01 to validation and
02 to test. Similarly, we split the multi-sequence bacterial
datasets into identifier-ordered halves (per-dataset assign-
ments in Appendix A). Every benchmark decision is eval-
uated using the validation dataset, and the test dataset was
only used for the final leaderboard score.
3.3. Specialized Trackers as Difficulty Probes
We argue that trackers’ errors are not independent and likely
identify mistakes in challenging links. We thus use three
different trackers with different inductive biases and use
their shared errors as a hard-mining signal to find such dif-
ficult links and mitigate uncorrelated wrong links, which
might be just a tracker’s shortcoming.
All three trackers jointly fail at 0.28% of the 1.5M can-
didate links (4,222 links exactly), so the dependence ar-
gument is supported empirically, as we would expect only
0.0022% shared errors under independence (33.2; stratify-
ing by sequence already accounts for some sequences being
uniformly harder; see Appendix A.3). Moreover, given one
tracker error, another tracker is 11.3–49.6 times more likely
to commit the same error. The trackers are:
• Hungarian Matching on Distance. It is a one-to-one
assignment baseline based uniquely on centroid distance.
As such, it doesn’t model appearance and divisions. Al-
though simple, this baseline scores fairly high on the
CTC’s LNK metric of 0.888. This reinforces that cell po-
sition is already a strong tracking feature.
• Trackastra.
This tracking method feeds cell-segment
features as tokens in a transformer network and directly
learns the association matrix.
Its LNK score is 0.963.

• HOCT. As a natural extension to Trackastra, this is a

Each model is trained and evaluated with k-folds, by
splitting the 15 tracking datasets into three groups of five
datasets each. To get the errors for one fold, we train on
the other two and evaluate on the held-out fold. All track-

we focus on association rather than detection quality. Ap-

3.6. Prompting and Harness Design
As described above, a benchmark instance specifies the
task, the candidate graph, and the ground-truth graph, and
their respective scene from the original image data, but not
the way in which this information is presented to a VLM.
As in previous work [48], we call this harness the envi-
ronment the models are subject to; it includes the image
rendering, the prompt template, the localization cues, the
available tools, the generation budget, the answer schema,
and the parser. The way these elements are chosen can have
a significant effect on performance by making cell localiza-
tion and visual comparison easier or more difficult.
We look at eight conditions, as summarized in Ta-
ble 1. Those were chosen considering different ways that
the model could operate. In each case, we use the same
frames, candidate graph, task instruction, answer schema,
and scorer; the only difference is the evidence interface.
The conditions vary by whether the cells are identified us-
ing normalized bounding boxes (text-based) or visual out-
lines (image-based), whether close-up crops are provided
in advance or requested by the model, and whether the
global two-frame overview is available. They involve two
main types of interface: an evidence-supplied single-turn
interface, which offers annotated frames and pre-rendered
cell crops, and an evidence-requested multi-turn interface,
in which the model chooses the areas to examine using
a crop_and_zoom tool. Condition c7 omits the global
overview and is considered merely as an information-loss
ablation. Appendix A.5 illustrates the two interface fami-
lies and all eight evidence presentations in Figures 4 and 5,
respectively.
The harness selection uses only the validation partition
that is disjoint in sequence. We assess all eight conditions
on the 205 validation neighborhoods and all three tasks us-
ing gpt-5.5 with a low level of reasoning effort, which
yields 4,920 turn-based conversations considering all prob-
lems, tasks, and conditions. Each request is given a total
budget of 8,192 tokens for both reasoning and tool use,
2,048 of which are set aside for the final structured an-
swer. The selection statistic, reported in Appendix Table 5,
is the average of the three dataset-macro Overall F1 scores;
not to be confused with the diagnostic NN-residual link-
ing F1 shown in Table 1. By this selection statistic, con-
dition c1 has the highest validation mean, 0.609, compared
with 0.547 for the next-best condition, c8. The difference
is 0.061, and the 95% hierarchical-bootstrap confidence in-
terval is [0.005,0.120]. We therefore chose c1, where the
model is given an unannotated overview of the two frames
and the normalized bounding boxes for each candidate cell,
and it can obtain closer views by using crop_and_zoom.
A post-hoc check carried out with claude-opus-4.8
confirms the advantage of tool-bearing coordinate interfaces
over the evidence-supplied interface, even though it does
not independently identify c1 as being better than c8.
We freeze the renderer, prompt, tool implementation,
parser, token budget, and decoding configuration after se-
lection. Each model included on the leaderboard is evalu-
ated using the same c1 harness without making any further
interface choices, and the test results take no part in the se-
lection process. Detailed versions of the prompt and tool
schema are given in Appendices A.4 and A.4.1.
