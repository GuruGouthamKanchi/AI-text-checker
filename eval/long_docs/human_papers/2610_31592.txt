ABSTRACT
Diffusion models have become strong priors for solving inverse problems. How-
ever, nonlinear inverse problems such as phase retrieval remain challenging when
multiple measurement-consistent solutions are distinguishable only by the prior;
we call such measurements ambiguous. Existing diffusion-based samplers rely
on local approximations during reconstruction. For an ambiguous measurement,
these approximations can allow measurement guidance to favor a measurement-
consistent mode that is close to a prior-supported mode but is itself not supported
by the prior. Evaluation often masks this type of failure by reporting the best-of-k
runs, where the best is selected by comparison to the ground truth. We introduce
Prior-Consistent DAPS (PC-DAPS), a variant of Decoupled Annealing Posterior
Sampling (DAPS) designed to address this failure case by maintaining multiple
candidate solutions within a single sampling run. As annealing noise decreases,
PC-DAPS prunes and replaces weaker candidates according to their prior consis-
tency, without any ground-truth access. PC-DAPS reduces the incidence of prior-
inconsistent samples and improves sample quality on nonlinear inverse problems
while preserving sample quality and diversity on linear inverse problems.†

process, the goal of the inverse problem is to recover the original signal x. For underdetermined
problems, in which many different signals x may give rise to the same measurement y, Bayesian
imaging defines the posterior distribution as p(x | y) ∝p(y | x)p(x), where the likelihood p(y | x)
captures measurement consistency of a candidate image x and the prior p(x) captures which candi-
date reconstructions are plausible natural signals. The goal of posterior sampling is thus to generate
candidate reconstructions that are simultaneously consistent with the measurement and the prior.
In Fourier phase retrieval, for example, multiple images can produce identical Fourier magnitude
measurements (Fienup, 1982). These measurement-equivalent solutions therefore have the same
likelihood, but can have very different posterior probabilities depending on their consistency with
the image prior. In particular, we observe that existing phase-retrieval solvers applied to face images
sometimes return a rotated or color-inverted face that is consistent with the measurement but im-
plausible under a natural-image prior concentrated on upright faces. The challenge is therefore not
simply to find measurement-consistent reconstructions, but to sample the measurement-consistent
solutions supported by the prior.
Diffusion models provide strong image priors for posterior sampling (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al.,
2021). At each noise level, a diffusion model implicitly defines a denoiser that maps a noisy sample
xt to an estimate of the corresponding clean image; this diffusion denoiser can be used to incor-
porate the prior p(x) during posterior sampling. Diffusion-based inverse solvers apply this prior
information along a sampling trajectory, i.e., the sequence of intermediate states that evolves from
a noisy sample toward a reconstruction. Existing methods primarily either tilt the unconditional
reverse diffusion process using an approximate likelihood gradient (Zhang et al., 2025; Chung et al.,
2023; Song et al., 2023; Boys et al., 2024) or alternate prior and measurement updates across noise
levels (Wu et al., 2024; Xu & Chi, 2024; Chen et al., 2026; Ahmed & Makin, 2026). In these
methods, the prior information available at each noise level is represented through a local denoising
approximation centered on a single clean-image estimate, rather than explicitly maintaining multiple
competing prior modes at intermediate timesteps.
When the measurement is ambiguous, as in the case of phase retrieval and other underdetermined
inverse problems, following a sampling trajectory can lead to premature mode selection. At high
noise levels, multiple prior modes may remain plausible, but the trajectory can only move towards
one of them. Once a trajectory selects a particular prior mode, subsequent refinement primarily
improves measurement consistency in the local neighborhood of that mode rather than considering
more distant alternatives. This becomes problematic when (i) the selected prior mode is not con-
sistent with the measurement, and (ii) its nearest measurement-consistent mode is not supported by
the prior. Trajectory-based samplers can prematurely commit to this pair of modes, ultimately re-
sulting in hallucinatory sampled reconstructions that are not supported by the true posterior or even
the true prior. Best-of-k evaluation partially masks this issue by generating multiple independent
reconstructions and selecting the one closest to the ground truth (Chung et al., 2023; Zhang et al.,
2025). However, this selection uses information unavailable at test time and does not ensure that the
produced samples are prior-consistent. Particle-based approaches maintain multiple candidates at
intermediate timesteps (Cardoso et al., 2024; Dou & Song, 2024; Wu et al., 2023), but have primar-
ily focused on linear inverse problems and particle diversity rather than prior-consistent sampling.
To address this issue, we introduce Prior-Consistent DAPS (PC-DAPS), which moves sample selec-
tion inside the sampler and removes the need for ground-truth-based best-of-k evaluation. PC-DAPS
maintains a bag of K particles annealed under the same measurement, and models the denoiser pos-
terior p(x0 | xt) with a K-component Gaussian mixture rather than a unimodal estimate. Early in
sampling, multiple candidates are kept while the noise level is high and the prior is still weakly in-
formative. As the noise decreases during annealing, particles with low prior consistency are pruned
and replaced by surviving candidates that are more prior-consistent. PC-DAPS thus delays mode
selection until the prior becomes more informative, performing within a single sampling run (and
without ground-truth access) what best-of-k does only after k independent sampling runs.
We evaluate PC-DAPS on FFHQ (Karras et al., 2019) and ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009) across three
nonlinear and seven linear tasks, including the eight tasks reported in DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025) as
well as high-pass filtering (Thaker et al., 2025) and 16× super-resolution. We report PSNR, SSIM
(Wang et al., 2004), and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) under both single-sample and best-of-k eval-
uation. We additionally compare the rate of prior-inconsistent hallucinations against independent
DAPS runs. PC-DAPS reduces the incidence of prior-inconsistent samples and improves perfor-

RELATED WORK
Likelihood-guided diffusion sampling. Given a diffusion prior that has learned the unconditional
score ∇xt log p(xt), we can decompose the conditional score (after observing measurement y) as
∇xt log p(xt | y) = ∇xt log p(xt) + ∇xt log p(y | xt). The likelihood term ∇xt log p(y | xt)
is intractable, so a family of methods approximates it to steer the reverse diffusion process.
In particular, DPS (Chung et al., 2023) evaluates the likelihood at the one-step posterior mean
p(y | xt) ≈p(y | E[x0 | xt]) and backpropagates to compute the score. For linear measurement
operators, ΠGDM (Song et al., 2023) and TMPD (Boys et al., 2024) instead propagate Gaussian ap-
proximations of p(x0 | xt), while DDRM (Kawar et al., 2022) utilizes a diagonalization approach.
MCG (Chung et al., 2022) adds a manifold-correction term to keep the guided iterate close to the
data manifold. Jin et al. (2026) target hallucination inside this family of approaches by stabilizing
the prior update, but remain committed to a single trajectory.
Split and annealed posterior sampling. In contrast, variable-splitting methods alternate between
prior-only and measurement-only steps. Among methods using a diffusion model for the prior step,
PnP-DM (Wu et al., 2024) uses a split Gibbs sampler for the likelihood, while DPnP (Xu & Chi,
2024) uses Metropolis-Hastings and DiffPIR (Zhu et al., 2023) utilizes a proximal step. Finally,
DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025) utilizes a repeated denoising-renoising scheme (Section 3.2). RePS
(Ahmed & Makin, 2026) adds restarts to that schedule, and DAPS++ (Chen et al., 2026) reports
that the DAPS reconstruction is largely driven by measurement consistency. We therefore design
PC-DAPS as a modification of DAPS that retains its variable-splitting structure but introduces prior-
based selection to discriminate between measurement-consistent reconstructions.
Particle-based posterior sampling.
Another group of approaches maintains many particles
throughout sampling, and uses sequential Monte Carlo to propagate, reweight, and resample par-
ticles to progressively improve measurement consistency (Cardoso et al., 2024; Janati et al., 2025).
FPS-SMC (Dou & Song, 2024) casts the reverse sampling process as a state-space model and ap-
plies a particle filter. TDS (Wu et al., 2023) uses twisting functions to obtain asymptotic exactness
for general measurement models. While particle-based methods are typically concerned with the
fidelity of the ensemble in the limit of many particles, we are concerned instead with the probabil-
ity that a single returned reconstruction is prior-inconsistent, and use particles for the purpose of
exploring the posterior landscape.
Diagnosing hallucination and prior misfit. Hallucination has been studied as a property of recon-
struction algorithms in inverse problems since before diffusion priors were adopted (Bhadra et al.,
2021), and refers to any reconstruction that is inconsistent with either the measurement or the prior.
Recent work has sought to quantify hallucination specifically for diffusion-based solvers (Jin et al.,
2026). At the level of the model rather than the individual sample, DiME (Wang & Bouman, 2026)
estimates the evidence of a diffusion prior by integrating over the time-marginals of a posterior
sampler, and can be run alongside DAPS to detect prior misfit. Closest to our framing, Burns &
Fridovich-Keil (2026) adopt a finite-sample perspective on reverse diffusion dynamics and show
that widely used guidance approximations misestimate posterior spread at intermediate noise lev-
els. This misestimation produces both prior modes absent from the true posterior and likelihood
(measurement-consistent) modes unsupported by the prior, which is the specific failure that our
PC-DAPS method aims to address. Benchmarks whose posteriors are analytically tractable pro-
vide a complementary check on the uncertainty a solver reports (Crafts & Villa, 2025; Zach et al.,
2026; Zheng et al., 2025). All of these existing hallucination diagnostics are applied after sampling;
PC-DAPS utilizes prior support during sampling to decide which reconstructions survive, thereby
reducing the incidence of prior-inconsistent hallucinations.

measurement- and prior-consistent samples.
3.2
DECOUPLED ANNEALING POSTERIOR SAMPLING (DAPS)
To develop a sampler which produces fewer prior-inconsistent samples, we build on existing ap-
proaches by (i) identifying the root causes that produce the inconsistent samples and (ii) making
adjustments to reduce the incidence of these samples. Specifically, we build on DAPS (Zhang et al.,
2025), a diffusion-based variable-splitting approach which repeatedly (i) denoises to a clean image
via the reverse diffusion process, (ii) improves the clean image’s likelihood consistency via MCMC
(e.g., Langevin dynamics (Roberts & Tweedie, 1996)), and (iii) renoises the MCMC output via the
forward diffusion process. By adding less noise back than was originally present, DAPS aims to
iteratively denoise towards a measurement-consistent clean sample.

Figure 2 illustrates this phenomenon. Here, the prior places mass on two candidate solutions, x1 and
x2, while the measurement y is explained by x1 and a third point x3 unsupported by the prior. In
analogy to Fourier phase retrieval, one can think of x1 as the desired reconstruction, e.g., an upright,
non-blue face that is both consistent with the measurement and supported by the image prior, x2 as
a realistic face inconsistent with the particular measurement, and x3 as a structurally implausible
transformation of the desired image x1, such as a rotated or blue face. The exact posterior should
favor the x1 mode while assigning zero mass to the prior-inconsistent x3. However, DAPS samples
from x3’s neighborhood by (i) sampling x2 from the prior and (ii) sampling x3 from x2’s local
likelihood. Once a particle enters this locally attractive likelihood-consistent region, subsequent
renoising and Langevin updates become trapped, resulting in premature mode selection.

4
METHOD
To address the presence of prior-inconsistent samples, a first approach might be to produce multiple
samples via DAPS and discard any which are prior-inconsistent. There are two main shortcomings
of this approach: (i) the effective sample size of this sampling method is suboptimal, as substantial
computational resources are used to produce samples that are then thrown out, and (ii) it is unclear
how to select which samples to discard without having access to the ground truth signal or posterior.
To address these issues, we introduce PC-DAPS, a procedure for correcting prematurely committed
particles during the sampling process without requiring access to the ground truth image or to any
pre-trained prior-inconsistency classifier. PC-DAPS maintains a bag of K particles, each of which
is denoised using the same annealing schedule and pretrained denoiser, but uses different initializa-
tions and random noise in its Langevin and renoising updates. If no additional modifications are
made to DAPS, the K particles will denoise independently, and can be partitioned into (nonempty)
sets of “bad” prematurely committed particles and “good” particles that are posterior-consistent.
The core idea of PC-DAPS is to improve DAPS’s existing renoising strategy by identifying prema-
turely committed particles during sampling and moving them to more promising modes discovered
by other particles in the bag. Additionally, PC-DAPS utilizes the particles to refine additional design
decisions made by DAPS, such as by replacing the unimodal Gaussian approximation to the denois-
ing distribution with an adaptive Gaussian mixture model (GMM) approximation. A toy example
illustrating the benefit of this Gaussian mixture approximation is shown in Appendix A.3.
What remains is to describe how PC-DAPS (i) identifies when particles commit prematurely, (ii)
identifies which particles are problematic, (iii) corrects the identified particles, and (iv) constructs
the multimodal denoising distribution approximation from the bag. PC-DAPS makes two core
changes to DAPS, utilizing K particles annealed in parallel: a pruning technique to correct parti-
cles trapped in prior-inconsistent likelihood modes in tasks with likelihood ambiguity (Section 4.1),
and a multimodal denoising distribution approximation estimated from the diversity of the particles
(Section 4.2). Additional implementation details and ablations are provided in Appendix A.4, such
as omitting the final Langevin refinement and adaptive ODE stepping from high to low noise to im-
prove reconstruction quality while reducing computational cost (Appendix A.5). The full algorithm
(Algorithm 1) can be found in Appendix A.1.
