Abstract
Individual preferences often become visible
only after deployment, when a user accepts
one decision or revokes it in favor of another.
Jev-style typed interfaces make this feedback
explicit by identifying the selected action and,
under revocation, the decision it replaces. We
study how these consequences persist across
requests as Decision-Level Continual Learn-
ing from Typed Feedback (Jev-DLC). Jev-DLC
treats each correction as both evidence about
a recurring action identity and a directed rela-
tion between the preferred and rejected choices.
It preserves these signals in complementary
bounded channels above a frozen scorer and
combines their scores to shape the next deci-
sion. The method retains no raw interaction
history, adds no scorer call, and performs no
runtime language-model backward pass. We
also introduce TCPA-BENCH, a chronologi-
cal suite that credits a correction only through
its effect on later decisions. On TCPA-Repeat,
Jev-DLC reaches 44.83% user-macro accuracy
overall, 2.43 points above Recent-4 history re-
play. After at least two earlier feedback events,
it reaches 56.07%, a 7.54-point margin over
the strongest reported comparison at this depth.
These results show how explicit typed correc-
tions can become compact causal state for con-
tinual personalization.

We evaluate this formulation with TCPA-

its effect on later decisions. On TCPA-Repeat, the
chronological repeated-action track derived from

44.83% user-macro accuracy on the full stream,
compared with 39.44% for the frozen scorer and
42.40% for Recent-4 history replay. After at least
two earlier feedback events, it reaches 56.07%, a
7.54-point margin over the strongest reported com-
parison at this depth. The results connect the re-
tained correction state to later decision quality un-
der a bounded serving budget. The experiments
also examine feedback depth, changing candidate
items, and alternative fusion strategies to character-
ize where this persistence is useful.
The paper makes three contributions.
• We formulate typed continual preference
alignment as a chronological decision prob-
lem in which accept and revoke feedback can
affect only later decisions for the same user,
and instantiate this contract in TCPA-BENCH.
• We develop Jev-DLC, which preserves recur-
ring action evidence and correction direction
in complementary bounded channels above a
frozen scorer. Our analysis connects typed ac-
tion sufficiency, local minimum-change repair,
and the geometry through which a correction
influences later decisions.
• We show how this compact state improves
repeated-action decisions without raw-history
replay or runtime language-model training.
Comparisons with history replay, user encod-
ing, temporal memory, and meta-adaptation
characterize its accuracy and serving tradeoff
under the same chronological feedback proto-
col.

Related Work
2.1
Personalization from User History
Personalization systems often treat past behavior
as evidence that must be revisited. In-context and
retrieval-based methods place previous interactions
back into the prompt, while other approaches learn
user representations or personalized parameters
(Jang et al., 2023; Ramé et al., 2023; Siththaran-
jan et al., 2023; Lau et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024).
LaMP makes this history-based formulation ex-
plicit across classification and generation tasks
(Salemi et al., 2024); MIND provides a large se-
quential recommendation setting built from news-
click histories (Wu et al., 2020). Recent-K is the
closest system comparison for our setting because it
preserves recent evidence and asks the same frozen
scorer to reinterpret it at every event, following
the history-based personalization protocol used by
LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024). Jev-DLC studies the
setting in which feedback has already exposed the
decision relation and therefore lets that relation per-
sist as a direct influence on future decisions rather
than retaining the full evidence that produced it.
Existing work thus solves how to infer a user’s
preference from past behavior; we study how an ex-
plicit typed correction persists after that inference
has already occurred.
2.2
Continual and Online Adaptation
Continual-learning methods manage the stability–
plasticity trade-off through replay, regularization,
parameter isolation, or dynamic capacity (Kirk-
patrick et al., 2017; Lopez-Paz and Ranzato,
2017; Parisi et al., 2019; De Lange et al., 2022).
Language-model studies apply related ideas to se-
quential tasks, instructions, and knowledge updates
(Jang et al., 2022; Scialom et al., 2022). These
methods make a model, adapter, or shared memory
mutable as new information arrives. Jev-DLC in-
stead freezes the scorer and restricts mutability to a
per-user decision state above it. Replay, subspace,
first-order, and second-order learners remain use-
2


ful comparisons because they represent different
choices about what is allowed to change and what
must be retained. Jev-DLC places the stability–
plasticity trade-off at the level of a per-user deci-
sion state and uses PA-I as its minimum-change
update. Existing work therefore studies how a mu-
table model remains useful across changing tasks;
we study how a frozen scorer can adapt for one
user through a small persistent decision state.
2.3
Typed Decisions and Online Decision
Updates
Jev-style systems expose finite candidate choices
and read a typed decision from the model out-
put (OpenJev contributors, 2026). This interface
makes an accept or revoke an explicit decision-
level correction, but it does not by itself define how
that correction should alter later requests. Pair-
wise preference models provide the correspond-
ing relative-decision language (Bradley and Terry,
1952).
Passive-aggressive learning supplies a
minimum-change online update with an optional
slack cap (Crammer et al., 2006), while adaptive-
gradient and online Newton methods provide al-
ternative update geometries (Duchi et al., 2011;
Shalev-Shwartz, 2012). Jev-DLC uses PA-I to con-
nect an explicit accept/revoke interface to that up-
date under a frozen typed scorer and a chronolog-
ical per-user serving contract, then tests whether
the resulting state changes later decisions. Conver-
sation benchmarks and preference arenas evaluate
broader response quality without making this state
transition the object of evaluation (Zheng et al.,
2023; Chiang et al., 2024). Existing work sep-
arately provides a typed readout or a minimum-
change online update; we connect the two layers
and measure the resulting adaptation trajectory.

Continual preference alignment studies whether
a user’s typed correction can improve later deci-
sions without changing the scorer or replaying the
interaction. We make the information boundary ex-
plicit before describing any particular update rule
or evaluation set.

The central question is whether past typed feedback

whose target has just been revealed. User-macro
accuracy is the primary metric because the unit of
personalization is the user. Other statistical infer-

with the experiments and in Appendix A.6.

5.3
From Local Repair to Persistent Decisions
The mixture in Eq. 11 brings the two persistence
geometries into one decision. The count term car-
ries evidence across events sharing an action iden-
tity, while the correction vectors transfer repair
according to candidate-space alignment. Because
the conservative margin includes the prior and the
responsive margin does not, the same feedback can
produce different updates in the two channels. The
user state preserves both trajectories, and the pre-
decision weights determine their contribution.
Together, action sufficiency and local repair ex-
plain how a correction can be retained without
its original language realization and reused on a
later slate. The experiments measure the resulting
decision quality. Appendix C gives the channel-
weighting analysis; Appendix E.4 reports other
implementations of the same two-channel fusion.
6
TCPA: Chronological Evaluation of
Typed Adaptation
TCPA evaluates whether typed feedback changes
later decisions for the same user. Every track fol-
lows one causal serving contract. The runner pre-
dicts and commits, the evaluator then reveals typed
feedback, the runner updates the user state, and
only subsequent decisions receive the benefit of
that update. This ordering separates continual adap-
tation from refitting the event that revealed its tar-
get.
6.1
Predict-Then-Update Protocol
For each user, events remain in source order and
users are disjoint across training, development, and
test. At event t, the runner sees the current context
and candidate slate, applies the frozen hard mask,
and commits a prediction before the evaluator ex-
6


poses the target or feedback. The evaluator returns
either an acceptance or a revocation with its replace-
ment. The runner then updates the user’s persistent
state. The state is reset when a new user stream
begins, and event t is scored before its feedback
can affect event t + 1 or any later event. Targets
are isolated from the serving process until the com-
mitted prediction has been recorded. Each scored
event exposes the correction prefix available before
commitment. Any measured gain can therefore
be attributed to information genuinely available
online.
6.2
TCPA-Repeat
TCPA-Repeat is our chronological repeated-action
personalization track derived from LaMP-2. It
presents repeated decisions under a stable finite
ontology and preserves chronological user streams
with enough repeated interaction to test whether ac-
cumulated feedback improves later decisions. This
setting makes the central continual-adaptation ques-
tion observable because the corrected action can
reappear after the correction. The converter, label
mapping, filtering rules, hashes, and split construc-
tion are specified in Appendix A.3.
6.3
Additional Diagnostic Tracks
TCPA-Open is a changing-item weak-transfer
boundary track derived from MIND. TCPA-
Dynamics provides controlled stability, shift, re-
currence, and noise diagnostics. Together, these
tracks complement TCPA-Repeat by probing trans-
fer across changing items and controlled temporal
regimes. Their full construction and results appear
in Appendices A.4 and F.
User-macro accuracy is the primary metric. Sys-
tem accounting records persistent state, retained
history, and extra model computation so that accu-
racy and serving cost are evaluated together. Boot-
strap inference and the complete metric definitions
are provided in Appendix A.6; representation anal-
yses appear in Appendix E.

7.1
Later-Decision Accuracy
The comparisons span different ways of carry-
ing user feedback into the next decision. Recent-
4 retains four typed interactions and asks JEV-
SFT to reinterpret them in an enlarged prompt.
PPlug encodes user behavior, TAP-PER queries
bounded temporal memory, and MRM adapts a
low-dimensional head. Our TCPA instantiations of
these three methods share Jev-DLC’s frozen scorer
cache and learned candidate projection. All com-
parisons use the same safe slates, targets, chrono-
logical order, and predict-then-update protocol.
Shared components are fitted on training users and
configurations selected on development users. Ap-
pendix B.5 gives the source method adaptations
and representation provenance of the archived on-
line references, including AdaGrad.
Persistent typed state improves TCPA-Repeat de-
cisions above the frozen scorer. Jev-DLC reaches
44.83% on the full stream, a 5.39-point gain over
Frozen and a 2.43-point higher accuracy than
Recent-4. It has the highest point estimate among
the reported external comparisons on Full and at
each reported feedback depth. After at least two
earlier feedback events, it reaches 56.07%, 7.54
points above the strongest reported comparison at
this depth. Because every prediction precedes its
feedback, these differences measure the use of ear-
lier corrections rather than refitting the decisions
that elicited them.
