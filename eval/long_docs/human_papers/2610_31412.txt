ABSTRACT
Vector quantization (VQ), in particular lattice-based methods, offer strong rate-
distortion trade-offs for low-bit large language models (LLMs), yet their behavior
under quantization-aware training (VQ-QAT) remains poorly understood. We
scale lattice VQ-QAT to billion-parameter LLMs and multi-billion-token training
regimes, and introduce SQUAT, a shape-gain VQ-QAT approach trained with
a separate straight-through estimator (STE) for the shape and gain. SQUAT
decomposes weights into direction and magnitude: normalized directions are
quantized using an E8-derived spherical codebook, while gains are quantized
independently with low-bit scalar quantizers. We show that, unlike a standard
STE applied to the full quantizer, which ignores the shape-gain structure, these
separate STEs induce tangent-space updates for directions, yielding an implicit
form of spherical (Riemannian) optimization. To enable scalability, we develop a
distributed exact nearest-neighbor assignment scheme, in which workers quantize
disjoint weight blocks and communicate only discrete indices, reducing VQ-QAT
training time by 12.8× and allowing us to scale it to 10B tokens. Across instruction-
tuned LLaMA-3.2-1B/3B, Qwen3-4B, and base Qwen3-1.7B, SQUAT consistently
outperforms standard VQ-QAT STE under identical quantizers and initialization,
and achieves SOTA VQ-QAT accuracy.

reduce footprint, while preserving quality (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023).
Most practical LLM quantization pipelines rely on integer scalar quantization. However, recent

To address this, we introduce SQUAT, a Spherical Lattice
Vector Quantization-Aware Training method for billion-
parameter LLMs, that trains shape-gain quantizers with
a factored straight-through estimator (STE), illustrated in
Figure 1. SQUAT represents each weight block using
separate trainable direction and gain parameters. In the
forward pass, the direction parameter is normalized and
quantized with an E8-derived spherical codebook, includ-
ing normalized spherical E8P12 codebook (Tseng et al.,
2024a) or our proposed E8S construction from equal-norm
lattice shells, while the gain is quantized independently
with a low-bit scalar quantizer. In the backward pass, the
STE is applied separately through the direction and gain
quantizers, rather than jointly through the reconstructed
weight. This factored STE induces tangent-space updates
for the direction parameter, yielding spherical optimization of quantized weight directions while
decoupling magnitude optimization into the gain parameter. While spherical parameterizations
themselves are well established in machine learning (e.g., DoRA (Liu et al., 2024a)) and quantization,
prior work has not analyzed how combining such parameterizations with STE-based quantization
alters the surrogate gradients and the resulting optimization dynamics.
To allow practical scaling to billion-parameter models and multi-billion token training, we introduce
distributed exact assignment, where workers quantize disjoint weight blocks against a shared codebook
and communicate only discrete indices.

BACKGROUND
2.1
SCALAR QUANTIZATION
Scalar quantization maps each weight independently to a low-precision discrete set, typically using
uniform integer levels (Jacob et al., 2018). During QAT, quantization is applied in the forward
pass and gradients are propagated with a straight-through estimator (STE). As our scalar INT-QAT
baseline, we use Stretched Elastic Quantization (SEQ) (Liu et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025), which
shifts the signed quantization grid by half a step and improves stability in low-bit regimes.
2.2
VECTOR AND LATTICE QUANTIZATION
Vector quantization (VQ) groups parameters into blocks w ∈Rd and maps each block to its nearest
codeword from a codebook C = {c1, . . . , cK} ⊂Rd:
j⋆= arg
min
j∈{1,...,K} ∥w −cj∥2,
Q(w) = cj⋆.
(1)
Here, j⋆is the discrete codeword index and Q(w) is the reconstructed quantized block. The storage
cost is log2(K)/d bits per weight. By operating in higher dimensions, VQ can exploit the joint
parameter distribution and achieve better rate–distortion trade-offs than scalar quantization.
Explicit learned or unstructured codebooks become infeasible in higher dimensions since the number
of required codewords (and the cost of nearest-neighbor search) grow exponentially in dimension
d. Lattice quantization instead uses implicitly specified, highly structured codebooks with strong
packing properties, retaining good rate–distortion behavior (Gibson & Sayood, 1988; Conway &
Sloane, 2013) while enabling scalable assignment procedures. Yet, VQ-QAT demands repeated
nearest-neighbor (NN) assignments during training, making efficient assignment a central challenge.
2.3

unclear how to use this geometry in QAT, where weights are repeatedly updated through STEs. In
this study, we analyze how STE updates commonly used in VQ interact with shape-gain.
2.4
LATTICE CODEBOOKS FROM E8
The E8 lattice is a highly symmetric lattice in R8 that achieves the densest known sphere packing
in eight dimensions (Conway & Sloane, 2013). Its points are organized into norm shells, where
Sr = {x ∈E8 : ∥x∥2
2 = r}. The first nonzero shells contain 240, 2160, 6720, and 17520 points,
corresponding to the coefficients of the E8 theta series. Using a single shell, or a union of shells, as a
codebook yields a spherical code where nearest-neighbor assignment depends on angular alignment.
Since E8 is infinite, finite low-bit codebooks require truncation. We use them either in Euclidean form,
retaining lattice norms, or normalized form, projecting codewords to the unit sphere for shape-gain
(SG) quantization. In our notation, non-SG variants use Euclidean form, while SG variants use the
normalized spherical form.
We study two E8-derived constructions. First, QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) introduces E8P12, a
structured 2-bit-per-weight vector quantizer with 216 possible codewords per 8D block. E8P12
is derived from an E8-related shifted ˆD8 construction and is designed for compact decoding: its
codewords are represented using a small base table together with parity, sign, and shift operations.
Second, we introduce E8S, a spherical E8 truncation that enumerates points from low-norm E8 shells,
optionally subsamples the final shell to match fractional bit budgets, and normalizes the selected
points to form a spherical code. See App. A for details.

SQUAT: SPHERICAL LATTICE VQ-QAT
Normally, VQ-QAT maintains full-precision shadow parameters in Euclidean space while emulating
low-precision inference by applying quantization only in the forward pass. For vector quantizers,
the forward map is typically discrete and thus non-differentiable; gradient-based training therefore
requires an explicit surrogate for backpropagation. The most common choice is the straight-through
estimator (STE). In shape-gain vector quantization, however, STE can be inserted at multiple plausible
locations in the computation graph, and these choices lead to different surrogate gradients and,
consequently, different optimization dynamics.
In this work, we study how STE choices affect optimization for shape-gain vector quantization. We
analyze two natural cases: in Sec. 3.1 we consider ‘Standard VQ-QAT:’ the standard joint STE applied
to the overall vector quantizer, and in Sec. 3.2 we consider our ‘Spherical VQ-QAT’ in which we
apply the STE separately to the shape and gain quantizers, coupled with a spherical parameterization
of the weights. We show that these choices induce distinct surrogate gradients and therefore different
effective updates on the implied Euclidean weight vector. Moreover, we interpret the resulting update
of Spherical VQ-QAT as a hyperspherical (Riemannian) update: a tangent-space step for the direction
combined with a radial update for the gain, where normalization acts as a retraction. We adopt this
update as our training rule, denoted SQUAT, and validate its benefit empirically. We emphasize that
SQUAT is a training rule agnostic to the specific spherical codebook used.

Geometric interpretation of SQUAT.
Spherical VQ-QAT (SQUAT) optimizes explicit shape-gain

one-step update for plain SGD is given in (12); we interpret this update through its geometry.

normalization yields the projector Ps = I −ss⊤, where s = u/(∥u∥2 + ε) is the current normalized
(unquantized) direction. Since ∂L/∂s ≈ˆγ g, the update direction on u is proportional to Ps(∂L/∂s),

step in u, the renormalization s+ = u+/(∥u+∥2 + ε) maps back to Sd−1 and acts as a retraction.

Euclidean step on the gain γ. This interpretation is exact for the one-step SGD analysis and remains
a useful description of the induced direction update under common optimizers.

Alternative parameterizations and STE assumptions.
While Secs. 3.1–3.2 focus on the two
primary regimes, the Euclidean shadow weights with a joint STE (Standard VQ-QAT) and a shape-
gain parameterization with factored STE (Spherical VQ-QAT, or SQUAT), other parameterization and
STE choices are possible. In App. B.1, we isolate the effect of the parameterization by using spherical
shadow variables (u, γ) but retaining a joint STE; this yields a tangential–radial update similar in
form to SQUAT, but driven by the unquantized (s, γ) rather than the quantized forward quantities
(ˆs, ˆγ). Conversely, in App. B.2 we isolate the effect of the STE placement by keeping Euclidean
shadow weights w while applying a factored (shape-gain) STE, which induces a direction–magnitude
decomposition of the Euclidean update without an explicit retraction step. Finally, App. B.3 shows
an implementation trick that recovers the exact Standard VQ-QAT update while storing parameters
in a factored form. Together, these variants clarify that both the parameterization and the STE
factorization materially shape the effective optimization dynamics.

Exact nearest-neighbor (NN) search is the main compu-
tational bottleneck in VQ-QAT. With N weight blocks
and a d-dimensional codebook of size K ≈2d·bpw, brute-
force search costs O(NKd) per quantization step. Unlike
PTQ, where search is performed once or a few times,
QAT repeats assignment on every forward pass to simulate
inference-time quantization. In our implementation, each
data-parallel worker computes exact NN (13) for a disjoint
subset of vectors using a shared codebook C, and only the
resulting integer indices are synchronized via all-gather.
Furthermore, a new Triton kernel fuses normalization,
codeword selection, and reconstruction. We also cache
quantized weights across gradient-accumulation steps, so
assignment is invoked only once per optimizer step. For
E8S on LLaMA-3.2-1B, these optimizations reduce train-
ing time from 62.5 s/it for vanilla VQ-QAT to 4.9 s/it,
yielding a 12.8× speedup while preserving the exact nearest-neighbor assignment, see Figure 3.
Overall, our lattice VQ-QAT implementation incurs only moderate overhead relative to scalar QAT.

and 10B tokens, SQUAT reduces WikiText2 perplexity from 17.90 to 13.74 on LLaMA-3.2-1B, from
15.00 to 10.83 on LLaMA-3.2-3B, and from 12.52 to 9.90 on Qwen3-4B. Downstream gains are also
substantial; for example, on Qwen3-4B, MMLU improves from 54.2 after PTQ to 69.5 after QAT.
Compared with scalar INT-QAT, SQUAT performs favorably overall. At 2.25 bpw and 10B training
tokens, it matches or surpasses INT-QAT on nearly all evaluated models and metrics, with Qwen3-4B
CSR as the only exception. At 2.0 bpw, the two methods are broadly comparable, although SQUAT
tends to perform better at lower training budgets (< 5B tokens), while INT-QAT often gains an
advantage with more extensive training.
