Abstract
Agentic reinforcement learning rewards a complete episode, yet the policy needs
credit for every decision inside it. Counterfactual branching estimates this credit
by re-running the episode after an alternative decision, but the branch draws its
sampling noise independently of the factual episode, so the outcome difference
mixes the effect of the decision with the luck of everything that follows. We observe
that in agentic search the retriever, the corpus, and the grader are deterministic, so
an episode is a deterministic function of the policy’s decisions and its sampling
noise. Twin worlds are therefore realizable: the same noise, a different decision.
We introduce TWICE (TWIn-world Credit Estimation), which keys the Gumbel
noise of every token by turn and line, rolls out counterfactual twins that inherit the
factual episode’s downstream noise, and turns their paired outcome differences into
turn-level advantages for policy-gradient updates on actor-sampled trajectories;
only the actor is deployed. Twin credit is unbiased for any coupling quality, its
variance is that of an independent counterfactual scaled by 1 −ρ, where ρ is the
twins’ outcome correlation, and under coupling the number of rollouts needed to
resolve an advantage of size ∆falls from Θ(∆−2) to Θ(∆−1). A square-root rule
spends the twin budget where outcomes are predicted to disagree per unit cost. On
seven question-answering benchmarks, TWICE improves the average exact match
of GRPO by 5.3 and 4.5 points on Qwen2.5-3B and 7B, outperforms tree-search
and counterfactual-branching baselines, and uses 68% fewer auxiliary tokens than
VinePPO.

Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) has turned language models into search agents that interleave reasoning
with retrieval calls and are rewarded only for the correctness of their final answer (Jin et al., 2025;
Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025a). The reward says whether
an episode went well; it does not say which decision—a query, the choice to search again, or the
final answer—was responsible. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) broadcasts one scalar to every token
of the episode, and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) relies on a critic that must learn values of long,
retrieval-laden contexts; either way, a good query in a failed episode is punished and a wasted query
in a lucky one is rewarded (Wei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a).
A growing literature assigns finer credit to agent decisions. Critics are hard to train on language
states (Kazemnejad et al., 2025), and hierarchical critics add their own machinery (Zhou et al.,
2024a); process rewards come from information gain, judges, or annotated sub-goals (Wang et al.,
2026a; Zheng et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a); identical states
can be grouped across rollouts (Feng et al., 2025); and tree rollouts compare siblings that share a

This choice has a cost that is easy to miss. Would this episode have succeeded had the agent
issued a different query? is a counterfactual question about the realized episode (Pearl, 2009). An
independently sampled branch answers an interventional question instead: the outcome difference
contains the effect of the decision plus the difference in luck of every later sampling step—which
follow-up query was drawn, whether the agent stopped early, how the answer was phrased. The
factual episode’s own luck never averages out, so estimators that use the realized return have a
variance floor that no number of extra rollouts removes (Figure 1a), and resolving an effect of size ∆
takes Θ(∆−2) rollouts (Figure 1c).
We observe that agentic search admits a better answer. The retriever, the corpus, and the grader are
deterministic, so once sampling is written with the Gumbel-max trick (Maddison et al., 2014; Oberst
and Sontag, 2019), an episode is a deterministic function of the policy’s decisions and its exogenous
noise. The factual episode’s twin world (Balke and Pearl, 1994)—the same noise, a different decision
at one turn—can then be rolled out exactly. Downstream luck is shared, so the twin’s outcome
difference isolates the decision, as in counterfactual generation from language models (Ravfogel
et al., 2025; Chatzi et al., 2025) and common random numbers in simulation (Glasserman and Yao,
1992; Ng and Jordan, 2000). Coupling is also strongest exactly where credit is hardest: a decision
that barely changes the downstream distribution yields a twin that reproduces the factual continuation
and ties, instead of a noisy ±1.
We build TWICE on this observation. It (i) keys the Gumbel noise of each token by episode, turn,
line, and position, so twins stay aligned when lengths differ and every trajectory remains an exact
sample from the policy; (ii) turns the paired outcome difference of a factual decision and its twin
into a turn-level advantage for PPO-style updates on actor-sampled trajectories; and (iii) branches
each decision with a probability proportional to

Related Work
Credit assignment for LLM agents.
Outcome-reward RL for agents builds on PPO, GRPO, and
their variants (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Ahmadian et al., 2024). Finer
credit comes from learned or hindsight critics (Zhou et al., 2024a, 2025; Tan et al., 2026; Wang
et al., 2026c), state grouping (Feng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026d; Zhu et al., 2026a), environment
feedback (Huo et al., 2026), and process rewards from information gain, judges, or verifiers (Wang
et al., 2026a; Liang et al., 2026a; Samarinas et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026b; Setlur
et al., 2025). Tree methods share prefixes and compare siblings (Hou et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Ji
et al., 2026; Zong et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Fu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2025a); SIPO notes
that adaptive expansion biases sibling values (Fu et al., 2026). Monte Carlo methods estimate values
from rollouts at intermediate states (Kazemnejad et al., 2025), resets resample continuations from
a decision (Samanta et al., 2026), error localization searches for the first irrecoverable step (Liang
et al., 2026b), and CRR forks the environment and contrasts the factual return with one sampled
counterfactual (Li et al., 2026). TWICE keeps this last, most direct estimator but changes what the
branch holds fixed: its downstream noise. Related uses of the same tree structure allocate a fixed
rollout budget toward prompt roots and turn-level prefixes that are likely to yield mixed rewards (Zou
et al., 2026a), or back the terminal reward up those prefixes and distill the resulting turn-level credit
into a bounded teacher (Zou et al., 2026b).
Counterfactuals and shared randomness.
Twin networks answer counterfactual queries by
sharing exogenous noise between factual and hypothetical worlds (Balke and Pearl, 1994; Pearl,
2009). Gumbel-max structural causal models make this concrete for categorical sampling (Oberst
and Sontag, 2019) and for language models (Ravfogel et al., 2025; Chatzi et al., 2025). In RL,
counterfactual reasoning appears in model-based policy search (Buesing et al., 2019), hindsight and
counterfactual credit (Harutyunyan et al., 2019; Mesnard et al., 2021; Meulemans et al., 2023), and
multi-agent baselines (Foerster et al., 2018); fixing random numbers to compare policies dates to
PEGASUS (Ng and Jordan, 2000) and common random numbers in simulation (Glasserman and
Yao, 1992). Shared-noise coupling of language-model samplers is also used for speculative decoding
and watermarking (Daliri et al., 2025; Kirchenbauer et al., 2023; Kuditipudi et al., 2024). To our
knowledge, TWICE is the first to use it for credit assignment in LLM training, where it changes both
the variance and the sample complexity of counterfactual credit.
RL for search agents.
Search-R1 and its successors train agents to call a search tool inside their
reasoning (Jin et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025;
Gao et al., 2025), refine rewards for efficiency and process quality (Wang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al.,
2025b; Wu et al., 2026), and extend to real web environments and large reasoning models (Zheng
et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025a). TWICE changes only how credit is estimated and applies to any of
these pipelines whose tools are deterministic or cached. Appendix A extends this discussion.

Preliminaries
Agentic episodes.
A question x starts an episode. At turn t, the policy πθ writes a turn yt ∼
πθ(· | st)—reasoning followed by one action, a search call or the final answer—given the context
st; a tool returns ot = E(yt), and st+1 = st ⊕yt ⊕ot. After at most H turns, a grader returns
a utility U ∈[0, 1] (exact match in our experiments). The retriever over a fixed corpus and the
grader are deterministic; web tools become deterministic within a training step by caching. We write
Qπ(st, y) = E[U | st, yt=y], V π(st) = Ey∼πQπ(st, y), and Aπ = Qπ −V π; the credit of a turn is
Aπ(st, yt).
Counterfactual branching.
Given a visited decision, a branch samples y′ ∼π(· | st), executes it,
and continues with π to reach U ′. The difference Dt = U −U ′ satisfies E[Dt | st, yt] = Aπ(st, yt)
whenever the continuation is a π-rollout, and this is the estimator of CRR (Li et al., 2026); VinePPO
instead estimates V π(st+1) −V π(st) with independent rollouts from both states (Kazemnejad et al.,
2025). Writing U = Qπ(st, yt) + ε, where ε is the luck of the factual continuation, any estimator
that uses the realized U inherits Var(ε), and estimators that avoid it pay for a second set of rollouts.

TWICE
4.1
Twin worlds from keyed noise
With Gumbel-max sampling, token i of turn t is arg maxv [ℓθ(v | ·)/τ +ξ(v)], where ℓθ are the logits,
τ the temperature, and ξ a vector of i.i.d. standard Gumbel variables; top-p restricts the arg max
to the nucleus. With deterministic tools, U = F(x; ξ1, . . . , ξH) is a deterministic function of the
per-turn noise ξt (Oberst and Sontag, 2019; Ravfogel et al., 2025). The twin of the factual episode
at turn t with alternative y′ evaluates F with turn t replaced by y′ and every later noise variable
unchanged (Balke and Pearl, 1994). This needs two properties: every trajectory must remain an exact
sample from π, and the twin must read the same noise as the factual episode for the same role.
TWICE indexes noise by a key κ = (e, t, ℓ, j)—episode e, turn t, line ℓof the turn, and position j
within the line—and sets ξκ(v) = −log(−log uκ(v)) with uκ from a counter-based pseudorandom
function seeded by κ. Keys never repeat within a trajectory, so every factual or twin trajectory samples
each token with a fresh Gumbel vector and is an exact draw from π (Appendix B.1). Keys are indexed
by turn and line rather than by a global token counter, so a twin whose turn t has a different length
still reads the factual noise at every later turn, and a divergence inside a line re-synchronizes at the
next line. A single sampler seed per episode, as exposed by serving engines (Kwon et al., 2023), is
also exact but aligns noise by token count, so a single length change decouples the rest of the episode.
Watermark-style n-gram keys (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023) re-synchronize faster but can repeat within
a trajectory, which breaks exactness. Keyed sampling is a logits processor that adds ξκ and takes the
arg max; it adds about 2% to generation time.

6.1
Setup
Data, models, and training.
We follow the Search-R1 protocol (Jin et al., 2025): training on the
union of NQ and HotpotQA training sets, retrieval of the top-3 passages by E5 (Wang et al., 2022)
from the 2018 Wikipedia dump (Karpukhin et al., 2020), at most four search calls, and exact match as
the reward. We evaluate exact match on NQ, TriviaQA, and PopQA (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Joshi
et al., 2017; Mallen et al., 2023) and on the multi-hop HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue, and
Bamboogle (Yang et al., 2018; Ho et al., 2020; Trivedi et al., 2022; Press et al., 2023). Backbones are
Qwen2.5-3B/7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2024); Appendix F adds Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025) and
