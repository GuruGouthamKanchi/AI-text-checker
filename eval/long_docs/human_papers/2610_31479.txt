Abstract
Apple Silicon is among the most widely used platforms for local LLM inference,
yet nearly all inference optimizations are designed and validated on server-class
NVIDIA GPUs under batched serving workloads. Which optimizations retain their
benefits when moved to Apple Silicon, and what determines their applicability?
We evaluate four optimization families: KV-cache pruning, speculative decod-
ing, ported GPU kernels, and post-training quantization. Experiments span four
Apple machines under a common single-request decode workload, with matched
RTX 4090 comparisons where possible. KV-cache pruning can reach up to 1.75×
end-to-end throughput on Apple but can reduce throughput on desktop NVIDIA
GPUs. Under the common benchmark, all evaluated speculative decoding meth-
ods reduce throughput on Apple, despite reported 2–6× gains on server GPUs.
CUDA kernel designs transfer only partially: public Metal lacks asynchronous
copy, and batch-one decode makes several serving-oriented mechanisms inappli-
cable, although transferable components yield up to 1.20× decode throughput.
Quantization gains vary by machine. Across four families, a reported speedup
is not a portable property of a technique alone but a joint function of technique,
runtime, hardware contract, and workload regime.
1
Introduction
Local LLM inference increasingly happens on Apple machines: llama.cpp treats M-series chips
as a first-class target [1], Apple ships MLX for unified-memory inference [2], and recent studies
describe M-series unified memory as the practical consumer path to large open-weight models [3].
The optimizations that would make this inference fast, however, carry no evidence for it: none
of the methods we study — KV-cache management [4], speculative decoding [5], the FlashAtten-
tion/FlashInfer, Marlin, and MegaBlocks kernels [6; 7; 8; 9], and post-training quantization [10] —
reports a measurement on Apple hardware in its original publication.
An optimization that reduces memory traffic or computation may still fail to improve end-to-end
throughput if it targets a secondary cost, adds runtime overhead, or relies on hardware features or
workload conditions that are absent from local inference. This motivates our central question: Which
LLM inference optimizations remain effective on Apple Silicon, and why?
Our experiments focus on the single-request, unified-memory regime that exists at consumer scale,
and we evaluate the bundle users actually deploy: hardware, runtime, and workload together. We
cover four Apple devices across three GPU generations, dense and hybrid architectures, and matched
RTX 4090 controls where configurations can be aligned.

Prior Apple-Silicon work has mainly evaluated models or complete software stacks. MLX provides a
framework for unified-memory inference [2], OpenELM reports local generation with MLX [11], and
Rajesh et al. [12] compare several runtimes on an M2 Ultra. That study establishes performance trade-
offs among Apple-Silicon devices. In contrast, we isolate individual optimizations, port representative
CUDA mechanisms to Metal/MLX, and measure their kernel-level and end-to-end effects.
Contributions. (1) A controlled comparison of four optimization families for local inference on
Apple Silicon. (2) Metal/MLX ports of representative CUDA attention, quantized-matmul, and MoE
mechanisms, evaluated at both kernel and end-to-end levels. (3) A cross-platform analysis explaining
when gains persist, disappear, or reverse because of hardware, runtime, and workload differences.

2.1
KV Cache
KV caching reduces autoregressive decoding cost by retaining the keys and values computed for
preceding tokens. This avoids repeated computation but introduces memory-footprint and bandwidth
costs that increase with context length [22]. We characterize cache growth and evaluate the latency
and memory effects of persistent prefix reuse and post-prefill pruning. Evaluating pruning-induced
changes in output quality is outside our scope; these effects are expected to be comparable across
platforms using identical pruning algorithms and configurations.
2.1.1
Experimental setup
Cache footprints are derived from the released configurations of mlx-community/Qwen3-4B-4bit,
mlx-community/gemma-3-4b-it-4bit, mlx-community/granite-4.0-h-micro-4bit, and
LiquidAI/LFM2.5-2.6B, together with the MLX-LM cache layout [13]. Cached tensors are as-
sumed to use bfloat16, independently of checkpoint weight precision. Pruning experiments use
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and LFM2.5-2.6B in bfloat16 through Transformers and KVPress [23; 14].

three Apple devices. The matched workloads use context lengths of 512 and 4,096 tokens and output
lengths of 1, 16, 128, and 256 tokens at batch size one.

2.1.4
Hybrid-model pruning and peak memory
The smaller amount of prunable attention state in LFM2.5-2.6B is accompanied by smaller latency
gains (Figure 4). At a 512-token context, every method remains between 0.92× and 1.03× across the
three Apple devices. At a 4,096-token context and 256-token output, speedups range from 1.17–1.25×
on M2 Pro, 1.09–1.13× on M3 Ultra, and 1.11–1.18× on M4 Pro, with ExpectedAttention highest on
each device. The maximum is thus 1.25×, compared with 1.75× for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. The
output-length dependence is qualitatively similar to Qwen, but attention-only pruning leaves LFM’s
22 short-convolution blocks and their state unchanged, limiting the inference cost it can remove.
Process-level peak memory also depends on temporary allocations rather than retained-cache size
alone (Figure 5). On M4 Pro at a 4,096-token context, Qwen peak-memory savings range from 20.4%
to 25.6% across the reported methods and outputs. LFM2.5-2.6B shows no material reduction for
outputs of 1 or 16 tokens, followed by savings of 8.2–8.3% at 128 tokens and 21.1% at 256 tokens. At
a 512-token context, pruning instead increases peak memory by up to 155.5% for Qwen and 48.5% for
LFM. These increases are consistent with transient scoring and compaction allocations outweighing
the smaller persistent cache. Retained KV state, process-level peak memory, and end-to-end latency
therefore measure distinct effects of pruning.
Persistent prefix reuse yields consistent latency reductions by eliminating redundant prefill without
token-selection overhead. Pruning gains depend on context and output length, device, and the fraction
of inference cost attributable to attention KV state. In the matched Qwen workloads, the largest gains
occur on Apple Silicon, while the RTX 4090 remains close to baseline or slows down; FastKVzip
slows execution on both platforms. Neither compression ratio nor processor generation alone predicts
end-to-end benefit, and reduced KV state does not ensure lower process-level peak memory.

2.2
Speculative Decoding
Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by separating token proposal from ver-
ification [5]. A cheaper drafting procedure proposes a sequence of candidate tokens, which the
target model evaluates in a single forward pass. Verification retains the accepted prefix and produces
the next target token, allowing several output tokens to be generated per target-model invocation.
Research on speculative decoding has developed this principle by reducing drafting cost, improving
the agreement between draft and target predictions, and increasing the parallelism of token proposal.
The classical approach, represented by Classic in our evaluation, uses a separate, smaller autoregres-
sive language model to generate the draft sequence. The target then verifies these proposals together,
without requiring retraining. The original method reports 2–3× acceleration for T5-XXL relative to
standard T5X inference [5]; a later greedy-decoding evaluation reports 1.93× on MT-Bench with
Vicuna-13B [18]. Although the auxiliary model is smaller, its sequential drafting steps still contribute
to generation latency.
One way to reduce this cost is to replace neural drafting with inexpensive statistical lookup. N-
gram drafting retrieves stored continuations of a matching token suffix, avoiding a neural forward
pass. Such a procedure can also assist a neural drafter: Cascade Speculative Drafting recursively
accelerates each drafter with a cheaper one, terminating at the statistical Max-Gram model [17]. Our
Cascade-n-gram evaluates this vertical cascade using N-gram proposals for a small neural drafter.
The full published method additionally assigns cheaper drafters to later positions and achieves 1.70×
speedup for Vicuna-7B on GSM8K (56.72 versus 33.34 tokens/s) [17].
A complementary direction improves draft quality by using features already computed by the target
model. The EAGLE family introduces lightweight learned drafting modules conditioned on these
features. EAGLE-3 predicts tokens from fused low-, middle-, and high-layer representations, with
a training-time test that feeds back the drafter’s own outputs to improve successive predictions;
inference can use a dynamic candidate tree. Under greedy decoding, it reports 5.58× and 4.40× on
MT-Bench with Vicuna-13B and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, respectively, and 6.47× on HumanEval
with Vicuna-13B [18].
Feature conditioning improves proposal quality, but autoregressive drafting still requires sequential
forward passes. More recent work therefore explores generating draft tokens in parallel. DFlash
combines target-feature conditioning with a block diffusion drafter that predicts masked positions
together in one pass, reporting 2.85× on MT-Bench and 6.09× on MATH-500 with Qwen3-4B [19].
DDTree extends this approach by constructing a candidate tree from the same per-position distri-
butions under a node budget, maximizing a surrogate for expected acceptance length before joint
verification. In its separate evaluation, DDTree improves DFlash’s MT-Bench speedup from 2.64×
to 4.18×, and its MATH-500 speedup from 5.54× to 7.50× [20]. These published gains motivate
our evaluation, although their models, workloads, and runtimes differ from ours; the learned and
block-drafting studies primarily use server-class NVIDIA GPUs and 16-bit targets.
6
