ABSTRACT
Understanding and controlling how systems evolve is central to science and
engineering, where many problems require reasoning about complete trajectories.
Existing approaches typically learn local dynamics or generate trajectories, but
objectives constructed from individual transitions can miss dependencies across
time, while generative models do not necessarily provide a tractable objective for
evaluating and optimizing arbitrary paths. We study action learning, the problem
of recovering a stochastic action from trajectory observations, yielding a common
objective for reasoning about dynamical systems. We introduce LEAP (Learned
Energy on Action Paths), which anchors the unknown action to a tractable refer-
ence process and learns the remaining correction by comparing observed trajecto-
ries with reference trajectories. This construction yields a differentiable objective
on path space that can capture both local dynamical structure and nonadditive
dependencies across time. Once learned, the same action can be flexibly reused
for path ranking and inference, mechanism steering, constrained optimization
and sampling. Across stochastic physical systems, many-body orbital dynamics
and molecular simulations, LEAP recovers underlying dynamics, captures depen-
dencies across time and enables transfer across inference objectives and physical
conditions. We show that action learning is a promising framework for turning
trajectory observations into reusable objectives for scientific inference and control.

Understanding how systems move between states requires reasoning about the trajectories that
connect them. In molecular dynamics, two conformations may be connected by several transition
routes that visit different intermediate configurations (Bolhuis et al., 2002; Shaw et al., 2010); in
robotics, agents act through sequences of decisions (Kalakrishnan et al., 2011; Janner et al., 2022).
In these settings, the object of interest is often the trajectory itself: which paths realize a transition,
which mechanisms are plausible and which paths remain likely under perturbations or constraints.
Answering these questions requires reasoning about complete paths. To infer representative tran-
sitions or impose new preferences, we need an objective that assigns probabilities or costs to
trajectories. When the dynamics are known, stochastic action functionals provide such an objective.

In statistical physics and stochastic control, actions such as the Onsager–Machlup functional,
Freidlin–Wentzell rate functional and path integral control costs define probabilities or costs on path
space (Onsager & Machlup, 1953; Freidlin & Wentzell, 2012; Kappen, 2005; Adib, 2007). Once
the action is available, we can use it for downstream tasks like constrained inference and sampling.
In many applications, however, we do not know the action. Instead, we observe trajectories from
simulations or experiments, such as molecular dynamics, sequences of state transitions, policy
rollouts or logs of stochastic processes (Lindorff-Larsen et al., 2011; Shaw et al., 2010; Glielmo
et al., 2021). Classical trajectory inference methods typically assume a known action, while learned
methods predict local dynamics, learn representations or generate trajectories directly (Chen et al.,
2019; Li et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021; Jing et al., 2024b). These objects are useful, but they do not
directly give the reusable path objective needed for downstream trajectory inference. We therefore
call the task of recovering an evaluable action from trajectory samples action learning.
Existing learned methods often model dynamics locally by fitting one-step transition laws and using
the resulting local model to evaluate complete trajectories (Chua et al., 2018). This requirement
can become restrictive when trajectories are selected by global events, e.g., successful transitions or
task-level constraints. A local predictor trained on such data estimates the one-step conditionals of
the selected process, which can differ from the underlying dynamics. Errors in local transition log-
likelihoods can also accumulate along a path and then distort the induced optimization landscape.
Since path-level quantities do not have to decompose into independent local decisions, optimizing
locally plausible segments can still produce a poor local minimum; Fig. 1 shows this distinction.
We propose LEAP (Learned Energy on Action Paths), a framework for learning a stochastic action
directly from trajectory samples. LEAP treats the action itself as the learning target, rather than a
local predictor or trajectory generator. It anchors the unknown action to a tractable reference process
and learns the remaining correction from complete trajectories, yielding an evaluable objective on
path space. In structured settings, this correction recovers changes in the underlying dynamics; more
generally, it can capture nonadditive interactions across transitions. Once learned, the action can be
reused with preferences that were not specified during training. For example, a reward for visiting
an intermediate basin can favor a different transition route, while a geometric penalty can discourage
paths outside admissible regions, in both cases without retraining the learned action.
Our approach applies broadly to trajectories from dynamical systems, including molecular simula-
tions, stochastic physical systems and logged sequential processes (Shaw et al., 2010; Kalakrishnan
et al., 2011; Janner et al., 2022). We evaluate LEAP across controlled physical systems, a 31-body
orbital benchmark with 186-dimensional states and an alanine dipeptide molecular benchmarks.
These experiments test physical recovery, nonadditive dependence across time and reuse across
inference objectives, with the double well and orbital systems providing exact actions for evaluation.
Our contributions are as follows: (i) we formulate action learning as learning a reusable objec-
tive directly on complete trajectories; (ii) we introduce LEAP, which learns a stochastic action by
comparing observed trajectories with samples from a tractable reference process; (iii) we establish
theoretical guarantees for recovering the action and show that dependencies across time can result
in trajectory objectives that cannot be represented by sums of local transition costs; and (iv) we
demonstrate recovery of underlying physical dynamics from terminally selected trajectories, mod-
eling of nonadditive dependencies in many-body systems, and reuse of a fixed learned action across
molecular inference tasks and physical conditions.

RELATED WORK
Sampling trajectories from known dynamics. Classical methods for sampling trajectories usually
assume access to the dynamics, energy landscape or action. When trajectories of interest occur of-
ten, it is sufficient to just do direct simulation. When relevant paths are rare, e.g., because an energy
barrier separates metastable states, methods can use known physical models to identify or sam-
ple transition pathways. The string method evolves discretized curves on an energy landscape (E
et al., 2002), while minimum action methods optimize action functionals derived from stochastic
dynamics (Freidlin & Wentzell, 2012). Stochastic control methods construct trajectory objectives
from known dynamics and costs to sample rare transitions (Kappen, 2005; Todorov, 2009; Williams
et al., 2017; Holdijk et al., 2023; Du et al., 2024). More recent methods use learned force fields or
pretrained generative models to get energies or dynamics for transition inference (Yuan et al., 2024;

Learning from trajectories. Learned trajectory models usually target evolution or generation.
Neural differential equation and latent dynamics models learn evolution from observations (Chen

and action matching methods learn vector fields or scores that transport or generate samples from

object: a stochastic action on complete paths, i.e., the trajectory-level objective used directly for
optimization, control and sampling, which can then be reused across new downstream objectives.
2
LEARNING ACTION FUNCTIONALS FROM TRAJECTORY SAMPLES
LEAP learns a differentiable stochastic action Sθ(Φ; c) directly from trajectory samples, providing a
reusable objective for evaluating and optimizing trajectories. The action assigns a scalar objective to
each complete trajectory, with lower action corresponding to higher probability under the observed
path distribution. LEAP requires trajectory observations but not the underlying drift, potential or
simulator likelihood. We construct the action by introducing a tractable reference process and learn-
ing a correction that describes how the observed path distribution differs from the reference. This
formulation can represent both local dynamical structure and dependencies across time, yielding an
objective that can be evaluated and reused for new inference tasks. See Alg. 1 for the algorithm.
2.1

2.2
REFERENCE PROCESS
The reference defines the baseline path measure and the inductive bias of the learned action. This
means we need a tractable path density, efficient sampling and sufficient probability mass over the
trajectories represented in the data. While its drift can include an available nominal drift or force
model, LEAP does not necessarily require one. For rare-event trajectories, we condition the refer-
ence on the same event as the data so that both contain trajectories selected by the same condition.
We use a discrete-time Gaussian Markov reference because its transition law is analytic and param-
eterized directly by drift and diffusion. Under shared diffusion, differences in drift induce an exact
path log-likelihood ratio, giving the learned correction a direct dynamical interpretation. We define

WHY LEAP RECOVERS AN ACTION

objective, the second identifies the drift correction under the structured model, and the third bounds
downstream inference error from action error. Full assumptions and proofs are in §A.

erence distributions have positive densities on the same support, the candidates are conditionally
independent and the score class contains G∗. Every population minimizer of Eq. (4) satisfies

pref(Φ | c) + a(c).

Sθ∗(Φ; c) = S∗(Φ; c) + β(c).

EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments evaluate four properties of action learning.
We show that LEAP can recover
physical dynamics from selected trajectories, capture dependencies across multiple transitions,
improve as the reference moves closer to the data and reuse the learned action under new physical
conditions and inference requests. See §B for the full experimental details.
4.1
RECOVERING DYNAMICS UNDER TERMINAL SELECTION

their final state. We use the overdamped diffusion dXt = b(Xt) dt +

binary and categorical neural ratio estimation (NRE) objectives (Miller et al., 2024). Without them,
binary NRE gives NRMSE 1.00000 ± 3 × 10−5. Adding them reduces error to 0.175 ± 0.011
with binary NRE and 0.228 ± 0.015 with categorical NRE (Table 3). Both objectives improve,
showing that the accumulated information from Eq. (23) drives the decrease. Because both are ratio
estimators on complete paths, the binary result also shows that action learning is not tied exactly to
the categorical objective in Eq. (4).
Scaling. At 42 bodies and 256 transitions, each path has 64,764 coordinates. LEAP-Global reaches
NRMSE 0.111, while LEAP-Local stays at 1.0015. Thus, even as the system grows to more bodies
and longer trajectories, LEAP-Global can accumulate the small effect of the hidden mass perturba-
tion across transitions, while models that treat each transition separately still stay near the constant
prediction baseline. See §E.4 for the full sweep.
