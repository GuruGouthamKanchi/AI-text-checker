ABSTRACT
Given an unseen graph, a start node, s, and an end node, t, can a language model
generate the shortest path from s to t? Prior work has found that models fail
to learn this task as the graphs scale. We show that this failure has two causes:
the supervision signal and the tokenization. First, we theoretically establish and
empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of multi-token prediction (MTP), and
show that the benefits of MTP are topologically agnostic. Second, we introduce
alternative tokenizations and show that the two causes are orthogonal. Using these
methods, we break through the scaling wall observed by prior work, consistently
and stably learning to solve the task at scales far beyond previous limits.

INTRODUCTION
Can a language model learn to search? A direct way to answer this is to test whether the model can
recover a shortest path in an unseen graph from a symbolic description alone. Here, success would
not measure world knowledge or memorization, but whether the model can carry out a multihop
computation over structure it has not seen before. Our purpose is not to benchmark models but to
understand why the task is hard to learn despite being within the capacity of small transformers. Prior
work has studied learnable search in controlled settings as a model for general planning and multihop
reasoning (Bachmann & Nagarajan, 2024; Saparov et al., 2025; Cohen et al., 2025), outlined below.
A multihop reasoning task is a tuple (W, q, a) where W is a world model (a set of facts, axioms,
etc.), q is a query, and a is a target answer. The task is solved by a sequence of k ≥1 inferential
steps that connect q to a, each licensed by some relation or rule derivable from W. Importantly, W
induces a latent reasoning graph, G = (N, E), where nodes are reasoning states and edges are viable
inference steps. The task solution is a path π = qu1 . . . uk−1a. When k > 1, a cannot be directly
inferred from q in a single step, but must be found by discovering a path π in G which connects q to a.
This is graph search; the difficulty of which is characterized by the topology of G, with the branching
factor and the path length k determining the size of the search space. As the reasoning graph is latent,
its topology is unknown, and we can not make topological assumptions. Crucially, search as a process
requires no prior knowledge of G’s topology: it discovers π by traversal alone. This makes graph
search a natural theoretical model for multihop reasoning as it captures the essential structural and
compositional difficulty without presupposing topological properties (unstated in prior work;
C).
Studying multihop reasoning using natural language data with LLMs introduces confounds that
obscure whether a model is genuinely traversing a reasoning graph, as it may produce the ‘answer’
via memorized semantic shortcuts rather than structural inference; shortcuts which come from co-
occurrences in the inaccessible training data (Ju et al., 2024; Balesni et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b).

Indeed, LLMs often ignore relational structure necessary for multihop reasoning in favour of semantic
shortcuts, even when it is explicitly given (Huang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025).
These confounds motivate the use of a highly controlled environment to study multihop reasoning via
graph search, which we inherit (Bachmann & Nagarajan, 2024; Saparov et al., 2025, §1.1). Graph
search in this environment is not easy for an LM to learn. Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) found
the existence of a structural shortcut that can prevent learning the task on a specific graph topology
with next-token prediction (NTP), which can be ameliorated via certain training conditions (§1.2).
However, even with better training conditions, prior work has found the models fail to learn this task
as graphs scale in size and path length, hitting a scaling wall at ≈50 nodes, where learning becomes
increasingly unstable (Frydenlund, 2025; Saparov et al., 2025, especially their Fig. 6).
We investigate and break through this scaling wall. As prior work has identified an issue with
NTP, and suggested MTP as a potential solution, we begin by considering if NTP is the cause of poor
scaling. To do this, we extend the results of Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) to general topologies (§2),
and then elucidate how MTP overcomes the issues of NTP in a (nearly) topological-agnostic manner,
and the various benefits this has for learnability (§2.1). We then hypothesize two other suspects: the
causal constraint and lack of permutation-invariance. These motivate the development of alternative
tokenization methods (§3 and 4). Together, these methods allow us to stably learn the task at scale.
Prior work has eliminated obvious alternative suspects: model size and scratchpads (Saparov et al.,
2025). Larger models do not improve training as the graph scales. This aligns with our strong
empirical results with small models (implying larger models are unnecessary), and theoretical results
that transformers with O(log k) layers can express the task (Sanford et al., 2024b;a; Frydenlund, 2024;
Merrill & Sabharwal, 2025).1 Test-time compute via scratchpads/CoT is not a viable solution. We
show that a BFS scratchpad, while learnable, introduces its own tokenization difficulties. Critically, it
also reduces the latent reasoning graph to a linear chain, breaking the theoretical model (
L.4).
1.1
THE SHORTEST-PATH TASK
Let G = (N, E) be an unweighted graph, directed or not, with |N| nodes and |E| edges. Let s, t ∈N
be the start and end nodes of the shortest path, π = u0u1 . . . uk of length k + 1 nodes. Then, given
some tokenization of G and a query, Q = ‘( s t )’ s.t. s = u0, t = uk, the task is to generate the
shortest path from s to t, node-by-node; thus modelling p(πb | sb, tb, Gb) for the bth sample (Gb, πb).
Each graph is tokenized as a list of edges, E = [ ‘ui uj |’ : ∀(ui, uj) ∈E ] where ‘|’ is the end-
of-edge marker. The list is shuffled before concatenation to avoid providing structural information
(Bachmann & Nagarajan, 2024), e.g. ordered by BFS from s (Ge et al., 2025). The source-side
prefix is the concatenation [Q, E] (Frydenlund, 2024). The target-side is π tokenized with start- and
end-of-task tokens. As the source-side must be supplied, we restrict prediction to the target-side
(
O). Thus, the task must be learned from the supervision provided by the single path, π.
The graphs are synthetically generated; nodes are sampled from a global node vocabulary, Nb ⊆V,
and the edges are sampled via some topology-specific process. Nodes are represented by their one-hot
IDs and are thus semanticless in that there is no relationship between nodes, i.e. ‘atomic facts’. This
prevents homophily shortcuts (Huang et al., 2024; McPherson et al., 2001; Xu et al., 2025; Guan et al.,
2025). Thus, the model must learn to solve the task via the structural information provided by
the prefix alone. We generate unique instances during training (Saparov et al., 2025; Frydenlund,
2025). This is critical to avoid spurious correlations (Frydenlund, 2024; Wang et al., 2023).
This is an implicit latent multihop reasoning task, excluding inference-time procedures such as beam
search or explicit intermediate token generations via scratchpads or CoT (Pan et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2025; Nye et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022,
L.4). As we train on many different graphs and evaluate
on unseen graphs, this is the in-context setting of the task (Noroozizadeh et al., 2026,
C.4). This
contrasts with the in-weights setting, which trains on a single graph and evaluates on unseen paths.
Altogether, this setting allows us to isolate the defining challenge of multihop reasoning: the
ability to compose and chain individual inference steps into a coherent solution path (
C).

Given a single training instance, (G, π), where |π| = k+1, NTP results in learning a joint distribution
of p(π | s, t, E) = p(s | s, t) p(u1 | s, t) . . . p(ui | π<ui, t) . . . p(t | π<t, t) with k + 1 terms. The
model learns positional shortcuts to trivially predict the first and last terms since s and t start and end
π (Frydenlund, 2025). This leaves k −1 intra-sample terms, giving a sequential sample efficiency
(SSE) of k −1 input-target supervision pairs or loss terms. However, another topology-induced
shortcut exists that can also absorb the supervision provided by these terms, further reducing the SSE.
Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) introduced the adversarially designed path-star task to undermine
the SSE under NTP with star-shaped graphs (Fig. 8). Each graph has a centre node, s, with d arms
of length k. The query is (s, t), where t ends an arm, making π the arm containing t. Let d(ui) be
the degree of node ui. Then d(s) = d and all other nodes have degree 2 or 1. This topology creates
an unsympathetic interaction with NTP due to teacher-forcing, which conditions each prediction
on the prior ground-truth sequence (Williams & Zipser, 1989). Consider p(ui | π<ui, t, E) when
1 < i ≤k −1. By conditioning on the subpath, π<ui, the model can trivially predict that ui follows
ui−1 via the single edge (ui−1, ui) ∈E as d(ui−1) = 2. Such a prediction does not support learning
multihop reasoning and, this shortcut, called the Clever Hans Cheat (CHC), will absorb all terms
except p(u1 | s, t), reducing the SSE from k −1 to 1 supervision pair on path-star graphs under NTP.
This leads to very unfavourable learning conditions, where LMs using NTP fail to learn the task on
graphs with only 9 nodes (d = 2 and k = 4). Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) showed that a type of
multi-token prediction (MTP) can ameliorate this complete failure, seemingly by restoring the SSE.
However, the CHC not only reduces the amount of supervision, but also the type. Consider the
remaining term, p(u1 | s, t). This requires k hops to solve, as the model needs to reconstruct the
subpath, π(u1, t) = u1 . . . t, to predict u1 (done backwards). Now consider the terms the CHC
absorbed. Each term requires reconstruction of a subpath, each one hop shorter than the last, e.g.
p(u2 | su1, t) requires |π(u2, t)| = k −1, etc. Thus, if not for the CHC, these terms would have
provided a mixture of supervision at k′ = 2, . . . , k −1 hops, as each term is a decomposed subtask,
inducing a curriculum-like range of difficulties (Bengio et al., 2009). As the original path-star task
was trained on graphs of the same size, the model is only provided supervision at exactly k hops.
The complete failure to learn on path-star graphs can be ameliorated by training on a mixture of hop
lengths, i.e. mixed multihop training (MMHT;
A.1). Frydenlund (2025) showed that this can
be achieved in multiple ways: by sampling graphs of k′ ≤k or sampling an arm-interior node to be
the target, t′. These methods explicitly mix values of k′ ≤k at the population level or inner-sample.
This can also be achieved implicitly intra-sample via MTP, which is equivalent to token masking and
discrete diffusion (
D.7), or by training on different topologies (§2). Thus, both the density and
the type of supervision matter. The importance of MMHT for efficient multihop learning has been
established for other tasks (Sanford et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025).2
Frydenlund (2025) considered MTP as it induces MMHT; while insufficient alone to break the scaling
wall, this motivates a analysis of when the CHC induces topological shortcuts and prevents MMHT

under NTP (§2).3 We then show MTP overcomes this by simultaneously increasing supervision

CHARACTERIZING SAMPLE EFFICIENCY WITH GENERAL TOPOLOGIES
We now formalize when and how sequential supervision supports learning beyond star-graphs to
general topologies by characterizing the SSE under NTP. We will then extend this to MTP in §2.1.
Let A be the adjacency matrix of G s.t. A(u, v) = 1 if (u, v) ∈E, else 0. We say a node, ui−1 at
step i −1, is a choice node for a path, π, if there is more than one possible continuation from ui−1,
given the subpath π≤ui−1 = π(s, ui−1). We define c(ui−1, π≤ui−1) as the choice set of node ui−1,
containing the unseen neighbours of ui−1 which may continue the prior path s.t. c(ui−1, π≤ui−1) =
{v ∈N : v /∈π≤ui−1 and A(ui−1, v) = 1}. The choice degree is |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| = d(ui−1) −
P
v∈π≤ui−1 A(ui−1, v), thus |c(s, {s})| = d(s) and |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| < d(ui−1), otherwise. A
node is a choice node when |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| > 1, since if |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| = 1, there is only a
single node that can continue the path and the CHC is sufficient for predicting it via the only viable
edge (ui−1, v ∈c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)). Only choice nodes support learning under NTP, as they
require the model to perform a latent multihop traversal of the graph to predict the succeeding
node from the choice set. The CHC absorbs all other nodes. Thus, the SSE for general topologies
depends on both G and π and is just the number of choice nodes in π, c(π).
For undirected graphs, any interior node ui−1 with d(ui−1) ≥3 is a choice node. Proof: ui−1 is
adjacent to only one observed node (ui−2), as an edge to any earlier uj (j < i −2) would yield a
shorter path to ui−1, contradicting it being a shortest path; thus |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| = d(ui−1)−1 ≥2.
Multiple choice nodes induce a subtask decomposition, implicitly providing MMHT. Proof sketch:
assume s and u′ = ui−1 are choice nodes in π = s . . . ui−1 . . . t. Then multiple nodes could follow
u′. To determine which node in the choice set is correct, the model needs to reconstruct the subpath
π(ui−1, t), which requires k′ = k −i + 1 < k hops. Hence, the subtask is simpler, with the position
of the choice nodes controlling the sub-sample difficulty in terms of hop size. As a consequence, SSE
and hop difficulty under NTP depend on both the graph topology and the sampled path. We could
maximize SSE and the mixture distribution by training on a topology that branches at each step in the
path, i.e. d-ary trees. This is not a scalable strategy when considering larger – more difficult – k, as it
requires graphs of size |N| = dk. However, MTP strongly reduces this dependency on topology.
2.1
THE SEQUENTIAL SAMPLE EFFICIENCY OF MULTI-TOKEN PREDICTION
MTP generalizes NTP to predict up to n future tokens from a given position. Consider conditioning
on the prior-path, π≤ui−1, i.e. up to position i −1. Predicting one step into the future, j = i,
recovers NTP. Predicting two steps, j = i + 1, results in a skip in the conditioning information
since the prior node is unobserved, i.e. implicitly masked out. Let D be the distance matrix of G,
s.t. D(u, v) is the distance between u and v. We extend the definition of a choice set to MTP as
c(uj−1, π≤ui−1) = {v ∈N : v /∈π≤ui−1 and D(ui−1, v) = j −i + 1} for uj, when conditioning
on π≤ui−1. Thus, the choice set for node uj contains all unseen nodes j −i + 1 hops from ui−1.
Under NTP, when j = i, non-choice nodes with |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| = 1 do not contribute to
supervision as they do not require multihop reasoning to predict the following node. This is not true
under MTP when j −i > 0, which introduces a skip of length j −i. This is because uj is j −i + 1
edges from ui−1, so any prediction must traverse j −i + 1 edges and no single edge lookup suffices.
This multihop mechanism is different than when |c(ui−1, π≤ui−1)| > 1. For either NTP or MTP,
when we have a choice node, multihop reasoning must be used to predict the following token from

latent reasoning graph has unknown topology, thus making topological assumptions impermissible
(§1). Given that our theoretical model was constructed to be agnostic to topology, and our finding
that the theoretical advantages of MTP share this agnosticism, we conjecture that the advantages of
MTP uncovered here apply to any multihop reasoning task. This would make MTP a principled
objective for any multihop reasoning task in general. Evaluating this is non-trivial, as our results rest
on each target token corresponding to exactly one hop, which is untrue for natural language (
B).

THE CAUSAL CONSTRAINT, SPARSITY, AND FRAGMENTED ROUTING
Causal LMs derive expressive power from sequential computation, but are restricted to prior tokens
only (Vaswani et al., 2017). Frydenlund (2024); Saparov et al. (2025) identified that the causal
constraint hinders information routing. We show this problem is severely compounded with
fragmented routing due to an interaction between the causal constraint, graph sparsity, and
tokenization. The model must route information across edges, which can only occur after an edge is
observed. Since our graphs are sparse and E is shuffled (to prevent structural shortcuts), the edge

stream forms many disconnected components until most of E is seen.5 As a result, routing is deferred
until most of E has been processed, exhausting the model’s sequential computation (Fig. 11 in

The causal constraint can be bypassed via repeating inputs (Xu et al., 2024; Springer et al., 2025;
Kuki´c et al., 2026). Thus, if we tokenized the input as [Q, E, E], then the second edge list can
fully observe the first. However, E provides an inconsistent scaffold, so we consider the node list,
N, as [Q, E, N] for a consistent means of accumulating edge information. N is sorted by order of
appearance of each node in the shuffled E, i.e. E is shuffled, but N is ordered relative to E.
To test the hypothesis that the node list bypasses issues relating to routing, we consider both causal
LMs and prefix LMs without causal masking on the source-side prefix (Raffel et al., 2020; Saparov
et al., 2025). Thus, if the only benefit to using the edge list is that it avoids routing issues, then we
would expect to see performance gains with the causal LM over a baseline, but not the prefix LM. We
refer to experiments that include the node list as ‘+ N’ compared to the baseline of only [Q, E].

POSITIONAL EMBEDDINGS AND PERMUTATION SENSITIVITY
Non-causal models like prefix LMs have been explored both to remove the attention restriction (as
per above) and for their perceived permutation invariance (Frydenlund, 2024; Saparov et al., 2025).
Permutation invariance is important; it reduces the space of possible tokenization orderings and
spurious correlations. Since permutation invariance of E is inherent to the task, this inductive bias is
well-motivated. However, prior work failed to adequately address this, as a more clandestine issue
breaks permutation invariance: positional embeddings (Chen et al., 2023b). This is clandestine
as positional embeddings are ubiquitous in LMs given their necessity for serial tokenization.
Standard transformers take a T-length sequence of content token IDs x alongside positional IDs ϕ.
Tokenizing an edge as ‘u v |’ means any node ID recurring at multiple positions is only distinguishable
through ϕ. Positional embeddings are thus necessary because tokens are not unique, but they break
the desired permutation invariance, so we make every ‘token’ unique without ϕ.
Note how the edge-marker creates sequential annotations of the edges. We follow Saparov et al.
(2025) in concatenating and projecting the content and positional embeddings for the baseline models.

We use ‘STAN’ instead of ‘permutation invariance’ to separate the method from the motivating
hypothesis, since STAN can apply to causal LMs, which are incapable of permutation invariance.
STAN has the practical benefit of reducing the sequential tokenization of E to 1/3 the original length.

path is not unique, the task accuracy is whether a valid shortest path was generated (TACC) (
