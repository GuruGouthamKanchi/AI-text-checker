Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference,
and the Collapse of Trust Calibration
Deepthika Sa, Pawni Yadavb, B. Sankarc,∗
aDepartment of Electronics and Communication Engineering, Amrita School of Engineering, Amrita Vishwa
Vidyapeetham, Coimbatore, 641112, India
bDepartment of Information Technology, Netaji Subhas University of Technology New Delhi, 110078, India
cDepartment of Mechanical Engineering, Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bangalore, 560012, India
Abstract
Research on sycophancy asks whether large language models (LLMs) bend towards what their
users want to hear. We ask the reverse question: when an LLM is confident, consistent, and
wrong, do users bend towards it? In a within-subjects experiment, 32 participants learned up
to five science topics by chatting with five assistants built on the same model (GPT-5-mini).
Unknown to participants, each assistant’s briefing had 0, 25, 50, 75, or 100% of its facts
replaced by plausible falsehoods, and every assistant spoke with full confidence. A surprise
quiz after each topic offered the correct fact, the planted falsehood, and two distractors, with
a confidence rating for every answer. Across 104 quizzes and 1,040 answers, scores fell from
7.4 to 2.9 out of 10, and the planted option was chosen on 60% of falsified items against
11% of correctly taught ones (odds ratio 12.5). Transcript coding showed that whenever
the assistant stated a falsehood, it was adopted about 65% of the time at every falsification
level. Absorbed falsehoods were held with high confidence (8.4/10), the modelled confidence
advantage of right over wrong answers vanished, and the discrimination of confidence for
correctness fell from an AUROC of 0.70 to chance, while post-task trust ratings barely moved.
Participants tended to return to the chat more when the assistant was worse, but returning
went with more absorption, not less, and 30% of all turns pasted quiz questions back to the
source being checked. The assistant itself was almost never sycophantic and never conceded
under pushback. We introduce a baseline-corrected Human Deference Index to quantify this
human-side sycophancy and argue that trustworthiness is a property of the human–AI pair,
that sycophancy evaluations should measure both directions, and that learning interfaces
must make reliability legible claim by claim.
Keywords:
trust calibration, sycophancy, large language models, human–AI interaction,
misinformation, automation bias, metacognition, design for trust

1. Introduction
For a species as social as ours, survival has never depended on the individual alone. In
evolutionary biology, fitness describes an organism’s capacity to survive and reproduce in its
environment; for humans, that capacity has been shaped by life in groups, where cooperation,
communication and resource sharing are routine. The cognitive demands of such relationships
are reflected in the unusually large primate neocortex, which has been linked to the need
to track relationships within social groups (Dunbar, 1992). Food sharing illustrates the
point. In many hunter-gatherer societies a successful hunt feeds more than the hunter: large,
unpredictably acquired food packages tend to be widely distributed, partly because the cost
of not sharing outweighs the benefit of eating alone (Hawkes et al., 2018). With repeated
interaction, sharing and reciprocity become patterned, particularly where camp membership is
stable (Smith et al., 2016). Cooperation among kin is explained by inclusive fitness (Hamilton,
1964), whereas cooperation among non-kin rests partly on the expectation that partners will
reciprocate (Rand and Nowak, 2013). Repeated cooperation therefore produces more than
an exchange of resources. It produces expectations about how others will behave, and those
expectations are a basis for trust.
As societies grew more complex, so did the relationships through which such expectations
form. Human social networks are layered, from an intimate core of family and close friends to
acquaintances at the margins, and the layers differ in contact frequency, emotional closeness,
reciprocity and trust (Dunbar, 2025). Nor is trust static within a relationship. It starts from
some baseline and moves as experience accumulates: perceptions of predictability grow from
observing the consistency of a partner’s behaviour (Camanto and Campbell, 2025), and trust
strengthens when positive expectations are confirmed and weakens when they are violated
(Lewicki et al., 2006). Trust is less a fixed property of a relationship than a running account
of expectation and outcome, and the degree to which A trusts B need not equal the degree to
which B trusts A.
Is a relationship even necessary? Not always. A patient trusts a doctor they have never
met, and a passenger trusts a pilot they will never see. Non-experts routinely rely on expert
knowledge they cannot verify, such as a physician’s diagnosis (Leefmann and Lesle, 2020).
Such trust rests on competence, professional standards, reputation and institutions rather
than on familiarity: “swift trust” forms among strangers through recognisable roles, and trust
in a stockbroker is bounded by regulation rather than by acquaintance (Lewicki et al., 2006).
A relationship is one basis for trust, not a prerequisite for it.
Yet the trust we extend to strangers is usually cautious. We hesitate before trusting
someone we have never met, not because they are untrustworthy but because we lack
information about them: their history, their intentions, the relationship between us. Seen this
way, an AI assistant is an extreme case. It has no personal history, no body, no reputation
built through shared experience and no intentions we can read, and it is not even a stranger
in the usual sense, because a stranger is at least another person. If people are wary of trusting
a human being they do not know, why would they trust something that is not human at all?
One answer is that such trust does not need a relationship, because it can rest instead on the
kind of reliability we attribute to machines.

Conversational AI breaks this arrangement (Figure 1, bottom). Large language models
have moved human–computer interaction from specialised, task-bound automation towards
systems that act as advisers (Henrique and Santos, 2024; Klingbeil et al., 2024). People ask
them “What should I do?”, “Which option is better?” and “Can you explain this to me?”,
questions without a single predetermined answer against which a response can be checked.
An AI system can also be wrong while sounding coherent and certain. With a calculator,
the user checks whether a function was performed correctly; with AI, the user must judge
whether the information itself is accurate enough to rely on, often on a topic they are trying
to learn precisely because they do not know it. Reliance on such systems is already known to
be unstable: people sometimes discount good computerised advice (Promberger and Baron,
2006) and, in other conditions, follow AI advice against contrary evidence and their own
judgement (Klingbeil et al., 2024). And if trust is built through repeated experiences of
something meeting our expectations, a system that is trained to meet expectations—to sound
right, to agree, to please—can meet them while being wrong.
This creates an under-studied failure mode at the intersection of two literatures. Research
on trust in automation asks whether people’s trust tracks a system’s reliability (Lee and
See, 2004; Hoff and Bashir, 2015). Research on sycophancy asks whether LLMs tailor their
3


answers to what users want rather than to what is true (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al.,
2024). Both typically treat one side of the human–AI pair as fixed. Sycophancy research, in
particular, measures whether the model adapts to the user: whether it abandons a correct
answer under pushback, mirrors a stated opinion or flatters the user’s self-image (Fanous
et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026). The reverse direction has received far less
attention: when the model holds its ground confidently and consistently, does the user adapt
to the model? We call this human-side deference: the convergence of a person’s beliefs on a
source’s claims irrespective of their truth. It is the mirror image of model sycophancy, and it
matters because modern assistants are, by design, fluent, assertive and unhedged (Zhou et al.,
2024).
We report HARICHAN (Human–AI Reliance in CHAt iNteractions), a controlled experi-
ment in which the same LLM taught five science topics while covertly falsifying between none
and all of its briefing facts, always with full confidence. We address five research questions:
RQ1. How much planted misinformation do learners absorb as falsification rises, and does
their acceptance of each stated falsehood change with the source’s overall reliability?
RQ2. Does learners’ confidence distinguish what they learned correctly from what they
absorbed falsely?
RQ3. Does their reported trust track the assistant’s actual reliability?
RQ4. How do learners check an assistant they have reason to doubt, and what happens
when they push back?
RQ5. Is the deference sycophancy on the model’s side or on the human’s, and how does
human-side deference relate to confidence?
Our contributions are fourfold. Empirically, we measure misinformation uptake across a
graded range of assistant reliability in open-ended conversational learning, and we separate
exposure to a falsehood from acceptance of it by coding every transcript for which facts the
assistant actually stated. Methodologically, we adapt baseline-corrected sycophancy metrics
from model evaluation to the human side, introducing a Human Deference Index and pairing
it with metacognitive measures of confidence. Conceptually, we argue that the “calculator
fallacy”—extending to a fluent, probabilistic system the trust that deterministic tools earn
through verification—explains why trust cannot calibrate when error is invisible, and that
trustworthiness is a property of the human–AI pair. For design, we derive strategies that
make reliability legible per claim, route verification outward and evaluate trust behaviourally.
2. Related work
2.1. Trust, reliance and calibration
Trust in automation is commonly defined as the attitude that an agent will help achieve
one’s goals in a situation characterised by uncertainty and vulnerability (Lee and See, 2004),
and in interpersonal terms as a willingness to be vulnerable that rests on perceived ability,
benevolence and integrity (Mayer et al., 1995). The design goal is not more trust but calibrated
trust: trust that matches a system’s actual capability (Lee and See, 2004; Wischnewski et al.,
4


2023), or, in the terms of Jacovi et al. (2021), warranted rather than unwarranted trust. Lee
and See (2004) also describe resolution, the degree to which changes in capability are mirrored
by changes in trust, and specificity, the degree to which trust attaches to particular functions
rather than to the system as a whole. Reviews of trust measurement emphasise that trust is
latent and multifaceted, that most scales are bespoke, and that behavioural measures should
accompany self-report (Gebru et al., 2022; Wischnewski et al., 2023). Trust and distrust also
appear to be distinct factors rather than two ends of one scale (Scharowski et al., 2025), and
healthy distrust may require both explicit error detection and an intuitive sense of when to
doubt (Peters et al., 2026).
Hoff and Bashir (2015) separate dispositional, situational and learned trust and note
that learned trust updates dynamically with reliability, but only when people can compare
the system’s performance with their own. Experiments bear this out. Stated and observed
accuracy both shape trust, but observed accuracy dominates once users can see it (Yin et al.,
2019); trust can drift away from reliability when no feedback is available (Rittenberg et al.,
2024); and displaying an AI’s case-level confidence helps calibrate trust where explanations
do not (Zhang et al., 2020). Computational models describe trust as moving towards recent
experience, cumulative trust and a prior expectation bias; without outcome feedback, the
experience term cannot register failure (Hu et al., 2019). For virtual and embedded AI,
high initial trust typically drops after errors are experienced (Glikson and Woolley, 2020),
and trust has affective as well as cognitive roots (Gillath et al., 2021; Choung et al., 2023).
The recurring condition across this literature is visibility: trust calibrates to what users can
observe.
2.2. Overreliance and automation bias with conversational AI
When verification is costly, people lean on automated aids. Automation bias, the use
of automated output as a substitute for vigilant information seeking, produces commission
errors even when contradictory cues are available (Skitka et al., 1999; Goddard et al., 2012),
and it shares an attentional mechanism with complacency that affects experts and novices
alike (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010). Labelling advice as algorithmic increases its weight
regardless of its quality (Logg et al., 2019), and AI-labelled advice can override both contextual
evidence and people’s own assessments, at real cost (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Appropriate
reliance requires both relying on correct advice and overriding incorrect advice (Schemmer
et al., 2023); explanations can reduce overreliance when they lower the cost of verification
(Vasconcelos et al., 2023) but can also inflate reliance when they merely justify (Dzindolet
et al., 2003). Sundar (2020) attributes part of this to a “machine heuristic”, the lay belief
that machines are objective and accurate, which invites users to outsource vigilance.
Large language models intensify the problem because they are fluent by default and
rarely express uncertainty; users rely on their confident outputs whether or not those outputs
are correct (Zhou et al., 2024), and overreliance on overconfident models appears across
languages (Rathi et al., 2025). Users overestimate the accuracy of model answers from default
explanations, and longer explanations raise confidence without improving discrimination
(Steyvers et al., 2025). Model-generated explanations speed up fact-checking but mislead
when the model is convincingly wrong (Si et al., 2024); users overlooked the errors in ChatGPT
answers to programming questions 39% of the time even though 52% of those answers were
5


incorrect (Kabir et al., 2024); and LLM-based search is fast but invites overreliance when the
model errs (Spatharioti et al., 2025). First-person hedging reduces overreliance only in part
(Kim et al., 2024), sources and visible inconsistencies help more than explanations (Kim et al.,
2025), and combining visual and verbal uncertainty cues can sharpen subjective discrimination
while worsening behavioural reliance (Ojewale et al., 2026). In conversational settings, dialogue
cues increase trust even when an assistant spreads health misinformation, and only acted-upon,
independent verification lowers it (Liao and Sundar, 2026). AI assistance can also make
people perform better while making them worse at judging their own performance (Fernandes
et al., 2026), and human confidence in themselves, rather than in the AI, often governs
whether advice is adopted (Chong et al., 2022). Most of this work, however, studies single
decisions with a fixed error rate. Less is known about learning from a conversational source
whose reliability varies, where the learner has no independent knowledge and the only readily
available check is the source itself.
2.3. Misinformation, fluency and epistemic vigilance
Research on misinformation explains why confidently stated falsehoods persist. Once
encoded, misinformation continues to influence reasoning even after it is retracted, and
corrections work best when they supply an alternative account (Lewandowsky et al., 2012).
Repetition increases perceived truth (Hasher et al., 1977), and prior knowledge offers sur-
prisingly little protection against this illusory-truth effect (Fazio et al., 2015). Belief in
misinformation is driven less by a lack of intelligence than by ordinary cognitive shortcuts,
including reliance on fluency and familiarity (Ecker et al., 2022). People do possess mecha-
nisms of epistemic vigilance that monitor the competence and benevolence of sources and the
coherence of what they say (Sperber et al., 2010), and much human knowledge legitimately
rests on trusting testimony we cannot check (Hardwig, 1991). These mechanisms evolved
for human informants, however, and fluent machine text offers few of the signals they rely
on; people form stronger and less revisable beliefs from sources that sound confident and
knowledgeable (Kidd and Birhane, 2023). Access to external information can also inflate
people’s sense of what they themselves know (Fisher et al., 2015; Sparrow et al., 2011), and
offloading cognition to tools depends on metacognitive judgements that can be miscalibrated
(Risko and Gilbert, 2016). Measuring such metacognition requires separating sensitivity
(does confidence discriminate right from wrong?) from bias (is confidence too high overall?)
(Fleming and Lau, 2014).
2.4. Sycophancy: the model adapts to the user
Sycophancy refers to the tendency of language models to tailor responses to what users
appear to want rather than to what is true. It was first documented as models repeating
back users’ stated views, increasingly so at larger scales (Perez et al., 2023), and has been
traced to human preference data that reward agreement and convincingly written responses
over correct ones (Sharma et al., 2024). Models agree with users on subjective questions
and follow plausible hints even on factual ones (Ranaldi and Pucci, 2023). A family of
benchmarks now quantifies the behaviour. SycEval classifies answer changes under rebuttal as
progressive (incorrect to correct) or regressive (correct to incorrect) and finds sycophancy in
58% of cases, with citation-based rebuttals producing the most regressive flips (Fanous et al.,
6


2025). SYCON Bench measures how many turns a model holds its stance under sustained
pressure and how often it flips (Hong et al., 2025). Adversarial scientific QA dialogues show
that resistance to misleading cues depends more on alignment and reasoning than on scale
(Zhang et al., 2025). ELEPHANT broadens the construct to social sycophancy, the excessive
preservation of the user’s face through validation, indirectness and acceptance of the user’s
framing, and subtracts a human baseline to score it (Cheng et al., 2026b). A psychometric
Social Sycophancy Scale distinguishes uncritical agreement, obsequiousness and excitement
as perceived by readers (Rehani et al., 2026). An expert survey shows that researchers share
concern about sycophancy but not its boundaries (Ye et al., 2026). Reinforcement learning
from human feedback can also make models better at convincing human evaluators that wrong
answers are right, raising evaluators’ false-positive rates (Wen et al., 2025), and training
models to be warm can increase sycophancy and reduce accuracy (Ibrahim et al., 2026). The
persuasive power of LLMs in conversation is itself well documented (Salvi et al., 2025).
2.5. Human consequences of sycophancy
A smaller body of work traces what sycophancy does to people. In a between-subjects
study, a custom GPT that never contradicted users lowered demonstrated trust, and the
decline in self-reported trust was significant mainly when participants could verify answers
(Carro, 2024). Sycophantic chatbots increase attitude extremity and certainty while being
perceived as unbiased, an effect driven by one-sided facts rather than by validation (Rathje
et al., 2025). Sycophancy decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence while users
continue to prefer and trust sycophantic models (Cheng et al., 2026a). Flattery combined
with agreement lowers perceived authenticity, whereas agreement delivered in a neutral tone
raises trust (Sun and Wang, 2026). Sycophantic assistants leave novices’ misconceptions
uncorrected in problem-solving tasks without the novices noticing (Bo et al., 2026).
Table 1 places our study relative to this work. Almost all of it asks whether the model
adapts to the user, and measures the model. We hold the model’s behaviour fixed—confident,
non-flattering and unyielding to pushback—and measure whether the user adapts to the
model.
2.6. Designing for calibrated trust
Design research treats trustworthiness as something communicated through cues. The
MATCH model distinguishes a system’s actual trustworthiness from the cues that signal it
and warns that some prominent cues, such as the chatbot medium and a polished design look,
are irrelevant to trustworthiness yet shape it (Liao and Sundar, 2022). Proposed remedies
include cognitive forcing functions that make users commit before seeing AI output (Buçinca
et al., 2021), adaptive cues delivered when over-trust is detected (Okamura and Yamada,
2020), seamful designs that expose a system’s limits rather than hide them (Ehsan et al.,
2024), deliberate micro-frictions that interrupt automatic behaviour (Cox et al., 2016), and
verification affordances that users act on (Liao and Sundar, 2026). In design practice itself, AI
assistance has been shown to hurt high-performing teams through misplaced reliance (Zhang
et al., 2021), and trust in generative AI rises and falls with many interacting factors during
co-design (Guo et al., 2025). All of these strategies presuppose a user who can recognise,

3. Method
3.1. Design
We used a within-subjects experiment with covert manipulation of assistant reliability.
Each participant learned five topics, each from a different assistant (Assistants A to E, in
presentation order), and each assistant was configured to one of five reliability levels in which
0%, 25%, 50%, 75% or 100% of its briefing facts were falsified. Levels were assigned by a
5 × 5 Latin square: participants received its rows in rotation as they enrolled, and the task
at position i received the level indexed by (i + row) mod 5. Apart from the first participant,
who received the same row as the fourth, any five consecutive participants therefore met
every level at every position equally often. Topic order was randomised independently for
each participant, so the pairing of topics with levels was left to chance; in the final data each
topic–level combination occurred in between one and seven quizzes, and topic is included as a
covariate in a sensitivity model. Figure 2 summarises the procedure.

chat is a direct signature of absorbed misinformation. Choosing it on an item that had been
taught correctly provides a baseline for how attractive the planted option is on its own.

3.4. Procedure
The consent page described the study as learning from AI assistants, did not mention
inaccuracy or a quiz, and asked participants to rely only on the assistant, without other
websites, books or people. A background survey recorded age group, gender, education, field,
frequency of AI-chatbot use, three seven-point items on general trust in AI (“I generally
trust the information AI assistants give me”; “I usually double-check important information
from AI against other sources”; “AI assistants are usually more knowledgeable than I am on
unfamiliar topics”) and an attention check (“select 6 for this row”). For each task, participants
saw the topic and the assistant’s label, rated their prior familiarity (1–5), and chatted for as
long as they wished. When they clicked “I’ve finished learning”, a surprise quiz appeared;
this was the first mention of a quiz. Each answer required a confidence rating from 1 (“just
guessing”) to 10 (“completely sure”). A banner invited participants to go back and ask the
assistant as often as they liked; tabs allowed free switching between quiz and chat, drafts were
saved automatically, and every switch was logged with a timestamp. Submission required all
ten answers and confidence ratings. Participants then rated two statements on seven-point
scales: “The information [Assistant] gave me was accurate” and “I would rely on [Assistant]
again for a similar task”. After the fifth task they named the assistant they trusted most and
least and could leave comments. A mandatory debrief revealed the design, apologised for
10


the concealment and listed, for every assistant, each false claim it may have made beside the
correct fact, flagging the ones the participant had chosen.
3.5. Participants
Thirty-two adults enrolled online. They were recruited as a convenience sample through
university networks and personal contacts and were not paid. Most were aged 18–24 (n = 21;
25–34, n = 8; 35–44, n = 3); 19 identified as women and 13 as men; 21 held a bachelor’s
degree, six a master’s degree, three a doctorate, and two a school qualification. Twenty-seven
(84%) used AI chat assistants daily, three weekly and two less often. On the general-trust
items, participants agreed moderately that they trust AI information (M = 4.34, SD = 1.07),
agreed that they double-check important AI information (M = 5.59, SD = 1.36) and agreed
that AI is more knowledgeable than they are on unfamiliar topics (M = 4.94, SD = 1.61).
Prior familiarity with the topics was low (M = 2.09 on the 1–5 scale; 69% of ratings were 1
or 2).

3.7. Transcript coding
Because a falsified fact can only be absorbed if it is actually said, we coded all 104 quizzed
task-runs (716 messages). For each of the ten quizzed facts, the coder recorded whether
the assistant’s messages stated the true version, the planted false version, both or neither,
separately for the learning phase and for the whole run. Each user turn was classified as
an open request (“tell me everything about ECG”), a specific question, a pasted or retyped
quiz question, an answer check, a format request, a challenge, a social or meta turn, or other.
For every challenge we recorded the targeted fact, whether the doubted claim was true or
false, and whether the assistant held, partly softened or conceded. Finally, the assistant’s
behaviour in each run was rated from 0 (absent) to 3 (pervasive) on six dimensions adapted
from the Social Sycophancy Scale (Rehani et al., 2026) and ELEPHANT (Cheng et al.,
2026b): validation or praise, obsequiousness, excitement, uncritical agreement, hedging and
assertiveness. Coding was performed by an LLM-based coder (four coder instances, each
coding a quarter of the runs) following a written protocol that supplied, for each fact, the
exact true and planted wordings; verbatim quotations were checked programmatically against
the transcripts. The coders’ notes on borderline cases (e.g., runs in which the assistant stated
both versions of a fact) were reviewed and resolved by rule.
