Selection for Dialog Systems that Engage Users with Alternative Views

Abstract
Systems that aim to help a user engage with
viewpoints other than their own have to de-
cide how different those viewpoints should be.
Viewpoints are mainly represented on a scalar
scale, too coarse to distinguish viewpoints that
disagree with the user about different things.
We introduce a selection policy in which a ban-
dit sets how far viewpoints may sit from the
user, and the policy selects the most novel view-
point within that bound. We vary only its stance
encoder, comparing a scalar score with Perspec-
tivized Stance Vectors, which break a position
into separate stances on concepts. We integrate
it inside a dialog system and run a between-
subjects study (N = 63). Concept-level stance
produces more varied exposure sequences and
broader coverage of the topic’s positions, but
does not change how deeply users justify view-
points or how accurately they reconstruct them.
Finally, we analyze user–viewpoint relations
at the concept level, showing that users recon-
struct a viewpoint least accurately on concepts
that one side took no position on, rather than
on concepts where the two disagreed. Our find-
ings open up opportunities for dialog systems
that support engagement with alternative view-
points by acting on which concepts a user and a
viewpoint disagree on, rather than on distance
alone.

2021). In politically contentious settings this is
associated with affective polarization, which in-
creases hostility toward opposing groups and re-
duces willingness to interact with them (Iyengar
and Westwood, 2015; Iyengar et al., 2019). One

Providing alternative views is not sufficient on
its own, because the relation between a new view
and a person’s existing position shapes how they
process it (Lewandowsky et al., 2012; Zhou and
Shen, 2024). A view close to what a user already
holds adds little, while one far from it may be dis-
missed rather than considered (Sherif and Hovland,
1961). Which views a user sees should therefore
depend on where that user stands, and several sys-
tems select on this basis, choosing what to show
next from the arguments a user has already been
exposed to (Aicher et al., 2022, 2023b,a). Such a
system compares each candidate viewpoint with
the user’s own position, and a viewpoint can differ
arXiv:2610.31459v1  [cs.HC]  22 Oct 2026


from that position in two ways. It can differ in
degree, by opposing the user more or less strongly,
and in kind, by opposing a different aspect of the
issue. Which of these differences a system selects
on is an open question.
How a system represents stance of a viewpoint
decides which of the two it can act on at all. Stance
is usually summarised as a single point on an or-
dinal scale running from opposing to supporting a
position, which records degree and discards kind.
Two viewpoints that oppose different aspects of an
issue can land on the same point of that scale, and a
system comparing them with a user sees one value
for both. Figure 1 shows this for hunting. Both
viewpoints sit two steps from the user’s position,
so a system using the SCALAR scale has no basis
for preferring either, even though the aspects they
disagree with the user about are not the same. A
system that cannot see a difference cannot select
on it, whatever rule it applies.
Finer representations do exist. Perspectivized
Stance Vectors (PSV), for example, record a sep-
arate stance toward each of several concepts in
an issue (Plenz et al., 2025), so two viewpoints
that oppose different aspects receive different vec-
tors (Figure 1, right). What such a representation
changes for a user has not been tested, because a
system that adopts a new representation normally
changes the rule that uses it at the same time, leav-
ing the two effects confounded. We therefore keep
selection policy–the rule that decides which view-
point to show next– fixed and swap only the stance
representation underneath it, comparing a single or-
dinal score (SCALAR) with Perspectivized Stance
Vectors (PSV). We implement this policy in a di-
alog system that presents the selected viewpoints
and asks users about each of them.
Isolating the representation in this way lets us
ask three questions. RQ1: How does represent-
ing stance at the concept level, rather than as a
single score, change the viewpoints shown to a
user? RQ2: Does concept-level stance represen-
tation affect how fully users justify viewpoints and
how accurately they reconstruct them? RQ3: How
does the concept-level relation between a user’s
stance and a viewpoint’s stance relate to recon-
struction accuracy? Answering them contributes
a viewpoint-selection policy that operates over in-
terchangeable stance representations, an account
of what each representation makes available both
to users and across the full stance space, and a
concept-level analysis of reconstruction that a sin-
gle aggregate score leaves hidden.
2
Related Work
Selecting viewpoints for a particular user draws
on three lines of work. Argument retrieval defines
how candidates are found. User-conditioned selec-
tion makes that choice depend on the recipient and
on what they have already seen. Work on stance
supplies the signal that relates a candidate to a user.
We review each below, asking in each case how the
relation between a user and a candidate viewpoint
is represented, since that representation is what we
vary.
Argument retrieval and user-conditioned selec-
tion.
Argument retrieval finds arguments relevant
to a query, including arguments with particular ar-
gumentative properties (Wachsmuth et al., 2018).
Argument recommendation conditions that selec-
tion on the recipient. Brenneis et al. (Brenneis et al.,
2021) introduce a dataset for evaluating argument
recommenders from personal attitudes and interac-
tions with arguments, more recent work incorpo-
rates user or stakeholder perspectives (Heinisch
et al., 2024; Aicher et al., 2022), and Perspec-
tiveArg2024 formulates perspective-aware argu-
ment selection as a retrieval task (Falk et al., 2024).
These approaches establish user and perspective
information as retrieval signals, but differ in how
they represent the relation between a user and a
candidate argument.
Selection can also unfold over a conversation,
with the user’s current state and the arguments al-
ready shown affecting what comes next. Aicher
et al. (Aicher et al., 2022, 2023a) model exposure
histories and user positions in argumentative dialog
systems to identify arguments that broaden subse-
quent exposure, showing that selection can operate
as a policy over an evolving interaction rather than
as one-shot retrieval. Our policy similarly selects
over a session rather than in one shot.
Stance as a retrieval signal.
Stance provides a
direct signal for distinguishing arguments in con-
troversial discussions. Methods exist for identify-
ing the position expressed toward a topic or tar-
get (Durmus et al., 2019; Kobbe et al., 2020), and
stance has been incorporated into retrieval and rank-
ing through stance-aware query expansion (Kiesel
et al., 2021) and stance-aware re-ranking for com-
parative retrieval (Reimer et al., 2023). These ap-
proaches treat stance as a property attached to an


argument. In our setting stance instead defines the
space in which candidates are compared, so that the
distance between a user’s stance and a candidate
determines eligibility and the stance representation
determines which differences a policy can distin-
guish.
Conditioning on stance distance is supported by
work on how arguments are processed.
Social
Judgment Theory models messages relative to a
receiver’s existing position (Sherif and Hovland,
1961), and studies of belief revision and misin-
formation show that the discrepancy between in-
coming information and prior beliefs affects how
that information is processed (Durmus and Cardie,
2018; Zhao and Fink, 2021; Lewandowsky et al.,
2012). This literature motivates conditioning selec-
tion on user–viewpoint distance without specifying
how that distance should be represented.
Fine-grained stance representations.
A com-
pact stance representation characterizes an argu-
ment’s position toward a target, but collapses dis-
tinctions between positions on different aspects of
an issue. Plenz et al. (Plenz et al., 2025) address
this with Perspectivized Stance Vectors, which rep-
resent an argument through its stances toward a
shared set of issue-specific concepts, making agree-
ment and disagreement explicit at the level of in-
dividual concepts. They use concept-level stance
to analyze agreement between arguments after re-
trieval. We use it instead as the representation over
which a viewpoint-selection policy operates.
3
Method
We formulate viewpoint selection as a retrieval
problem over a fixed corpus of viewpoints. Our
methodology represents the stance of the partici-
pant and candidate viewpoints, computes their dis-
tance in the resulting stance space, and uses this dis-
tance together with novelty to select and order five
viewpoints. We integrate this selection policy into
a dialog system that encodes the participant’s free-
text stance and presents the selected viewpoints.
Figure 2 illustrates this selection framework. The
remainder of this section describes the viewpoint
corpus, stance representations, selection policy, and
the dialog system.

3.2
Stance Representations
A stance representation encodes a position so that
two positions can be compared numerically. We
compare a scalar representation with a concept-
level representation based on PSVs.
SCALAR.
A stance is encoded as an ordinal value
ka ∈{1, . . . , 5}, from strongly against to strongly
for, following a common stance operationalization
(Chuang et al., 2026). The distance between two
stances is dS(a, b) = |ka −kb|. This representa-
tion distinguishes five overall positions but does
not encode which aspects of the issue produce the
difference.
PSV.
Following Plenz et al. (2025), a stance is
encoded as an N × 3 one-hot matrix P, with each


row representing a stance (against, neutral, or for)
on concept i. Distance is the number of concept–
stance changes between two positions:

3.3
Selecting and Ordering Viewpoints
We formulate viewpoint selection as retrieval over
a fixed corpus, because rather than generating new
content, the system selects one unseen viewpoint
at each step. Let V be the corpus (Section 3.1),
ϕ : Σ∗→S the stance encoder, and d a distance
over the resulting stance space (Section 3.2). A
participant’s stance is u = ϕ(xu), where xu is their
own view on the topic, written as free text before
the session.
Let H denote the viewpoints already shown. At
each step, the policy first restricts selection to un-
seen viewpoints within a distance bound δ of the
participant’s stance. This defines the eligible set:
W = {v ∈V \ H : d(ϕ(v), u) ≤δ}.

viewpoint is selected. To favor viewpoints that add
new stance information, we score each candidate

Ties are broken by smallest distance and then uni-

Distance and novelty serve complementary pur-
poses. The distance bound limits how far a view-
point may differ from the participant, measured
as the difference between overall ratings under
SCALAR and the L1 distance between concept-
level stance vectors under PSV. Novelty favors
viewpoints that introduce stance features not cov-
ered by the session history. Without novelty, the
policy could repeatedly select nearby viewpoints
with highly similar positions.
The stance representation determines how both
criteria operate. SCALAR encodes five overall po-
sitions and assigns one feature to each viewpoint,
making novelty binary. At any given distance from
the participant, at most two scalar positions are
possible. PSV represents each viewpoint through
five concept–stance pairs, allowing novelty to re-
flect which aspects of the issue have not yet been
encountered.
At the start of each session, an ε-greedy ban-
dit (Sutton and Barto, 2018) selects δ from the
distances available in that condition. The bound
remains fixed throughout all five viewpoints. The
bandit’s arm values are updated between sessions
using the depth of participants’ answers (Sec-
tion 4.3); responses do not change the sequence
within the current session.
Dialog system.
The policy runs inside a text-
based dialog system. Each turn presents a view-
point unchanged from the corpus, framed as the
view of a person identified by an arbitrary unisex
first name. Five names are used, with no further
information provided about these individuals. The
system then asks five fixed Socratic-style questions
(Danry et al., 2023; Ang et al., 2023) that scaffold
perspective-taking by asking the participant to artic-
ulate each position from the speaker’s point of view
(Reniers et al., 2022). They move from restating
the position, through the reasons and assumptions
behind it, to how it relates to the participant’s own
view (Appendix C). Participants answer in free text.
A session presents k = 5 viewpoints and therefore
25 written answers from each participant. The ban-
dit uses the depth (Section 4.3) of these answers
to update its arm values, informing the distance
bound selected for subsequent sessions.
4
User Study
4.1
Study Design and Procedure
We conducted a between-subjects study on Should
animal hunting be allowed?. The study was online.
Participants first completed a consent form, the
perspective-taking subscale of the Interpersonal
Reactivity Index (IRI-PT) (Davis, 1983), and wrote
their own view on the issue as free-text. Then they


interacted with the dialog system (Section 3.3),
followed by the reconstruction task (Section 4.3).
4.2
Participants
Participants were auto-balanced between the two
conditions. Of 79 participants, 63 completed the
study, 31 in SCALAR and 32 in PSV condition.
They were recruited through personal networks and
were not compensated. Conditions did not differ
on IRI-PT, gender, or age bracket (Appendix E).
4.3
Measures
Exposure (RQ1).
From session logs, we count
distinct exposure sequences and the concept–stance
pairs covered per session. We also replay the policy
over the full corpus for every possible participant
position.
Justification depth (RQ2).
To measure whether
participants gave reasons rather than merely restat-
ing a viewpoint, each written answer is scored from
0 (bare assertion) to 3 (two or more distinct rea-
sons), following Trénel (2004); Steenbergen et al.
(2003). This measure reflects the role of addressing
reasons in engaging with opposing positions (Kuhn
and Udell, 2007). Scores are averaged across the
five answers for each viewpoint and rescaled to
[0, 1].
Reconstruction accuracy (RQ2, RQ3).
After
each session, participants reconstruct one randomly
selected viewpoint by labeling each of its five con-
cepts as against, neutral, or for. We compare the
reconstructed profile ˆP with the true profile P us-
ing 1 −L1( ˆP, P)/(2N), yielding scores from 0 to
1, with chance at 1/3.
Target specificity (RQ3).
We score the recon-
struction of one randomly selected viewpoint
against all five viewpoints shown in the session.
Specificity is the score difference between the tar-
get and the other viewpoints, adjusted by the score
expected from a reconstruction with no knowledge
of the target. It therefore measures whether the
reconstruction identifies the intended viewpoint be-
yond what could be explained by how distinct the
session’s viewpoints are.
Concept-level relation (RQ3).
For each con-
cept, we classify the participant’s and viewpoint’s
stances as matching, opposing, or involving at least
one neutral stance. We also test whether errors on
differing concepts tend to reflect the participant’s
own stance, comparing observed errors with a per-
mutation null.
4.4
Analysis
Only the condition comparison of reconstruction
accuracy was pre-specified; all other analyses are
exploratory. We report estimates with confidence
intervals and apply Holm correction within test
families. Per-concept outcomes are analyzed using
binomial GEE with participant-clustered standard
errors, and justification depth using a mixed model
with participant random intercepts. The corpus
replay covers all participant positions and distance
bounds, with 100 seeds per setting.
5
Results
5.1
Concept-Level Stance Individualizes and
Broadens Exposure
RQ1 asks how concept-level stance representation
changes the viewpoints shown to participants. Fig-
ure 3 shows each viewpoint’s distance from the
participant in the representation used for selection.
Because the panels use different scales, we com-
pare counts rather than distances across conditions.
We count distinct distance sequences, each defined
by the five distances in presentation order; sessions
with the same distance sequence count as one even
if they show different viewpoints.
Sequences.
The 31 SCALAR sessions produced
6 distinct distance sequences, compared with 24
across the 32 PSV sessions. Under SCALAR, the
first viewpoint matched the participant’s stance in
all 31 sessions, the second was one step away in
all 31, and the third was one step away in 27. The
fourth and fifth returned to the participant’s stance
in 23 and 26 sessions, respectively. The sequence
is fully determined by the participant’s stance and
the distance bound, with each of the 9 observed
combinations producing exactly one sequence (Ap-
pendix F). Over the condition as a whole, 82 of
the 155 viewpoints shown (52.9%) sat at the par-
ticipant’s own stance. Under PSV the first view-
point matched the participant on all five concepts
in 20/32 sessions. The second sat at the furthest
distance the bound allowed in 27/32, after which
distance fell across the remaining slots, with means
of 7.1, 6.1, 3.6 and 2.5 at slots 2 to 5.
Coverage.
The issue has 15 concept–stance com-
binations, one for each of the three stances on each
of the five concepts, and a session covers those its


viewpoints take between them. PSV sessions cov-
ered 13.7 of the 15 on average (SD 2.2), against
11.0 under SCALAR (SD 2.4). All 15 appeared
in 22 of 32 PSV sessions and in 1 of 31 under
SCALAR.
Distance and coverage.
At each sequence posi-
tion, participant distance was strongly correlated
with the number of newly introduced concept–
stance combinations under both PSV (ρ = .72–.95)
and SCALAR (ρ = .73–1.00), where both measures
varied. Thus, more distant viewpoints tended to in-
troduce more new combinations, linking exposure
to disagreement with the novelty criterion used by
the policy.
Corpus replay.
To test whether these patterns
depended on the participants sampled, we replayed
the deployed policy across the full corpus for all
possible participant positions, 5 scalar ratings and
243 PSV profiles. We evaluated every distance
bound using 100 seeds. Each SCALAR rating–
bound combination produced exactly one sequence.
At the widest bound, PSV covered all 15 combina-
tions in every run, compared with a mean of 12.7
(SD 1.2) under SCALAR, which covered all 15 in
only 5% of runs (Appendix G). At the narrowest
bound, mean coverage was 9.1 under PSV and 8.3
under SCALAR; PSV selected the nearest unseen
viewpoint as a fallback in 52.5% of selections. The
corpus contained at most two scalar rating levels
at any given distance, compared with a median of
6–12 PSV profiles at each L1 distance from 4 to 8.
Overall, concept-level stance representation pro-
duced more varied distance sequences and broader
coverage of concept–stance combinations. The
distance bound constrained how far selected view-
points could differ from the participant, while nov-
elty shaped which viewpoints were selected within
that constraint.

5.3
Neutral Positions, Not Disagreement, Are
Hardest to Reconstruct
Participants were right on 55.6% of concepts where
they and the viewpoint took the same side (79/142),
51.2% where they took opposite sides (22/43), and
34.9% where at least one side was neutral (44/126),
against chance at 33.3% (Table 1). Accuracy was
higher where they agreed than where either side
was neutral (OR 2.09 [1.20, 3.65], Holm p = .029),
and no different where they disagreed (OR 1.19
[0.59, 2.42], p = .63). Disagreeing with a view-
point therefore did not make it harder to read.
The shortfall sat where the participant was neu-
tral, at 24% (11/45), below chance and below agree-
ment (OR 0.28 [0.13, 0.61], p = .001). Where only
the viewpoint was neutral it was 42% (22/53), no
different from agreement. Participant-neutral labels
were also the least stable when re-measured, 10 of
25 changing, so we treat this split as exploratory.
The pattern held in both conditions (p = .85)
and under offline re-measurement (OR 1.86, Holm
p = .048).

Discussion
We investigated whether the stance representation
a policy uses to select viewpoints changes what a
user encounters and what they take away from it.

stance at the concept level, rather than as a single
score, changes which viewpoints are selected. It
does. Both conditions ran the same selection policy,
