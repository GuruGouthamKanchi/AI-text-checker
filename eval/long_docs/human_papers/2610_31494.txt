Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) can perform a broad range of tasks across diverse settings, yet their
performance in agentic contexts remains poorly understood. Although online evaluations in simulators
and real-world environments are essential for measuring interactive agent performance, they are costly and
challenging to scale. Complementary offline VQA benchmarks can enable faster and more reliable comparisons
across agentic domains, yet existing benchmarks remain limited and fragmented. To address this gap, we
introduce AgentVQA, an offline benchmark for evaluating VLMs on realistic agentic scenarios across five agent
domains: Web Agents, Robotics, Egocentric Videos, Games, and Spatial Planning. We build AgentVQA by
manually selecting 1000 challenging, real-world agent examples from 23 source datasets and adapting them for
reliable offline evaluation. The resulting benchmark preserves realistic scenarios from interactive environments
across diverse domains, and its model rankings track those from corresponding online evaluations. Across the
22 evaluated models, the best achieves only 58.5% accuracy. Our cross-domain analysis shows that errors
are driven primarily by failures to ground and maintain accurate representations of the current and evolving
visual state, including localizing relevant UI elements, interpreting motion, tracking state changes over time,
and inferring 3D spatial relationships, rather than by task misinterpretation or downstream logical reasoning.

1 Introduction
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are quickly becoming the decision-making core for agentic systems spanning
robotics, web navigation, or gameplay. By prompting or fine-tuning, VLMs often outperform their domain-
specific counterparts (Koh et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Sarch et al.,
2025) and serve as the backbone for top-performing action models (Black et al., 2026, 2025; Ji et al., 2025).
In domains like mathematics and coding, standardized benchmarks have accelerated progress by systematically
tracking model capabilities and reliably exposing performance gaps (Jimenez et al., 2023; Balunović et al.,
2025). For agents, online evaluations in real or simulated environments remain the gold standard for assessing
agentic performance (Yao et al., 2024), but due to computational cost and reproducibility, they present
practical challenges for rapid, large-scale model comparison (Henderson et al., 2018; Dasari et al., 2022).
As a result, VLMs’ agentic capabilities lack comparable evaluation frameworks. State-of-the-art models are
often assessed on general-purpose benchmarks like MMMU (Yue et al., 2025), MMBench (Liu et al., 2024) and
GPQA (Rein et al., 2024). While these benchmarks assess broad vision-language and language understanding,
their questions and visual inputs do not reflect agentic decision-making scenarios. They primarily assess
general visual understanding or knowledge-intensive reasoning. Recent works (Yao et al., 2024; Yehudai
et al., 2025; Wong et al., 2025) show that performance on general-purpose VQA benchmarks does not reliably
correlate with success in agentic tasks.
To address this, several domain-specific benchmarks have advanced evaluation of agentic capabilities within
their respective settings (Koh et al., 2024; Rawles et al., 2024; Bonatti et al., 2025; Jang et al., 2024; Yang
et al., 2025b). However, these often provide narrow domain coverage, inputs and action spaces that do not
reflect real agentic scenarios, or evaluation sets that are either saturated or affected by noisy annotations.
We introduce AgentVQA, a manually curated benchmark for systematically evaluating general-purpose
VLMs on realistic agentic scenarios. AgentVQA contains 1,000 single-image, multi-frame, and video-based
multiple-choice questions curated from 23 datasets across five domains: Web Agents, Egocentric Videos,
Robotics, Games, and Spatial Planning. These domains represent distinct forms of agent–environment
interaction, including GUI navigation, temporally extended first-person understanding, embodied perception
and action, strategic decision-making, and 3D spatial planning, as illustrated in Figure 1. By evaluating these
settings within a common benchmark, AgentVQA supports both aggregate model comparison and analysis of
whether agentic capabilities generalize across domains.
We construct AgentVQA through a human-centered, model-assisted pipeline designed to provide broad
coverage, standardized evaluation, and meaningful difficulty. VLMs screen candidates and propose hard
distractors, while annotators select, repair, and verify every retained example. Only about 22% of the initial
candidates survive the full curation pipeline, as human review identifies subtle ambiguities and annotation
errors missed by automated checks and removes examples that are insufficiently challenging. The final
benchmark expresses heterogeneous targets, including coordinates, actions, spatial relations, and predicted
states, through a common multiple choice interface while preserving the semantics of the original tasks.
Model rankings on AgentVQA correlate with those from SpatialWorld (Gao et al., 2026), an online evaluation
of agentic capabilities in simulation, supporting its use as a scalable complement to interactive evaluation. At
the same time, its rankings differ substantially from those on broad visual and reasoning benchmarks such as
MMMU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond, and ChartQAPro, suggesting that performance on conventional benchmarks
does not reliably predict agentic competence. Across the 22 open- and closed-source models we evaluate, the
strongest model achieves only 58.5% accuracy. Moreover, the strongest model differs across domains, showing
that aggregate performance can obscure substantial variation in domain-specific capabilities.
Our cross-domain error analysis reveals a shared limitation that manifests differently across agentic settings.
Failures are dominated by grounded scene and state understanding: models struggle with UI localization in
Web Agents, motion interpretation in Egocentric Videos, 3D spatial reasoning in Robotics and Spatial
Planning, and visual state comprehension in Games. In contrast, failures arising from misunderstanding
the task or selecting an answer inconsistent with the model’s reasoning are rare. These results suggest that
current VLMs often understand the requested objective and can reliably reason over perceived inputs but fail
to construct and maintain the grounded representation of the environment needed to act correctly.

2 Related Work
VLM as agents. Recent advances in multimodal AI have been driven by the development of Vision-Language
Models (VLMs). These models, such as QwenVL (Bai et al., 2023), GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024), and
Gemini (Deepmind, 2026), are typically pretrained on vast, web-scale datasets of paired images and text.
This equips them with a broad general-purpose understanding of both visual concepts and natural language,
making them an ideal compute unit for agents. Several works have successfully adapted VLMs into specific
domains, either by fine-tuning or scaffolding pre-trained models (Black et al., 2024; Koh et al., 2024; Zhou
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Sarch et al., 2025). AgentVQA evaluates agentic capabilities across domains.
General visual question answering. A wealth of benchmarks have been developed to assess the general
capabilities of VLMs, including SEED-Bench (Li et al., 2023a), MMBench (Liu et al., 2024), or MMMU (Yue
et al., 2025). While invaluable for measuring core competencies and reasoning, they are ill-suited as proxies
for agentic intelligence.
Agentic tasks often require episodic memory (Majumdar et al., 2024) because
either the modalities are iterative, e.g., a stream of image observations, or the task requires interactive
reasoning, e.g., tool-calling. Yao et al. (2024) found that GPT-4o, despite excelling on traditional benchmarks,
achieves a success rate less than 50% on realistic customer service tasks requiring multi-turn interactions. In
Fig. 6, we confirm this observation by the divergence between rankings in AgentVQA vs MMMU-Pro and
GPQA-Diamond.
Agentic benchmarks.
The agentic evaluation landscape spans language-only benchmarks, e.g., SWE-
bench (Jimenez et al., 2023) and AgentBench (Liu et al., 2023), which test capabilities such as code generation
and tool use, and vision-language benchmarks requiring visual grounding and spatial reasoning. These
benchmarks can also be categorized as online or offline. Online benchmarks, e.g., (Savva et al., 2019; Fan
et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023b; Koh et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), evaluate agents through environment
interaction and therefore capture non-deterministic dynamics important for real-world deployment, but are
often costly and infrastructure-intensive (Henderson et al., 2018; Du et al., 2024). Offline benchmarks, e.g.,
(Rawles et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025a; Team et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2025), instead
use verifiable question-answer tasks paired with visual inputs. However, existing offline benchmarks are
largely domain-specific and differ in answer formats, option difficulty, and sample distributions, making direct
aggregation and cross-domain analysis difficult (Table 4). AgentVQA is curated by filtering and transforming
samples into a standardized, unambiguous multiple choice format with hard negatives.

3 The AgentVQA Benchmark
We define agentic tasks as those that evaluate action selection or the perceptual and reasoning capabilities
required for goal-directed interaction with an environment Bengio et al. (2025). To support scalable offline
evaluation of these capabilities, AgentVQA comprises 1,000 manually curated multiple-choice questions drawn
from 23 datasets spanning five agentic domains. Its construction is guided by three principles: comprehensive
coverage across domains to assess skill generality; standardized evaluation using manually curated questions
with unambiguous ground truth; and challenging examples, states, and negative options designed to expose
fine-grained weaknesses in current models.
To provide comprehensive coverage, AgentVQA spans five distinct forms of agent-environment interaction:
digital interaction and GUI navigation in Web Agents; embodied perception and action in Robotics;
temporally extended first-person planning in Egocentric Videos; strategic decision-making in Games; and
spatial navigation and planning in Spatial Planning. The benchmark is balanced across domains, with 200
questions per domain (Figure 2). Its inputs range from individual screenshots and embodied-scene images to
multi-frame trajectories, egocentric videos, and gameplay videos. Heterogeneous targets, including coordinates,
actions, spatial relations, and predicted states, are represented through a common multiple-choice format
while preserving observations of the agentic scenario. See Appendix B for full details of the datasets.
3.1 Data Curation and Construction
We construct AgentVQA through a five-stage, human-centered pipeline. VLMs assist with large-scale screening
and hard-negative generation, while human annotators determine which examples are retained and verify the
correctness, clarity, and difficulty of every final question.
Stage 1: Candidate collection. We assemble a diverse candidate pool from 23 source datasets spanning five
agentic domains (see Appendix B for full dataset list). Candidates are sampled across datasets, task types,
and input modalities to cover a broad range of realistic observations, states, and decisions, while initially
retaining their original questions, annotations, and answer formats.
Stage 2: MCQ construction. We convert the candidate tasks into a common multiple-choice format. For
examples that are not originally multiple choice, Gemini 2.5 Pro proposes three hard negative options using
the visual input, task prompt, correct answer, action history, and relevant task metadata. The generated
distractors include both near-miss negatives, which differ from the correct answer in a fine-grained way, and
semantically similar negatives, which correspond to plausible but incorrect objects, actions, or locations.
Stage 3: VLM-assisted screening. We use a multi-turn verification pipeline with Gemini and Claude
to identify converted questions with ambiguous instructions, inconsistent annotations, multiple plausible
answers, malformed states, implausible distractors, or other quality issues. This high-recall screening removes
approximately 45% of the initial candidate pool, reducing the amount of manual review required while
preserving human judgment for cases involving subtle visual or domain-specific ambiguities.

Human annotators then inspect the remaining candidates for
correctness, difficulty, diversity, and relevance to agentic decision-making. Approximately 60% of the samples
that pass automated screening are still rejected because they contain subtle ambiguities, annotation errors,

retaining them for the final evaluation format.

second time to ensure that exactly one answer is correct and that the distractors are plausible, challenging,
and unambiguously incorrect. This step along with Manual Curation is especially important for grounding
tasks, where bounding boxes and automatic spatial checks can admit false positives or reject valid interaction
points, as illustrated in Figure 4. We revise questions, annotations, and options as needed, then assemble a
balanced final benchmark of 1,000 manually verified questions, with 200 examples per domain and 40 or 50
examples from each source dataset.
4 Experiments
In this section, we investigate the ability of different models to perform agentic visual tasks across the diverse
inputs curated in AgentVQA. We first describe the evaluation setup and model suite in Section 4.1. We then
report overall model performance across domains, domain-level trends, action-history effects, open- versus
closed-source models, and reasoning variants in Section 4.2, compare AgentVQA rankings with existing offline
benchmarks in Section 4.3, and assess its reliability as a lower-cost alternative to online agent evaluation
through a comparison with SpatialWorld in Section 4.4. Finally, we examine the failure modes in Section 4.5.
4.1 Experimental Setup
Models. We evaluate a diverse suite of 22 prominent VLMs to ensure a comprehensive assessment of the
current landscape. Our selection includes closed-source models: GPT-5.6-sol thinking-high/none (OpenAI,
2026), GPT-5.6-luna thinking-high/none (OpenAI, 2026), GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024), Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google,
2026) and 3.7 Flash (Deepmind, 2026), Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic, 2026a) and Sonnet 5 (Anthropic, 2026b),
and Qwen3.7 Plus and Flash (Qwen Team, 2026b). We also include a wide range of open-source models:
the GLM series (4.6V (Z.AI, 2025), 4.5V (GLM-V Team, 2026), 4.1V-9B-Thinking/Base (GLM-V Team,
2026)), Kimi K3 (Team, 2026), Llama 4 Maverick (Meta, 2025), the Qwen3.5 series (397B-A17B, 122B-A10B,
35B-A3B, 27B) (Qwen Team, 2026a), and Qwen3.8 27B (Qwen Team, 2026c).

Evaluation setup. For models that do not natively support video input, we sample 32 frames at evenly spaced
intervals. This number is validated by our own ablation studies and prior work (Yang et al., 2025a). Models
are evaluated with a temperature of 0.8 and 0.2 for reasoning and non-reasoning models, respectively. For
models without manual temperature like GPT series, we use the default.
We use a standard prompt that provides the question and any relevant context, querying the model to output
only the single letter corresponding to its choice. For questions involving spatial coordinates, options are
formatted in each model’s native convention (e.g., normalized [0, 1000] in (y, x) order for Gemini, absolute
pixel coordinates for Qwen). Final answers are extracted programmatically, and all visual inputs are provided
at native resolution. Further details on prompts and answer extraction are in Appendices K and E.

4.2 Results on AgentVQA
We report the results on AgentVQA in Table 1. We draw the following conclusions:
Top models struggle with AgentVQA. Even the top performing model, Gemini 3.7 Flash, achieves only 58.5%
accuracy. A clear tiering is observable: the largest proprietary models (GPT-5.6, Gemini 3.7 Flash, Gemini
3.1 Pro) are at the top, followed by other competitive models such as Qwen3.7 Flash and the open-weight
Qwen3.8 27B, with smaller and older-generation models in the lower tiers. Among the five domains, models
perform best on Egocentric Videos and Games, followed by Spatial Planning, Robotics, and worst on Web
Agents. This indicates that current VLMs are stronger at temporally contextualized visual understanding
and game-state reasoning than at precise GUI grounding and spatial reasoning.
Large variance per domain. Current VLMs are strongest on temporally grounded understanding (Egocentric
Videos: 59%, Games: 51.5%) and weaker on precise grounding and embodied reasoning (Spatial Planning:
6


46%, Robotics: 38%, Web Agents: 29%). Web Agents is the hardest domain, with only three models
exceeding 40%. Games exhibits the largest spread (23% to 70%), suggesting strategic reasoning is strong
only in the most capable models. No single model dominates across all domains: Gemini 3.7 Flash leads
Games (70%) but falls behind GPT-5.6-sol thinking-high in Web Agents (40.5% vs. 47%), while Qwen3.7
Flash leads Egocentric Videos (72.5%) and Gemini 3.7 Flash leads Spatial Planning (62.5%) and Robotics
(52.5%). These cross-domain rank shifts indicate highly uneven capabilities across different settings.

Low correlation with existing benchmarks. To validate the benefit of a specialized agentic benchmark, we
compare model rankings for a subset of models on AgentVQA to those on a subset of MMMU-Pro (Yue et al.,
2025) (250 samples), ChartQA-Pro (Masry et al., 2025) (250 samples), and GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al.,

diverge substantially. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients are moderate for MMMU-Pro (𝜌= 0.67) and
GPQA-Diamond (𝜌= 0.59), and weak for ChartQA-Pro (𝜌= 0.44).
