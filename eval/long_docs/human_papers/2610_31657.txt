Cost-Aware Abstention for LLM-Based
IT Ticket Classification
Sachin Gupta
Independent Researcher
United States
sachinkg12@gmail.com
Abstract—Wrong automatic routing of an IT support ticket can
cost more than human triage. We evaluate cost-aware abstention
as an offline, cost-conditional readiness assessment: a routing
policy is selected on calibration data under asymmetric costs
and evaluated once on held-out test data. On a public 47,837-
ticket, eight-class corpus, we compare four trained classifiers with
four large language models under zero- and few-shot prompting.
With illustrative per-ticket costs of zero for a correct route, one
for human triage, and five for a wrong route, term-frequency–
inverse-document-frequency logistic regression selects a threshold
of 0.60 and reduces test cost from 0.763 to 0.472, a 38.1 %
relative reduction (95 % bootstrap confidence interval, 35.7 %–
40.4 %), while lowering wrong routes from 15.3 % to 3.5 %. In
a post-hoc 16-setting analysis, calibration-selected policies beat
both always-route and always-defer in 14 settings. The strongest
prompted configuration reaches 0.477 accuracy on a stratified
1,000-ticket subset, versus 0.879 for fine-tuned DistilBERT on the
same tickets. Isotonic recalibration reduces expected calibration
error but does not reliably improve ranking discrimination; one
GPT-4o-mini setting emits a single confidence value and has
correctness area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
of 0.500. At the break-even probability of 0.80, seven prompted
conditions defer every ticket, and Haiku few-shot routes 19 of
1,000. A language-model-only agreement control and margin- and
entropy-based rejection scores for the logistic-regression baseline
provide nontrivial comparators. The findings are scoped to this
dataset, prompt design, model endpoints, and illustrative costs;
the work is an evaluation framework, not a new abstention
algorithm or universal deployment certification.
Index Terms—trustworthy machine learning, calibration, cost-
aware abstention, selective prediction, large language models, text
classification, IT service management
I. INTRODUCTION
Enterprise IT service desks process tens of thousands of
internal tickets per month, where the “customer” is an em-
ployee asking internal IT for help with hardware, access, or
software (not an external consumer). HDI/MetricNet reports
the North American average cost per voice IT service-desk
ticket at US$17.19, with walk-up tickets averaging US$37.52
[1]. Routing happens under an asymmetric cost structure.
Sending a ticket to the correct queue is nearly free. Sending it
to a human triager is moderately costly. Sending it to the wrong
queue is much more expensive once you account for handoff
latency, double work, and missed service levels. Accuracy or
macro-F1 alone cannot represent this asymmetry or indicate
when to trust an automated decision [2], [3].
Two trends make this gap more pressing. First, large lan-
guage models (LLMs) can be prompted as zero- or few-shot
text classifiers [4], [5], potentially reducing the need for task-
specific training. Second, modern neural classifiers, including
LLMs, are known to be poorly calibrated. They often produce
high confidence values regardless of actual accuracy [6]–[9].
Together these trends raise a concrete operational question:
should a prompted LLM be allowed to auto-route IT tickets,
and if so, under what abstention policy?
We study this question on the public IT Service Ticket
Classification Dataset (47,837 tickets, eight categories). We
treat cost-aware abstention not as a way to improve a classifier
but as an empirical readiness test under stated assumptions.
Predictors, confidence estimators, rejection scores, and policies
remain separate. Our contributions are:
1) A comparison of four trained non-LLM classifiers and
eight prompted LLM conditions on the same public
corpus, including a common 1,000-ticket test subset.
2) A calibration analysis that reports expected calibration
error (ECE), correctness area under the receiver oper-
ating characteristic curve (AUROC), and tie-aware area
under the risk–coverage curve (AURC), plus separate
mixed (M=9) and LLM-only (M=8) agreement pools.
3) A calibration-selected, paired-bootstrap cost analysis
with top-probability, probability-margin, and entropy
rejection scores; a post-hoc 16-setting cost-parameter
analysis; and LLM policies that use a cost-derived
threshold rather than test tuning.
4) A reproducible evaluation framework and per-ticket ar-
tifacts that expose prediction, confidence, rejection, and
cost as separately auditable stages.
The main observation is simple: abstention does not repair a
weak classifier. It exposes when auto-routing is cost-preferred
under a particular dataset, confidence signal, and cost model.
Positioning. The components we combine are individu-
ally established: Chow-style error-reject [3], Elkan-style cost-
sensitive decision-making [10], ECE-based calibration diag-
nostics [6], [11], isotonic recalibration, and self-consistency-
inspired agreement signals [12]. We introduce no new absten-
tion algorithm. This paper’s contribution is an empirical evalu-
ation of deployment-relevant assumptions and its reproducible
implementation, not a universal readiness certificate.

II. RELATED WORK
Selective prediction and abstention. The classical frame-
work for rejecting uncertain inputs traces back to Chow’s
error-reject trade-off [3]. It has been formalized for modern
classifiers under the names selective prediction [2], [13] and
learning with abstention [14]; a recent survey organizes LLM
abstention methods, benchmarks, and evaluation metrics [15].
The closest line is learning to defer, where a classifier is
trained jointly with the option to pass an instance to a human
expert [16], [17]. Our training-free confidence-threshold policy
addresses the same route-or-defer action space without joint
training. We evaluate its decisions under an explicit cost model
following Elkan’s cost-sensitive formulation [10].
Calibration and overconfidence. Guo et al. [6] docu-
mented systematic overconfidence in deep neural classifiers
and popularized Expected Calibration Error (ECE) [11] as
a diagnostic. A growing literature studies whether LLMs
are well-calibrated [7]–[9], [18]. The broad finding is that
verbalized confidence runs too high. We contribute a public-
dataset measurement of LLM ECE in an asymmetric-cost
classification setting, and report agreement-across-predictors
as a confidence signal inspired by self-consistency [12].
LLMs for text classification. Off-the-shelf prompting of
LLMs as classifiers [4], [5], [19] is operationally attractive,
but on short, lexical-signal-heavy tasks strong classical text
classifiers such as linear SVMs [20] remain highly competi-
tive, as our IT-ticket results confirm (§V-A). Cost-driven LLM
cascades and routing lower inference cost by escalating only
hard inputs to a stronger model [21]; we instead hold the model
fixed and use abstention to decide when not to auto-route at
all. Our evaluation lands in the same mixed picture for the
IT-ticket domain.
IT ticket classification. Automated routing of support
tickets has a long applications-track history [22], [23] but is
rarely evaluated under explicit cost models or with abstention.
Our framework is structured as a deferral layer that could be
integrated into an IT service management pipeline rather than
as a new classifier.
III. METHOD
A. Problem setup
Let X be the space of ticket texts and Y a set of K disjoint
downstream queues. A base classifier h : X →Y emits a
label. The policy-wrapped decision d : X →Y ∪{⊥} either
routes h(x) or abstains (⊥); abstained tickets go to a human
triager. Each decision incurs one of three costs: cok (correct
auto-route), ctriage (human triage), and cwrong (incorrect auto-
route). The expected per-ticket cost is
E[c] = P(⊥)ctriage + P(correct, ¬⊥)cok
+ P(wrong, ¬⊥)cwrong.
(1)
Our primary analysis uses (cok, ctriage, cwrong) = (0, 1, 5).
These values are illustrative rather than calibrated to any
single organization, but they encode the qualitative ordering
that motivates abstention: a misroute is costlier than a human
triage because it still incurs the triage after a delayed hand-off
plus rework, whereas a correct auto-route is effectively free.
Because the ratio is an assumption rather than a measured
quantity, every deployment conclusion is conditional on it.
Equation (2) shows how local costs determine the routing
threshold; an organization must estimate its own triage and
misroute costs before use. As a post-hoc sensitivity analy-
sis, we also cross ctriage ∈{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2} with cwrong ∈
{2, 5, 10, 20} while fixing cok = 0. The (2, 2) pair is an equal-
cost boundary; the other 15 pairs retain cwrong > ctriage.

C. Confidence and rejection scores
We evaluate probability estimates and ranking signals sep-
arately.
Model-reported. For a non-LLM baseline this is the maxi-
mum class-probability estimate returned by that model: multi-
nomial probabilities for logistic regression, sigmoid-calibrated
outputs for the SVM, tree-vote fractions for the random forest,
and softmax for DistilBERT. For LLMs it is the confidence
field parsed from the JSON output.
Cross-predictor agreement. Given M predictors evaluated
on the same ticket, the agreement-confidence for the primary
predictor is the fraction of the M predictors that emit the
same label. No held-out labels are used to compute this score
at application time. In our mixed configuration M=9: the
four LLMs in zero-shot and few-shot settings (eight prompted
variants) plus the classical TF-IDF + LR baseline. Because
this pool mixes the eight LLM variants with the stronger
classical baseline, an LLM’s agreement partly reflects con-
cordance with a more accurate supervised predictor, not peer
consensus alone. We therefore also report an LLM-only M=8
control. Operational trade-off: agreement requires running all
M predictors at inference. Because the pool is heterogeneous,
its dollar cost and latency depend on the endpoints and are
not necessarily M× those of the primary alone. Isotonic
incurs one calibration-time pass per prompted predictor and
a constant-time lookup at inference.
Isotonic regression on a held-out calibration split. LLM-
emitted confidence fields are bounded in [0, 1] but are not
logits, so parametric Platt or temperature scaling do not apply
cleanly. We instead fit one isotonic regression per prompted
LLM condition on the calibration split: pairs {(ci, 1[ˆyi =
yi])} of raw confidence and ground-truth correctness, mapped
via sklearn.isotonic.IsotonicRegression [28]
(monotone non-decreasing, output clipped to [0, 1]) into a
recalibration c 7→ˆPr(correct). For each LLM predictor we
fit on a stratified 500-ticket subsample of the calibration split
(4,000 calibration queries across the 8 prompted variants).
Calibration and test splits are disjoint: the isotonic mapping
is learned only from calibration pairs and applied unchanged
to test confidences. ECE is computed with 10 equal-width bins
on test predictions.
Discrimination metrics. ECE measures probability-level cal-
ibration but not whether confidence ranks correct predictions
above errors. We therefore report correctness AUROC (ties
receive half credit) and area under the risk–coverage curve
(AURC; lower is better). Since LLM scores contain many
ties, our AURC adds each equal-score group as a block and
integrates the right-continuous risk step, avoiding arbitrary
ordering within ties.
Nontrivial rejection comparators. For TF-IDF + LR we also
use the top-two probability margin p(1) −p(2) and normalized
inverse entropy 1 −H(p)/ log K. Each score’s threshold is
selected on calibration over a 0.01-spaced grid in [0, 1] and
then evaluated once on test.

For each post-hoc cost-sensitivity setting, we select on

pcok + (1 −p)cwrong ≤ctriage,
(2)
which gives the fixed break-even threshold p ≥0.80 under
(0, 1, 5). Raw verbalized scores are not treated as probabilities
for policy optimization.

B. Is the confidence signal useful?
Every prompted LLM is overconfident: raw ECE spans
[0.332, 0.594], and GPT-4o-mini few-shot assigns confidence
1.000 to every ticket despite 0.459 accuracy. Isotonic regres-
sion fitted on the disjoint calibration subset reduces LLM ECE
to [0.014, 0.049], an 86–98 % relative reduction.
That ECE result must not be read as improved error ranking.
Isotonic is monotone and changes correctness AUROC by
at most 0.007 in these runs; AURC also does not improve
consistently (Table III). The limiting example is GPT-4o-mini
few-shot: raw confidence is the constant 1.000, isotonic maps it
to the constant 0.480, and both correctness AUROC and AURC
remain 0.500 and 0.541. Isotonic corrects the probability level
by mapping a non-discriminative score to the base accuracy;
it does not create information that was absent from the score.
Agreement can carry ranking signal, but the voter pool mat-
ters. Table IV separates the mixed M=9 pool from the LLM-
only M=8 control. The M=8 correctness AUROC spans
0.590–0.773, descriptive evidence that peer-only agreement
contains ranking signal on these data. Its ECE also improves
over raw confidence in all eight conditions, but ECE alone
would not establish discrimination. The mixed pool is better
in every listed ECE and AUROC cell, but it includes the
supervised TF-IDF + LR vote. Thus M=9 uses no held-out
calibration labels when a score is computed, yet it is not free
of supervised information. It also requires all predictors at
inference.
