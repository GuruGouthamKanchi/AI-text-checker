Abstract
Accurate hippocampal segmentation can be a useful tool for diagnosing and monitoring neurological conditions such as
Alzheimer’s disease and epilepsy. While numerous automated segmentation methods exist, their clinical adoption remains
limited. Reliable uncertainty assessment can enhance trust and facilitate clinical translation. This study evaluates
ensembles of five heterogeneous hippocampal segmentation methods — InnerEye, ASHS, FastSurfer, HippoSeg, and
FreeSurfer — across two dementia datasets and one epilepsy dataset. The sub-ensemble containing InnerEye, FastSurfer,
and HippoSeg emerged as both accurate and efficient, highlighting the feasibility of balancing computational cost and
performance. Additionally, ensemble-derived uncertainty quantification with sample variance, mutual information, and
predictive entropy is shown to reduce inaccurate segmentations by flagging low-confidence cases, potentially providing
a mechanism for automatically escalating ambiguous cases for expert assessment. Lastly, this study explores the
downstream clinical utility of ensemble-derived uncertainty, finding a significant correlation with motion artefact severity
and a modest contribution to Alzheimer’s disease classification.

1. Introduction
Hippocampal volume changes are associated with several
neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s and epilepsy (Jack,
1994; Duan et al., 2020). Accurate volumetric measure-
ment can serve as a biomarker for diagnosis and disease
monitoring (Schuff et al., 2008; Briellmann et al., 2002).
While manual segmentation by an expert is still consid-
ered the gold standard, its time-consuming nature and the
well-documented intra- and inter-reader variability remain
significant limitations (Boccardi and et al., 2015; Geuze
et al., 2005). Although a concerted effort has taken place
to establish a harmonised protocol for manual hippocampal
segmentation (Frisoni and Jack, 2015), multiple different
protocols for hippocampal segmentation exist, complicating
the notion of a ground truth segmentation (Geuze et al.,
2005).
Numerous methods for automatic hippocampus seg-
mentations have been proposed, ranging from atlas-based
approaches (Dill et al., 2015), to deep learning methods
(Schroder et al., 2023). Despite the range of available
methods, clinical adoption remains limited. Key concerns
hindering the clinical translation of these tools include the
risk of silent failure, and a lack of robustness to unex-
pected distribution shifts. Reliable uncertainty estimation
can play a central role in strengthening the trustworthi-
ness of these algorithms, e.g. by escalating uncertain cases
for human review, and various approaches for assessing
predictive uncertainty have been explored in the literature
(Abdar and et al., 2020). While Bayesian neural networks
offer the most principled framework for posterior approxi-
mation, they are often computationally prohibitive (Abdar
and et al., 2020). Monte-Carlo Dropout and ensembles
have emerged as practical methods for Bayesian approxima-
tion (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2016; Gal and Ghahramani,
2015). However, these approaches differ substantially in
computational cost. Monte-Carlo sampling increases in-
ference time through repeated stochastic forward passes,
while ensemble-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) re-
quires running multiple models or segmentation pipelines,
making inference cost scale with ensemble size. This cre-
ates an important trade-off between uncertainty reliabil-
ity, scalability, and computational efficiency, particularly
when methods are deployed across large imaging cohorts or
resource-constrained clinical environments. Beyond provid-
ing uncertainty estimation, ensemble methods consistently
achieve superior performance over individual models, as
repeatedly demonstrated by results from the Brain Tumor
Image Segmentation Benchmark (BRATS) (Menze and
et al., 2015) and the White Matter Hyperintensity Seg-
mentation Challenge (Kuijf and et al., 2019). Ensemble
predictions can be aggregated through multiple strategies,
with stacking of heterogeneous models proving highly ef-
fective and simple averaging providing a stable method for
integrating their predictions (Müller et al., 2022). Although
deep ensembles often outperform single models, their com-
bined outputs are not automatically well-calibrated, even
when each constituent network is well-calibrated on its own
(Wu and Gales, 2021; Rahaman and Thiery, 2021). Cali-
bration deteriorates further under distribution shift (Ovadia
et al., 2019), a problem that has been traced in part to in-
sufficient diversity among ensemble members (Javanbakhat
et al., 2024). To counter this, the present study assesses the
disagreement of deliberately heterogeneous segmentation
methods and treats that disagreement itself as an uncer-
tainty estimate, a strategy previously explored by Kofler
et al. (2021).
Another growing concern is the computational cost and
energy consumption of the segmentation methods. Recent
work has highlighted the potential environmental impact
of large-scale deep learning models (Strubell et al., 2020;
Wu and et al., 2022), emphasizing the need for sustainable
solutions. High computational demands not only contribute
to a significant carbon footprint, but also limit accessibility
for hospitals and research institutions with limited resources.
Addressing both the need for reliable uncertainty estimation
and computational efficiency will be conducive for enabling
broader clinical adoption.
Beyond performance accuracy, ensembling methods may
prove useful to provide robust uncertainty estimates that
can be employed for downstream clinical tasks. One such
application relates to quality control and particularly mo-
tion artefact detection. There are a number of sources
that can lead to motion artefacts in brain magnetic reso-
nance imaging (MRI), including patient movement, invol-
untary physiological motion (e.g., respiration and cardiac
pulsation), swallowing, and flow of fluids such as blood or
cerebrospinal fluid (Zaitsev et al., 2015). Motion artefacts
can negatively impact image quality and lead to erroneous
interpretations, in particular in acquisitions with low signal-
to-noise ratio (Havsteen et al., 2017). Accurately detecting
and correcting motion-related image degradation has long
been a challenge in medical image processing (Zaitsev et al.,
2015). While both traditional machine learning and deep-
learning-based methods have demonstrated strong perfor-
mance (Vakli et al., 2023), their effectiveness often does
not generalise well across vendors or acquisition protocols.
Segmentation uncertainty, which tends to increase as image
quality degrades, may therefore offer a complementary sig-
nal for flagging motion-corrupted scans, with previous work
showing that ensemble-derived uncertainties are particularly
useful for quality control (Ng et al., 2023).
A further potential application of uncertainty estima-
tion in the context of hippocampal segmentation is disease
classification. Previous work has shown that hippocampal

1.1 Contributions
Our contributions are three-fold. First, we provide a com-
prehensive benchmark of five heterogeneous hippocampal
segmentation methods, evaluating their accuracy, calibra-
tion, and carbon footprint across two dementia cohorts and
one epilepsy cohort. From this benchmark, we identify a
resource-efficient triad — InnerEye, FastSurfer, and Hip-
poSeg — that matches the full ensemble’s Dice performance
while reducing inference CO2 emissions by approximately
82%, and show that simple averaging of probabilistic out-
puts yields the best integration strategy. Second, we demon-
strate that uncertainty estimates derived from the triad’s
probabilistic outputs can support automated quality control:
aggregated uncertainty successfully flags gross segmenta-
tion failures across datasets, and correlates significantly
with motion artefact severity, suggesting a mechanism for
automatically escalating problematic cases for expert review.
Third, we investigate the clinical utility of ensemble-derived
uncertainty for disease classification, and show that combin-
ing uncertainty metrics with hippocampal volume features
improves discrimination between cognitively normal and
Alzheimer’s disease groups.

for ASHS we used the Penn Memory Center 3T ASHS Atlas

2.3 Processing
Inference for the ADNI dataset and MR-ART was performed
on a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster. For car-
bon footprint benchmarking, the data was processed on
a dedicated node equipped with 4 NVIDIA GTX 1080 Ti
GPUs, an Intel Xeon E5-2640 v4 CPU (2.40GHz), and
64GB of RAM, with no concurrent jobs from other users.
The local hospital data was processed entirely on a local
MacBook Pro (Intel Core i5-5257U CPU @ 2.70 GHz, with
∼8GB of memory) due to data sharing limitations in ethics.
For fair comparison, same software versions were installed
on the cluster and on the local machine. An older ver-
sion of FreeSurfer (v6.0.0) was selected to accommodate
the memory constraints of the local machine. FastSurfer
(v2.3.3) was run from a Docker image on the local machine,
and via Singularity (v3.8.5-2.el7) on the reserved cluster
node. ASHS (v2.0.0, July 2018) was run natively on the
respective host system. Because epilepsy can cause uni-
lateral hippocampal atrophy the left and right hippocampi
were processed separately to avoid averaging out potential
differences in segmentation performance. This separation
allows for evaluating whether some methods underperform
specifically on the atrophied side. Energy consumption and
time to completion was tracked with the Python package
Codecarbon (v2.8.3). Carbon tracking was performed in
machine mode on the dedicated cluster node, using the
United Kingdom country-level grid intensity estimate avail-
able to CodeCarbon for the location of the datacenter. The
reported values therefore represent inference emissions only,
including CPU, GPU, and RAM energy during runtime,
and do not include model training, data storage or trans-
fer, hardware manufacturing, or other lifecycle emissions.
CodeCarbon recorded a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of
1.0, so no additional data-centre cooling or infrastructure

2.5 Ensemble performance & calibration
Segmentation performance was evaluated using the Dice
Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and the 95th percentile Haus-
dorff distance (HD95), predictive uncertainty with sample
variance, mutual information, and entropy, while uncertainty
calibration was evaluated in terms of expected calibration
error. As initial benchmarking, the five individual segmenta-
tion methods, all possible triads formed from them, and the
full ensemble of five methods were compared on the ADNI
dataset. The same ADNI dataset was therefore used both
to identify the best-performing triad and to report its ADNI
performance. Consequently, any performance advantage
of the selected triad over other sub-ensembles on ADNI
should be interpreted as an in-sample estimate that may be
partially inflated by selection on the same data. The Local
Dementia and Local Epilepsy datasets were not used for
triad selection and therefore provide the held-out assessment
of cross-domain segmentation performance and calibration.
For each triad and for the full ensemble, the final prediction
mask was obtained via majority voting. Performance was as-
sessed in terms of DSC, ECE, and estimated CO2 emissions.
The expected calibration error (ECE) of each sub-ensemble
was approximated by interpreting the number of votes from
the T = 3 ensemble members as discrete confidence levels.
Specifically, if n models predicted the class hippocampus for
a voxel v, the ensemble confidence was defined as pv = n
T .
For the winning triad, minor changes to their code were in-
troduced to enable saving of the probabilistic outputs. The
relevant amended scripts are publicly available on GitHub:
https://github.com/zfdiwkm/multi-software-uncertainty.
2.5.1 Ensemble integration strategy
Next, we compared different strategies for integrating the
predictions of the best-performing triad to assess their im-
pact on segmentation accuracy and uncertainty calibration.
These ensemble strategies were: (1) simple averaging of the
output probabilities from each ensemble member (denoted
as ‘Ensemble Averaged’ in the results), (2) weighting each
prediction by the model’s confidence (‘Ensemble Weighted’),
and (3) assigning each voxel to the prediction of the most
confident model in a winner-takes-all approach (‘Ensemble
Triage’). Due to non-normality of studied distributions,
Friedman tests were performed to assess significant differ-
ence in performance. To account for multiple comparisons,
p-values were adjusted with the Holm-Bonferroni method.
Resource consumption was estimated in terms of duration
and CO2 expenditure for each method and each subject.
2.6 Uncertainty-based quality control
We investigated whether ensemble-derived uncertainty can
support automated quality control, both by identifying
unreliable segmentation outputs and by flagging low image
quality cases.
2.6.1 Silent failure detection
We evaluated whether hippocampus-wide aggregated un-
certainty could serve as a predictor of low-quality seg-
mentations. To this end, uncertainty values were aggre-
gated across the region of interest (ROI), defined as the
union of predicted segmentations with a one-voxel mar-
gin.
Morphological dilation was performed once, with
scipy.ndimage.binary_dilation with a square connec-
tivity = 1 (6 connected kernel). The hippocampi with the
highest uncertainty were excluded, and the resulting impact
on segmentation performance was assessed to determine

2.6.2 Motion artefact detection
We further assessed whether uncertainty correlates with im-
age quality in the presence of motion artefacts. Using the
MR-ART dataset, uncertainty metrics were computed for
each hippocampus and grouped by the image quality score
(1 = good, 2 = medium, 3 = bad) assigned by expert neu-
roradiologists. To test for a monotonic relationship between
quality score and uncertainty, a within-subject permutation
test was performed (10,000 permutations), using Spear-
man’s rank correlation as the test statistic. Quality score
labels were permuted within each subject to preserve the
repeated-measures structure. Pairwise comparisons (Score
2 vs 1; Score 3 vs 2) were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-
rank tests on the subset of subjects with scans in both
categories being compared (n = 37 and n = 43 subjects,
respectively). For each subject, uncertainty values were av-
eraged across hippocampi within each quality category prior
to testing. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons
using the Holm-Bonferroni method, applied across all six
pairwise tests (3 metrics × 2 comparisons).
2.7 Uncertainty for Alzheimer’s disease classification
We investigated whether ensemble-derived uncertainty could
improve classification of Alzheimer’s disease from hippocam-
pal features. From the ADNI baseline stratified cohort, 150
subjects were selected with balanced representation across
diagnostic groups (50 were cognitively normal, 50 with mild
cognitive impairment, and 50 with Alzheimer’s disease).
Hippocampal volumes were extracted from each segmenta-
tion method and normalised by intracranial volume (ICV),
with ICV estimates provided by HIPPOSEG. Voxel-wise
uncertainty metrics (sample variance, mutual information,
and predictive entropy) were aggregated as the mean across
the hippocampal ROI for each hemisphere, then averaged
across hemispheres to yield subject-level features. This
scalar aggregation was chosen to keep the feature space
small relative to the cohort size and to reduce the risk of
overfitting that would be introduced by higher-dimensional
spatial uncertainty features. Age and sex were included
as covariates. To assess the discriminative power brought
by uncertainty estimates we compared models including

3. Results
3.1 Ensemble performance & calibration
3.1.1 Benchmarking cost and performance
The ensemble consisting of InnerEye, FastSurfer, and Hip-
poSeg produced segmentations at the same level of accuracy
as an ensemble consisting of all five softwares, while sub-
stantially reducing carbon emissions (from 4.65 kg to 0.85
kg CO2eq for processing the 30 ADNI patients) and retain-
ing a comparably well-calibrated uncertainty measure (Fig.
2). For the remainder of the paper, any mention of ’Triad’
will therefore refer to this specific ensemble.
3.1.2 Ensemble integration strategy
Simple mean averaging of the ensemble members’ proba-
bilistic outputs yielded the highest Dice performance across
all datasets (Fig. 3, p < 0.05 after Holm-Bonferroni cor-
rection). For HD95, no significant difference was observed
between the mean ensemble and other ensemble integration
strategies on the epilepsy dataset. On the Local Demen-
tia dataset, the mean ensemble did not significantly differ
from InnerEye, while it significantly outperformed all other
methods (Fig. 3, p < 0.05, Holm-Bonferroni corrected).
3.1.3 Uncertainty calibration
The averaged probabilistic output by the Triad members
produced the best calibrated confidences, with an ECE of
0.05 across all three datasets (Fig. 4). The second most
calibrated segmentation method was HippoSeg, with an
ECE slightly higher than 0.05 averaged across all datasets.
InnerEye (ECE ≈0.08) and FastSurfer in particular (ECE
≈0.16) were less well-calibrated.
3.2 Uncertainty-based quality control
3.2.1 Silent failure detection
Applying dataset-specific uncertainty thresholds demon-
strated varying effectiveness across UQ metrics and datasets
(Fig. 3). For Dice-based outlier detection (Dice < 0.82),
entropy achieved the highest performance with 91.7% recall
and 44.2% precision, while variance and mutual information
both achieved 83% recall with ∼35% precision. For HD95-
based outlier detection (HD95 > 2.5mm), entropy again
performed best with 88.9% recall and 40.6% precision, com-
pared to variance and mutual information achieving 55.6%
recall with 28.9% and 30.6% precision, respectively.
3.2.2 Motion artifact detection
A significant monotonic relationship was observed between
image quality score and uncertainty across all three metrics
(permutation test, p < 0.01; Spearman ρ = 0.30–0.36).
Pairwise comparisons showed that uncertainty was signif-
icantly higher for clinically unusable scans (score 3) com-
pared to medium quality scans (score 2) for all metrics
(Wilcoxon signed-rank, p < 0.05, Holm-adjusted; Fig. 5).
However, the difference between good (score 1) and medium
(score 2) quality scans was not significant (p = 0.18). En-
tropy showed the strongest effect, with the largest corre-
lation coefficient (ρ = 0.36) and most significant pairwise
difference (p < 0.001).
3.3 Uncertainty for Alzheimer’s disease classification
Adding uncertainty metrics to hippocampal volume features
did modestly improve classification of Alzheimer’s disease.
For CN vs AD classification, the volume-only baseline (total
intracranial volume (TIV)-normalized bilateral hippocampal
volume with age and sex covariates) achieved an AUC
of 0.81 ± 0.04. The best-performing model (Full_mean,
logistic regression) achieved AUC 0.87 ± 0.03, representing
a modest improvement of +0.06 (permutation test, p <
0.001; Fig. 6). In practical terms, using the Full_mean
feature set led to one additional subject (out of 49) being
correctly identified as AD and four additional subjects (out
of 48) being correctly identified as CN (Fig. 7). Adding any
individual uncertainty feature to volume (Vol+UQ variants)
provided no benefit over volume alone (∆AUC ≈0, p >
0.05). For CN vs MCI (baseline AUC 0.73) and MCI vs
AD (baseline AUC 0.70) classifications, uncertainty features
consistently were unable to improve predictive performance
(supplementary figures S1 to S4).
