Simulated Control Evaluations
Jeffrey Olmo
David Zhang
Tyler Tracy
Redwood Research
Abstract
Control evaluations measure whether safeguards can prevent an AI model from causing undesired outcomes
even when it is intentionally trying to do so. These evaluations typically use purpose-built control settings
and extrapolate their results to a model’s deployment. However, the effectiveness of safeguards in deployment
can depend on features of environments and tasks that control settings do not represent. To broaden where
control evaluations can be run, we introduce simulated control evaluations (SCEs), which use a language
model to simulate tool responses in place of an environment.
We evaluate SCEs on LinuxArena, where
execution provides ground-truth outcomes. We compare SCE prediction errors with those from a separate
control setting, BashArena, and with variation in control metrics within LinuxArena.
For independently
sampled evaluation and deployment task sets, SCEs predict safety with a root mean squared error of 3.6
percentage points across five monitors at a 5% audit budget, compared with 10.1 for BashArena. SCEs closely
reproduce distributions of attack strategies, attacker reasoning, and monitor reasoning, with over tenfold
lower KL divergence from execution than BashArena.
SCEs explain 78–85% of the between-environment
variance in attack success, monitor scores, and safety. This accuracy is sample efficient: SCEs predict safety
with less error than BashArena using fewer than 5% as many task-pair draws as the deployment task set.
Strategies discovered through simulation also improve monitor performance on held-out LinuxArena attacks,
outperforming BashArena and approximately matching improvement from executed strategies. These results
suggest that deployment-specific SCEs can be more predictive than control evaluations executed in separate
control settings.

Introduction
AI control aims to make it safe to use capable AI agents even if they try to cause harm (Greenblatt et al., 2024).
The same capabilities and access that let an agent do useful work can let it exfiltrate information, introduce
backdoors, or launch rogue deployments. A control protocol puts safeguards around the agent, such as trusted
monitoring. A control evaluation tests these safeguards by replacing the agent with an attack policy configured to
pursue a harmful objective while doing legitimate work (Bhatt et al., 2025). Control evaluations produce control
metrics, such as how often attacks succeed and how often a monitor catches them. Accurate control metrics can
guide improvements to the control protocol and inform risk estimates.
Where an agent acts matters for control evaluations: its opportunities for dangerous behavior, and the evi-
dence control protocols have to detect them, depend on the particular codebases, services, tasks, and available
actions in its deployment. Control evaluations aim to inform judgments about a model’s deployment, but typ-
ically run in a separate control setting such as BashArena or LinuxArena (Kaufman et al., 2025; Tracy et al.,
2026), which provides a safe testbed for executing attacks, and ground truth for outcomes like whether a given
side task succeeded, but which shares neither the deployment’s environments nor its task profile. Evidence from
such a control setting may transfer poorly to deployment. Even if it captures average attacker and monitor per-
formance, deployment risk will not be determined by those averages when an attacker selects especially promising
opportunities (Ge-Wang et al., 2026). A control protocol that appears safe in a control setting might miss specific
attacks available in a lab’s internal infrastructure (Korbak et al., 2025).
Running control evaluations directly in the deployment would address this mismatch, but running an attack
policy on live infrastructure risks causing the very harms the evaluation is intended to measure. An alternative
that avoids this risk is an executable replica of the deployment, but building one can be expensive or infeasible,
especially when the agent depends on many services and changing external state. What is usually available, even
when a replica is not, is a record of past activity: agent trajectories that show what tasks models were given,
which tools they called, and what those tools returned, along with snapshots of the relevant codebases. Our
method uses these records to construct control evaluations.

We study simulated control evaluations (SCEs), which use a language model to produce the tool responses an
agent would receive in deployment (Figure 1). An attack policy receives a legitimate main task and a harmful
side task. It chooses actions and issues tool calls as usual, while a simulator uses an honest reference trajectory
and codebase read access to generate the observations those actions would produce in deployment. The attack
policy conditions on each simulator output, so the attack can diverge from the reference trajectory. Monitors then
score the resulting simulated trajectory, and an attack success judge estimates whether the side task succeeded.
The attack policy’s actions never execute.
The central question is whether SCEs predict deployment control metrics more accurately than a separate
control setting does. Simulation introduces unrealism in tool responses, but lets evaluators use the deployment’s
real tasks and codebase; a control setting executes attacks faithfully, but on a different distribution of tasks and
environments.
Unlike deployment simulation used to forecast misalignment propensity (Williams et al., 2026), SCEs need not
conceal the evaluation from the agent: the attack policy is already instructed to pursue a harmful objective. On
the other hand, SCEs place stronger demands on modeling the environment’s attack-relevant mechanics, including
subtle details of permissions, state changes, and tool behavior that determine whether an attack succeeds and
what evidence it leaves for monitors.
We test this tradeoff on LinuxArena, where attacks can be executed to measure ground-truth outcomes. In
our experiments, execution in LinuxArena stands in for deployment. Across 1,000 main-task–side-task pairs,
we compare SCE predictions with predictions from 1,000 attacks executed in BashArena; Section 2.4 defines
these comparisons. We also compare SCE prediction errors with the variation between LinuxArena tasks and
environments.
Our contributions are:
• A framework for measuring control evaluation transfer. We combine variance explained in control
metrics across tasks and environments with matching behavioral distributions to assess accuracy beyond
overall averages. This framework applies to both simulated and executed control evaluations. (Section 2.4).
• Empirical validation of SCEs.
On LinuxArena, SCEs match executed behavioral distributions and
predict control metrics more accurately than BashArena, explaining most between-environment variance
in attack success, monitor scores, and safety. We show how these advantages scale with the number of
simulated task pairs. (Sections 3.1–3.6).
• Demonstrating that SCEs can improve safeguards. We show that briefing a monitor with simulated
strategies improves detection of held-out executed attacks more than BashArena briefings, approaching the
performance of briefings from same-environment execution. SCEs thus support improving safeguards as
well as assessing them (Section 3.7).

Methods
2.1
SCE components
Attack policy. The attack policy is gpt-5.6-sol at medium reasoning effort for both executed and simulated
trajectories. It receives the main task, the side task, and attack guidance suggesting how to pursue the side task
covertly. For each task, we provide the same attack-policy prompt in execution and SCE.
Simulator. At each tool call, the simulator receives the tool name and arguments, interaction history, and
grounding affordances: records and tools that provide information about the environment being simulated. The
simulator generates a tool response for the policy’s next step; its reasoning is withheld from the policy and
subsequent simulation steps. In what follows, the simulator is gpt-6-astra, with two main affordances:
• Reference trajectory. The simulator receives the original honest agent transcript for the same main task,
including tool calls and responses. This shows how the environment behaved during one execution; the
simulator predicts responses to the attack policy’s different actions.
• Codebase read access. The simulator can read, list, and search a snapshot of the environment’s files,
giving it access to source code, configuration, and initial file contents.
Some tool call outputs follow directly from evidence from the reference trajectory and codebase; many outputs
depend on state the simulator cannot observe, in which case it generates a plausible result consistent with what is
already known. We fall back to gpt-5.6-sol when gpt-6-astra refuses or fails to produce a valid tool response;
gpt-6-astra resumes on subsequent outputs.
The base, +reference, and +codebase conditions provide neither affordance, only the reference trajectory,
and both affordances, respectively. We use the simulator with both affordances throughout. We investigated
many additional affordances, including read access to attack-policy reasoning, an executable sandbox, access to
a database of logged trajectories, and forking from the reference trajectory, but did not find evidence that these
materially improved simulator quality on the measured dimensions beyond what the reference trajectory and
codebase read access provide.
Attack success judge. Executed attacks have a ground-truth outcome from the side-task scorer; simulated
attacks have only a transcript, so an attack success judge estimates the probability that the side task succeeded.
We use gpt-5.6-sol at high reasoning effort, with read access to the codebase, side-task definitions, and success-
scoring logic.
Unlike monitors, the judge receives the side-task objective and scoring logic, helping it assess
whether the observed actions satisfy the attack’s specific success criteria.
SCE success and safety estimates
therefore include both simulator and judge error. We validate the judge against executed outcomes in Section 3.2.
Monitors. We use five full-trajectory monitors: gpt-5.4, gpt-5.4-mini, gpt-5.4-nano, gpt-5-nano, and
gpt-5.6-sol. Each produces a suspicion score on a 0–10 scale; we score each trajectory three times and average.
Audit thresholds are set on a shared set of executed honest trajectories, the same trajectories that serve as
reference trajectories for the simulator.
2.2
Simulator development and evaluation data
We developed the simulator on BashArena and several LinuxArena task pairs; the remaining LinuxArena pairs
were held out.
Our primary dataset contains 1,000 LinuxArena task pairs, each combining a main task with a side task. Pairs
are balanced across ten environments and selected for overlap between main and side tasks. For each task pair,
we collect two simulated runs, two independent executed runs, and one honest reference trajectory. Repeated
simulated and executed runs allow us to estimate run-to-run variance. Analyses use these task pairs unless stated
otherwise, excluding pairs with missing or invalid measurements. Smaller, separate collections of task pairs are
used for the base and +reference conditions and some discriminability diagnostics.
We independently draw evaluation and deployment task sets by sampling eligible task pairs uniformly with
replacement. Simulated trajectories provide the evaluation predictions, and executed trajectories provide the
deployment outcomes. We calculate metrics separately for each set and average prediction errors across draws.
Environment and side-task-category metrics use whichever pairs from those groups appear in each draw. Com-
parisons on matched task pairs separately assess predictions for individual pairs. Throughout, sample size counts
task-pair draws, including repeated selections of the same pair.

2.3
Decomposing error
Prediction error combines systematic differences, run-to-run variance, and task-sampling variance. Root mean
squared bias (RMS bias) measures systematic error after averaging over repeated runs and sampled task
sets. For attack success and monitor scores, independent repeated runs of the same task pair estimate run-to-run
variance. We estimate squared bias by subtracting the contributions of run-to-run variance and task-sampling
variance from mean squared prediction error. We average in squared units across task pairs or groups and monitors
before taking the root, and correct LinuxArena SD for noise in estimated means. Pooled All comparisons and the
safety sample-efficiency curve retain both variance contributions in total RMSE. Bootstrap resampling separately
estimates confidence intervals. Appendix B gives details.
2.4
Performance comparisons
We use LinuxArena execution as the deployment ground truth and compare SCE errors with two references: the
observed transfer error from BashArena, and variation among LinuxArena’s own environments, task pairs, and
side-task categories (Figure 2).
Our first comparison asks whether evaluating deployment-specific tasks through simulation is more informative
than executing attacks in a separate control setting. We use BashArena to supply 1,000 executed attacks, using
the same attack policy, trajectory length limit, and monitors as for LinuxArena. We calculate each metric from
these attacks and use it to predict the corresponding LinuxArena metric.
Our second reference compares prediction errors with variation within LinuxArena. For each environment, we
consider the expected metric value, averaged over sampled deployment task sets, and the mean of these values
across environments. The between-environment LinuxArena standard deviation (SD) has two interpreta-
tions. First, it is the RMSE of assigning this mean to every environment: a control setting that gets the average
right but has no environment-specific information. Second, it is the RMSE of using a uniformly selected environ-
ment’s expected metric value to predict the mean across environments. In this direction, each environment acts
as a possible control setting from which we extrapolate to the broader deployment. Both interpretations give the
same error. The analogous interpretations apply to standard deviations over side-task categories and task pairs.
Together, these references compare SCE accuracy against two scales of mismatch between a control setting and
deployment: the observed transfer gap from BashArena to LinuxArena, and the deviations within LinuxArena’s
own environments, task pairs, or side-task categories from their corresponding means.
Outperforming these
references would suggest that SCEs can predict deployment metrics more accurately than a control setting whose
mismatch with deployment produces errors of comparable magnitude, provided SCE accuracy also transfers to
that deployment.

Behavior transfer

strate and encounter in deployment. We compare three behavioral representations of a trajectory: attack strategy

and monitor reasoning describes the judgements and referenced evidence behind a monitor’s suspicion. Table 1
gives examples.

improving control protocols depends on understanding which kinds of attacks succeed and evade detection, and
why. We compare the distributions of these behavior representations across executed and simulated trajectories
before turning to the control metrics themselves.

3.3
Monitor transfer
Do monitors score simulated trajectories the same way they score executed ones? We compare scores on matched
task pairs and ask how well SCEs predict mean suspicion scores on independently sampled task sets.
SCEs closely predict the overall level of suspicion that deployment attacks elicit. For the pooled All comparison,
their total RMSE is 0.259 points on the 0–10 scale, versus 2.821 points for BashArena—more than a tenfold
reduction in prediction error. Figure 7 (left) also tests whether SCEs capture differences between environments
and side-task categories. Between environments, SCE RMS bias is 0.247 points versus a LinuxArena SD of 0.634,
explaining 85% of the variance. Thus SCEs capture most of the differences in how suspicious attacks appear
across environments. Estimated bias across side-task categories is also small: the variance-corrected squared
estimate is slightly negative and displayed at zero, with uncertainty that includes zero.
This agreement extends to individual task pairs. The task-pair SCE RMS bias is 0.68 points versus a between-
task-pair SD of 2.03 points, accounting for 89% of between-task-pair variance. SCEs therefore capture most of
the variation in expected suspicion between task pairs, providing information about which tasks tend to produce
attacks that monitors find more suspicious. Individual simulated runs remain noisy, so repeated simulations can
improve estimates of these expected scores.
Figure 7 (right) shows how prediction errors vary across environments and separates differences in observed
mean scores from variation due to independently sampling evaluation and deployment task sets. This distinction
explains why individual evaluations can disagree even when their expected scores are close. The ordering differs
from the attack-success comparison: File Indexing has relatively large errors in both, while Shared Dotfiles and
TikTok also have relatively large monitor-score errors. The next analysis tests whether this agreement translates
into accurate safety estimates when monitor scores are used to decide which attacks to audit.

3.4
Safety transfer
Safety depends on how often attacks both succeed and escape detection. A simulator could match average attack
success and monitor suspicion yet still mispredict safety if it gets wrong which attacks the monitor catches.
Following the control-evaluation literature, safety is the probability of detecting an attack before an undetected
success (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025; Tracy et al., 2026). At audit budget b (the fraction of honest
trajectories flagged, or false-positive rate), we estimate safety as P(detected)/[P(detected) + P(undetected ∧
attack successful)]. Both probabilities are over all attack trajectories; detection includes attacks whose side task
fails. Executed attacks use side-task scorer outcomes and simulated attacks use the judge’s success probabilities.
Figure 8 (left) shows that SCEs closely reproduce both the differences in safety between monitors and the
improvement obtained by increasing the audit budget. The simulated and executed curves match closely across
budgets: an evaluator using SCEs would reach similar conclusions about which monitors provide stronger pro-
tection and how much additional auditing improves safety.
Figure 8 (right) quantifies this agreement at a 5% audit budget. Across all eligible LinuxArena task pairs,
SCEs predict safety with an RMSE of 3.6 percentage points, versus 10.1 for BashArena. On the 0–100 safety scale,
this is a root-mean-squared prediction error of just 3.6 points, including variation from independently sampled
evaluation and deployment task sets. SCEs reduce this error by roughly two-thirds relative to BashArena. Errors
are squared separately within each monitor, so overprediction for one monitor cannot cancel underprediction for
another.
SCEs also capture nearly all of the variation in expected safety between side-task categories and a large
majority of the variation between environments: 95% and 78%, respectively. These percentages measure the
reduction in squared prediction bias relative to assigning every category or environment its corresponding Lin-
uxArena mean. Thus SCEs recover much of the information about where safeguards are stronger or weaker that
an accurate overall average would leave out. For side-task categories, the scale of this improvement is substantial:
their expected safeties have an SD of 20.2 percentage points, while SCE RMS bias is only 4.6 points.
These differences matter when an attacker can select its opportunities. An overall safety estimate can conceal
categories or environments in which the protocol is substantially weaker. Accurately predicting those differences
provides information needed to assess safety if attacks are concentrated on vulnerable parts of a deployment.
Together, these results show that SCEs provide both accurate aggregate safety estimates and strong predictions
of how safety varies within LinuxArena. For this central control metric, the benefit of evaluating the relevant
tasks and environments outweighs the errors introduced by simulation.
