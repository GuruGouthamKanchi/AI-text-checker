The Geometry of Metaphor: Probing and Steering Metaphor
Representations in Large Language Models
Shihui Li*, Mette Andersen∗, Martha Lewis
Institute for Logic, Language, and Computation
University of Amsterdam
shihui.li@student.uva.nl, {m.f.andersen, m.a.f.lewis}@uva.nl
Abstract
Metaphor is a crucial aspect of human cogni-
tion. As such, we would like to understand
how it is represented in large language models
(LLMs) and how it might be controlled. We
use mechanistic interpretability techniques to
identify causal subspaces in a range of LLMs
covering different architectures and sizes. We
show that we are able to steer output on classi-
fication tasks across different parts of speech.
We are also able to steer output on generation
tasks, indicating that these techniques can be
used to control the metaphoricity of LLM out-
put.

Introduction
Metaphor is an essential part of everyday hu-
man language use. Sources such as Steen et al.
(2010); Shutova (2010) show that metaphor per-
vades language. Furthermore, metaphor forms a
basis for the fundamental creativity and concept
formation that we are capable of (Lakoff and John-
son, 2008). Large language models such as Llama
3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) have some capacity to
understand and produce metaphorical language, al-
though whether they are up to human performance
is debated (Chakrabarty et al., 2021; Stowe et al.,
2022; Ichien et al., 2024).
One way to understand the capacities of large
language models is via mechanistic interpretabil-
ity: by examining the internals of large language
models via, for example, the Logit Lens1, causal
interventions (Meng et al., 2022), or circuit discov-
ery (Conmy et al., 2023). Sharkey et al. (2025)
provides a thorough review and discussion of open
problems in the area. One recent paper, Marks
and Tegmark (2024), argued that Llama models
exhibited a ‘truth direction’ at particular layers,

which was common to different datasets, and that
the models’ output could be steered by causal in-

Finding such a direction for metaphor would
give insight into how models represent metaphor.
This has been investigated to some extent (Boisson
et al., 2024) but not yet at the mechanistic level.
Finding this direction could also provide a useful
‘dial’ that could potentially be used to steer the
metaphoricity or literalness of output text, using
techniques from e.g. Li et al. (2023). In this paper,
we use activation patching (Meng et al., 2022) and
distributed alignment search (Geiger et al., 2024) to
find a ‘metaphoricity direction’ or subspace within
a given model. We show that these directions are
causally effective: we are able to change the output
of models in classification tasks for metaphor iden-
tification and interpretation; and in a generation
task we are able to steer the model towards more
metaphorical output.
Contributions
• We identify directions and subspaces for
metaphoricity in a range of models, and show
that these have some causal effect.
• We show that these directions are somewhat
consistent across different metaphor datasets.
• We test seven models from four different
model families (Gemma (Riviere et al., 2024),
Qwen (Bai et al., 2023), Llama (Grattafiori
et al., 2024), Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023))
across small (7-9B) and medium-large (27-
70B) sizes.
• We show that steering models in a generation
task can change model output from more lit-
eral to more metaphorical.

Background and Related work
Metaphor identification and interpretation
In
metaphor processing, research has focussed on
two main tasks: metaphor identification, that is,
identifying whether a word is metaphorical, and
metaphor interpretation, that is, identifying the
meaning of a metaphorical utterance. Work in us-
ing LLMs for metaphor identification include Fuoli
et al. (2026); Reimann and Scheffler (2025). On the
metaphor interpretation side, there has been a range
of work showing that modern LLMs have relatively
strong performance at understanding metaphor, es-
pecially when fine-tuned (Ichien et al., 2024; Liu
et al., 2022b). Since metaphor is a key part of hu-
man language, we would like to understand how
LLMs interpret and identify metaphor.
Activation Patching
Mechanistic interpretabil-
ity techniques attempt to understand and affect the
output of large language models by inspecting their
internal representations, for example by looking at
the vectors of activations produced at each layer of
the model (Meng et al., 2022; Marks and Tegmark,
2024). A common technique is to then causally
intervene on these representations. This means that
we examine how a model’s output changes when
we alter its internal representations, for example
by activation patching, introduced in Meng et al.
(2022); that is, we save the activations for one in-
put, and patch these into the activations for another
input, examining which layers and tokens affect
the output.
In Marks and Tegmark (2024), the authors use
activation patching to find a combination of layer
and token input that represents a concept of truth
in a language model.
Distributed Alignment Search (DAS)
(Geiger
et al., 2024) is a method for finding causal con-
cepts in model activations, based on the assump-
tion that full-vector activations may be polyseman-
tic, i.e. may be activated for different concepts.
The method has been used in existing benchmarks
(Mueller et al., 2025) for causal variable local-
ization, and compared to other methods (SAEs,
PCA, full-vector), DAS has been shown to find
concepts that achieve superior causal performance.
In this paper, we use DAS to find a causal concept
of metaphoricity in the model’s residual stream,
and compare the performance of steering using
DAS with the existing mass-mean steering used by
Marks and Tegmark (2024).
Controlling Generation
Controlling the style
of LLM generated text is also an active area of re-
search. Techniques used include training a separate
model (Liu et al., 2022a), or prompt-based style
control such as in (Liu et al., 2024). Relatedly, Li
et al. (2023) alter the truthfulness of generated text
via inference-time interventions.
Summary
Metaphor identification, interpreta-
tion, and generation are key aspects of language
use. So far, in LLMs, these abilities have mostly
been investigated by looking at behavioural out-
puts. In this paper, we use activation patching
and distributed alignment search to investigate
firstly whether metaphorical and literal sentences
are linearly separable in activation space, secondly
whether we can steer models towards literal or
metaphoric output, and lastly whether the steering
vectors are common to different datasets. We con-
trol the metaphoricity of generated output using the
inference-time intervention technique introduced
by Li et al. (2023). To our knowledge, this is the
first attempt to control the metaphoricity of LLM
output via intervention on the activations, rather
than via prompting or building an external model.

Datasets and Tasks
3.1
Datasets
We use three datasets covering two different parts
of speech (nouns and adjectives)2. Using mecha-
nistic interpretability techniques requires that mod-
els are able to perform to a high standard on the
task, and the structure of the dataset also needs to
be fairly controlled. We therefore use relatively
simply structured datasets. Following Marks and
Tegmark (2024), we use datasets with the following
properties: 1) each sentence contains exactly one
metaphorical target word; 2) sentences are short
and structurally simple; 3) within each dataset, sen-
tences have the same syntactic structure.
We use the following three datasets. Examples
of sentences with metaphorical nouns and adjec-
tives are shown in Table 1.
Noun dataset (NOUN).
We start from the small
training set of Fig-QA (Liu et al., 2022b), which
contains 200 human-written creative metaphors.
Each example is paired with one correct rephrase
and one incorrect rephrase in a Winograd-style

coffee”) or metaphorical (e.g. “warm welcome”).
We evaluate on n=100 per steering direction from
the held-out set not used for training DAS. In the
remainder of this paper we refer to this dataset as
DOM.

Methods
4.1
Identifying areas encoding metaphoricity
For each of the datasets and tasks described above,
we use one of two techniques designed to isolate
the part of the model used to encode metaphoric-
ity. For the identification and interpretation tasks
across NOUN, ADJ, and DOM, we use activation
patching. For DOM and for the generation task,
we also use distributed alignment search (DAS)
to identify a linear subspace encoding metaphoric-
ity. We use the mass-mean probe from Marks and
Tegmark (2024) and the learned DAS subspaces
to steer model output in the identification and in-
terpretation tasks, requiring single-token output.
Finally, we use the mass-mean probe and DAS
subspaces to steer generation over multiple tokens,
finding that it is possible to steer models towards
more metaphorical or more literal output.
4.1.1
Activation Patching
In the identification task, we use the following
procedure. On ADJ, we insert the following sen-
tence into the prompt template in Section 3.2 to
form a prompt pL= The skin is dry. For prompt
pL we expect the output ‘literal’. We then form
a paired prompt pM replacing skin with wit. For
this prompt we expect the output ‘metaphoric’. To
find out which hidden states contain information
about the metaphoricity of the sentence, we run
the model on the input pM and cache the activa-
tions hi,ℓ(pM) for each token position i and layer
ℓ. Then, for each i and ℓ, we run the model on
PL and modify the model’s forward pass by swap-
ping hi,ℓ(PL) for hi,ℓ(PM). Finally, we record the
difference in log probability between the tokens
"metaph"3 and "literal". The larger the difference,
the more influential the hidden state in position
i and layer ℓon the model’s prediction. For the
dataset NOUN, we use the prompt pair pL=The
breeze is cold and pM=The breeze is ice. For the

4.1.2
Distributed Alignment Search
We use Distributed Alignment Search (DAS)
(Geiger et al., 2024) to localize, if it exists, a low-
dimensional, linear subspace encoding metaphoric-
ity. After identifying the most causally implicated
token-position in the residual stream using full-
vector activation patching, DAS trains a rotation
matrix on top of these activations. The rotation
matrix is trained to maximize Interchange Inter-
vention Accuracy (IIA), which is the fraction of
samples where patching flips model prediction in
the expected direction.
Concretely, let h ∈Rd denote the residual
stream at a fixed layer ℓand token position p. As
will be seen in Figure 3, the full stop token at the
end of the target sentence in each prompt is highly
causally implicated. We perform DAS at this token
at the layer that optimises the IIA score.

During training, we alternate intervention direc-
tions: on even steps we patch literal representations
into metaphor prompts (aiming to flip the output to
literal), and on odd steps the reverse. The loss for a
single example is L = logit(other) −logit(target)
where target is the correct post-intervention label
and other is the original label. R is updated via
Adam for 1,000 steps while the model weights are
frozen.
IIA is the fraction of samples that are cor-
rectly flipped. In our case, if hbase is predicted
as “metaphor” for the model, it should flip to “lit-
eral” post-patching to contribute positively to IIA.
Hence, IIA = 1.0 means we have found the variable
capable of flipping all held-out examples.


4.2
Model steering
We evaluate whether we can steer incorrect pre-
dictions towards correct ones using two methods:
full-vector mass-mean steering, as used by Marks
and Tegmark (2024), and steering using the mean
activations for metaphor-sample and literal-sample
activations in the DAS subspace. The means are
computed from correctly predicted samples, and
we steer incorrectly predicted samples in both di-
rections.
Mass-mean steering is implemented as:
hsteered ←h + α(µmet −µlit)
(1)
DAS steering is implemented as:.
hsteered ←h −α RR⊤h + α RR⊤µtarget
(2)
We set α = 1 for the identification and inter-
pretation tasks on NOUN, ADJ, and DOM. On
DOM we train DAS matrices firstly across the
whole dataset, and subsequently for each individ-
ual domain.
4.2.1
Generation of multiple tokens
To test whether the DAS subspace encodes a
general-purpose concept of metaphor, which ex-
tends beyond the prediction task it was trained
on, we steer free-form generation. We prompt the
model as described in section 3.2. During decod-
ing, we steer every generated token as in Li et al.
(2023): we use a hook function to save the acti-
vation we use for patching, and inject it at every
generated token.
We compare baseline generation, mass mean,
and DAS outputs across α ∈{1, 2, 5, 10} and both
steering directions (lit →met and met →lit).
Evaluation using LLM-as-judge
To quantify
the degree to which generated output changed
from explaining a literal use of an adjective to a
metaphorical use and vice versa, we use Qwen 2.5
72B Instruct as a judge. Only the first sentence of
each completion is judged, and a degeneracy filter
applied before judging: a completion is marked
degenerate if any single word repeats ≥3 times.
The judge is prompted using the prompt shown in
Appendix K.
Metrics
We report the following metrics: Flip
rate (MM / DAS): fraction of of completions
where judge label matches the target direction. De-
generate responses are counted as 0, and we only
consider the samples where the judge was able
to judge correctly on the baseline generation pre-
steering.

Activation patching

we observe three groups of activations that most
