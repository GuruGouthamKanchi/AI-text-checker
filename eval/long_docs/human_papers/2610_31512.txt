ABSTRACT
Users increasingly rely on AI systems for extended, multi-turn tasks, yet evidence
indicates that LLM-based system performance degrades over prolonged interac-
tion. To evaluate and improve AI assistants in such settings, we need user simu-
lators that faithfully follow complex user intents while exhibiting realistic conver-
sational behavior. Existing approaches manifest a trade-off: prompted assistants
can follow complex user intents but lack behavioral fidelity, while specialized
user simulators better approximate conversational behavior but struggle to remain
faithful to complex intents. In this paper, we propose a reinforcement learning
(RL) recipe that jointly rewards task intent adherence and behavioral natural-
ness. A crucial contribution of the method is operationalizing naturalness with
a lightweight discriminator trained in an alternating, adversarial loop with the
user simulator. We release the resulting model, UserLM-2.0, which produces
valid simulated conversations (i.e., meeting both task-faithfulness and naturalness
criteria) 46.2% of the time, over three times the rate of the strongest prior user
simulator (14.1%).

INTRODUCTION
Users increasingly expect AI assistants and agents to help them accomplish complex tasks through
sustained, multi-turn interaction, rather than provide isolated answers to individual queries (Handa
et al., 2025; Chatterji et al., 2025). Yet prior work has shown that current LLM-based systems
severely degrade in performance in such interactive settings (Laban et al., 2025; Tack et al., 2026).
Understanding and addressing this gap requires evaluations that capture how real users communicate
their goals and respond to assistants over multiple turns (Chang et al., 2025a). A crucial component
of interactive benchmarks is a user simulator, which takes as input a high-level task intent (such as
the one shown in Figure 1) and generates plausible user utterances through iterative interaction with
an assistant (Yao et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025).
We focus on two essential properties of user simulators: task fidelity and behavioral naturalness.
Task fidelity requires the simulator to convey the information specified in the task intent over the
course of the conversation, without omitting or contradicting the specification. Behavioral natural-
ness requires the simulator to resemble real users in both how it phrases individual messages and
how it conducts the interaction across turns. A simulator that omits task requirements can make as-
sistant models appear to fail when the simulator is at fault, making benchmarking unreliable (Mehri
et al., 2026c; Zhou et al., 2026), whereas a simulator that does not adequately capture the interactive
behavior of real users may yield conclusions that do not transfer to real interactions (Zhou et al.,
2026; Seshadri et al., 2026).
We show experimentally that current approaches favor one requirement at the expense of the other.
Prompting LLM assistants to act as users is the most common method for simulating users (Yao
et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). While prompted assistants can stay on task,
they lack behavioral naturalness (Naous et al., 2026; Mehri et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026). This
is illustrated in the example in Figure 1, where GPT-5.6-Sol (a strong assistant LLM) accurately
enacts the task intent but expresses it through unusually polished and exhaustive messages, unlike
the conversational patterns in real user-AI exchanges.
An alternative is to train user simulators by “flipping the dialogue”: supervised finetuning an LLM
on user utterances from a corpus of user-assistant interactions (Naous et al., 2026; Suh et al., 2026).
As reported in prior work, such finetuned user simulators exhibit realistic interaction behavior but
often struggle to precisely enact the task intent they are given (Naous et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026).
In the example in Figure 1, the SFT-trained UserLM-8B (Naous et al., 2026) ends the conversation
prematurely, before conveying all of the specified task requirements to the assistant.
We propose a post-training recipe that improves task fidelity while preserving behavioral natural-
ness in user simulators. We first finetune an LLM on user turns from a corpus of user-assistant
interactions, then post-train it using multi-turn reinforcement learning.
Our RL training objective jointly rewards task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. Rewards related
to task fidelity use LLM-based judges (Zheng et al., 2023), for example by evaluating the extent to
which a simulated conversation has conveyed the information specified in the task intent. We find
that LLM-as-a-judge for behavioral naturalness fails to reliably encourage realistic user behavior.
Instead, drawing on adversarial training (Goodfellow et al., 2014a) and its application to dialogue
generation (Li et al., 2017), we propose a discriminator-based reward for behavioral naturalness.
Specifically, we continually retrain a discriminator to distinguish real user conversations from con-
versations generated by the user simulator being trained. This choice defines the naturalness reward
in terms of how indistinguishable a given simulated conversation is from real user conversations.
We update the discriminator as the simulator evolves to keep the naturalness reward responsive to
its changing outputs and reduce opportunities to exploit a fixed evaluator (Gao et al., 2022).
We train, evaluate, and release the resulting artifact, UserLM-2.0. Among the simulators we evalu-
ated, UserLM-2.0 lies on the Pareto frontier for task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. We count
a simulated conversation as valid if it adheres to our quality and termination criteria, achieves at
least 80% coverage of the task intent and passes our naturalness criterion, assessed using three dis-
criminators including Pangram (Glickenhaus et al., 2026). By this definition, UserLM-2.0 yields
valid conversations for 46.2% of task intents, more than three times the 14.1% rate of the strongest
prior user simulator (SFTUser-14B). This gap grows even further to a factor of 12 at higher task
complexities, highlighting the effectiveness of our method.

METHOD
Our goal is to train a user simulator that takes a high-level task intent as input, and simulates realistic
user utterances in a multi-turn user-assistant conversation. As previously discussed, a good user
simulator must (1) generate natural utterances, and (2) obey the task intents closely.
2.1
PROBLEM DEFINITION
Each conversation is a multi-turn interaction where a user simulator U interacts with an assis-
tant model A to satisfy an input intent c.
This intent c captures the high-level goal of the
user without detailing behavior on a turn-by-turn basis. Given c, we sample conversations τ =
(c, u1, a1, . . . , un−1, an−1, un), where ui and ai represent the sampled i-th user and assistant utter-
ances respectively. A conversation terminates either when the maximum number of user turns N is
reached or when the user outputs an <|endconversation|> tag.
Prior work (Wang et al., 2025; Naous et al., 2026) trains user simulators via SFT on a large corpus of
user-assistant interactions. These exhibit the desired naturalness behavior, but are extremely poor at
faithfully enacting task intents. We propose treating such SFT models as initializations rather than
the final user simulator. We train our own SFT model, UserLM-2.0-SFT, and design an RL-based
post-training pipeline that improves its task fidelity without sacrificing naturalness.
We perform multi-turn reinforcement learning using the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al., 2024). Our
reward balances the above two goals of user simulation, task fidelity and behavioral naturalness.
Although these are orthogonal, models can easily reward hack one at the cost of the other, such
as by unnaturally including all information in the first utterance for perfect task coverage. The
combined reward composition safeguards against such behaviors.
2.2
REWARD DESIGN FOR REINFORCEMENT LEARNING
We first describe the individual reward components and then our formula for combining them:
Naturalness:
The naturalness reward should score how closely the user utterances of simulated
conversation resemble the way real users interact with AI assistants in a target corpus. Let Pdisc
be a discriminator that can distinguish between real and simulated user utterances. Our naturalness
reward measures how well the user simulator fools such a discriminator based on Pdisc(y = real |
ˆu1... ˆuk, c), where ˆu1... ˆuk is the multi-turn output from the user simulator and c is the associated
task intent. For training, we set our naturalness reward to be binary; it is 1 if this discriminator score
is above a threshold (0.5 in our experiments) and 0 otherwise.
Broadly, we have two options for discriminators: LLM judges or specially trained discriminators.
LLM judges are ill-suited for this task. Naturalness captures multiple implicit dimensions such as
the surface-form of the utterance, pace of the conversation, coherence of follow-up utterances, etc.,
all of which might be impossible to extract from the target corpus and enumerate as instructions.
In fact, prior work (Russell et al., 2025) has shown that prompted LLM judges are ineffective at
distinguishing between real user and AI text.1 In our work, we train lightweight discriminators
on top of ModernBert-Large (Warner et al., 2025) to distinguish between real and simulated user
utterances. This allows us to both limit the costs and latency during RL and specialize to the user
behaviors in the target corpus. We include ablations to empirically demonstrate the issues with using
LLM judges and static discriminators in Section 4.4.
Coverage:
This reward component quantifies how completely the user simulator follows a task
intent. We define coverage as the proportion of the sub-goals of the task intent that are included in
the user utterances generated by the simulator. This is instantiated with an LLM judge.
We highlight that coverage can be an easy metric to reward hack for user simulators. For example,
given a detailed task intent with fully specified sub-goals, a user simulator can maximize coverage

by simply paraphrasing the sub-goals. This is not ideal as it disincentivizes diverse simulations for
the same task intent. Therefore, we introduce an information asymmetry between the task intent
provided to the user simulator as input (c) and the one used to compute the coverage reward by the
LLM judge (c∗), where c is a more abstract version of the fully-specified task intent c∗. This ensures
the model receives poor coverage when simply paraphrasing, and encourages exploring more diverse
behaviors.
In addition to coverage, we include two other reward components for task fidelity. These target
proper formatting of the user utterances and elimination of trivial failure modes of the SFT models.
Quality:
Our quality reward is a binary score that assesses the baseline quality of a simulated
conversation. It targets three common error modes of the SFT models: (1) verbatim repetition of
task intent, (2) verbatim repetition of past user turns, and (3) switching roles from user to assistant
mid-output. We first check the initial two conditions programmatically and otherwise use an LLM
judge. A simulated conversation is given a reward 1 if all three conditions are met, and 0 otherwise.
Termination:
The goal of the termination reward is to ensure that user simulators properly ter-
minate conversations when all sub-goals in c∗are met and penalize early termination. We give a
positive reward λ for including the termination tag <|endconversation|> in the last utterance
if all sub-goals have been met in the earlier utterances and a negative reward −λ if the conversation
is terminated without satisfying all sub-goals. Note that this ties the termination reward to coverage.
In some cases, the user simulator fails to terminate the conversation at all, i.e. does not generate the
<|endconversation|> tag; in these cases, we give it a 0 termination reward.
Final Reward:
We combine all four reward components into a overall reward score for a simulated
conversation using the following formula: roverall = rquality ·(rcoverage + rnaturalness + rtermination). Note
that the overall reward is 0 if the quality reward is 0. Therefore, the basic quality checks serve as a
necessary condition for any successful simulation.
2.3
GAN-STYLE TRAINING WITH DYNAMIC DISCRIMINATORS
One limitation of trained naturalness discriminators, especially lightweight ones with fewer parame-
ters, is that a larger user simulator trained against it will quickly learn to reward hack. It will quickly
converge on a narrow set of behaviors that reliably fools the specific discriminator but does not learn
broader behavioral naturalness. Our initial experiments validated this intuition – user simulators
trained against a static discriminator maximized the naturalness reward within 75 steps by greatly
increasing average utterance length while reducing conversation turns.
To address this, we propose a GAN-style training recipe that periodically re-trains the discriminator
every k steps. This improves discriminator quality by exposing it to synthetic conversations with
distinct behavioral patterns and further helps detect and discourage reward hacking behavior.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
3.1
DATASET CONSTRUCTION
Dataset Processing.
We run our experiments on the non-toxic subset of WildChat-4.8M (Zhao
et al., 2024). We build on top of the dataset processing scheme of Naous et al. (2026), additionally
removing interactions with only one user turn as our goal is to simulate multi-turn interactions. This
reduces the WildChat-4.8M dataset size from 3,199,860 to 199,249 interactions. We also follow
Naous et al. (2026) in how we construct train, validation and test sets. We hold out 10,000 and 1,000
conversations from the train and validation set for RL training, 3,200 and 1,000 conversations from
the train and validation set for discriminator training, and use the rest for SFT. Refer to Appendix A
for more details on dataset construction.
Intent and Subgoal Extraction.
We need to extract both intents (given as input for simulation)
and fine-grained sub-goals (used for LLM-based coverage reward).
We use Qwen3-30B-A3B-
Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025) to extract intents for all 199,249 conversations. For sub-goals,
we few-shot prompt the best available GPT models without reasoning. Details are in Appendix A.
4


Test dataset.
We report all results on a 2000 intent test set, constructed such that the number of
intents per sub-goal count (ranging from 1 to 7) is balanced. For each user simulator, we sample
four conversations per intent with Qwen3.5-9B as the assistant.
3.2
TRAINING DETAILS
User Simulator Training.
We use Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the backbone
for UserLM-2.0. We replicate the SFT training of Naous et al. (2026) and select the checkpoint with
the best validation loss for subsequent RL. This checkpoint is UserLM-2.0-SFT. For RL, we simulate
conversations with a maximum turn count of N = 5, and use Qwen3.5-9B without reasoning (Team,
2026) as the assistant model. We set λ = 0.2 for the termination reward, batch size to 64, group size
to 8 and KL hyperparameter β = 0.001. We measure model performance on the validation set every
25 steps for checkpoint selection. See Appendix B for further training details.
Naturalness Discriminator Training.
We use the 395M ModernBERT-Large (Warner et al.,
2025) as the discriminator. For each task intent, we sample one simulated conversation (using the
user and assistant models) and one real conversation (from the ground truth dataset).2 This ensures
that our dataset is always balanced. We re-train our discriminator every 25 steps of RL training.
At each iteration, half of our training dataset consists of conversations based on task intents from
the most recent 25 RL steps, while the other half is sampled uniformly from the remaining training
history. We train for five epochs and pick the checkpoint achieving the best validation accuracy.
Refer to Appendix B.3 for exact hyperparameters and training details.
3.3
BASELINES
We compare UserLM-2.0 against two categories of models: assistant LLMs prompted to behave
like users, and specialized finetuned user simulators, as well as held out reference users.
Finetuned Models.
We compare against USP (Wang et al., 2025), UserLM-8B (Naous et al.,
2026), UserLM-2.0-SFT (our SFT initialization), SFTUser-14B (Suh et al., 2026) and HumanLM-
Opinion (Wu et al., 2026). UserLM-8B and SFTUser-14B are trained on WildChat-1M (Zhao et al.,
2024) via SFT but differ in their backbone initialization. USP is also SFT-trained but on a subset of
94,874 LMSys-Chat conversations (Zheng et al., 2024). HumanLM is trained via RL to align with la-
tent states such as user goals, opinions or emotional valence. We use the HumanLM-Opinion check-
point as other HumanLM checkpoints have not been released. For USP and HumanLM-Opinion, we
include the input intent and termination instructions in system prompts (details in Appendix C.1).
Prompted assistant LLMs.
We use a range of small and large, open- and closed-source mod-
els. Specifically, we evaluate: Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (Llama-3.1-8B in the
tables), Qwen3.5-9B (Team, 2026), GPT-5.6-Luna, GPT-5.6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026), DeepSeek-V4-
Pro (Xu et al., 2026) and Kimi-K2.6 (Moonshot AI, 2026). We follow the prompting strategy of
Naous et al. (2026), with prompts and inference details in Appendix C.2.
3.4
EVALUATION METRICS
We evaluate all simulators on a common set of 2,000 test task intents. For each task intent, we sim-
ulate four independent conversations and compute three evaluation metrics that assess task fidelity
and three metrics that assess naturalness. Finally, we define a compound metric that assesses the
overall validity of each simulated conversation.
Task Fidelity Metrics.
We assess each generated conversation by reusing the Terminated, Quality,
and Coverage as defined in Section 2.2. In short, Terminated is the percentage of conversations with
an explicit <|endconversation|> present. The Quality metric is a binary check for three
undesirable behaviors (e.g., verbatim copying of the task intent in a single user utterance). For the
evaluation, we used Qwen3.5-9B as the LLM judge for Quality, and the stronger GPT-5.6-Luna

for Coverage, as we found this additional model capacity allowed to extract additional per-turn

Naturalness Metrics.
We report naturalness using three complementary discriminators.
We
trained the first two discriminators in analogous ways to discriminators trained in Section 3.2. Both
use ModernBert-Large as the base.
1. Naturalness-Specific: We train a separate naturalness discriminator for each simulator to
evaluate. The metric reports the average P(y = real | ˆu1... ˆuk), where { ˆu1... ˆuk} is a simulated
conversation on the test set.3 This is the strictest test for naturalness among our metrics as each
discriminator specializes on patterns of individual simulators. For Human Reference interactions,
we report average P(y = real | u1...uk) for real conversations {u1...uk}, averaged over all model-
specific discriminators.
2. Naturalness-General: We train a general discriminator on data from all simulators, which
includes our models, baselines from §3, and experiments with reward design alternatives in §4.4.
This discriminator is generally less strict than the previous one, as it must distinguish between
simulations from multiple distributions.
3. Pangram: As a dataset-agnostic discriminator, we use Pangram-4 (Glickenhaus et al., 2026),
a state-of-the-art AI detection model trained to distinguish human and AI assistant text. We re-
port Pangram’s recorded fraction of human-written text. For each intent, we randomly select one
sampled conversation. To take into account’s Pangram’s limitations around short texts, we remove
termination tags and concatenate a conversation’s user turns through the first whole turn that exceeds
a cumulative 100 words, or use all user turns if the conversation is shorter.
Naturalness Pass Rate (%).
A conversation passes the overall naturalness criterion if at least
one of the individual naturalness scores (specific, general, Pangram) is at or above 0.5. This pass
rate represents the proportion of simulated conversations where at least one discriminator assigns
majority probability to the conversation being classified as real (i.e., the discriminator is “fooled”).
Conversation Validity Metric.
We define an aggregate metric that measures whether a conversa-
tion passes both task fidelity and naturalness expectations. This single, over-arching metric captures
the proportion of conversations produced by a simulator that are usable for downstream purposes.
Under our definition, a simulated conversation is valid if it meets four conditions: (1) a Quality
score of 1, (2) a Terminated score of 1,4 (3) a Coverage score of at least 0.8, and (4) passing the
naturalness criterion. We also report estimated cost in USD per 100 valid conversations (lower is
better), with base-model pricing proxies for fine-tuned models.

4.2
STRESS TESTING UNDER INCREASING TASK COMPLEXITY
Setup. The key use-case for user simulators is supporting downstream evaluation and training. In
particular, a user simulator must be capable of simulating realistic and valid interactions as task
complexity varies. For evaluation purposes, we decomposed each test task intent into a set of sub-
goals: units of information that are part of the task intent. We argue that the number of subgoals
of a task intent roughly correlates with task complexity, as more subgoals typically require longer
conversation to convey, leading to more opportunity for user simulators to omit or contradict task
information. As a first analysis, we break down the performance of user simulators as the number
of subgoals increases. Table 2 reports coverage, naturalness pass rate, and overall validity against
