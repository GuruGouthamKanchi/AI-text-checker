PIANO2POP: EXPANDING PIANO DEMOS INTO POP PRODUCTIONS WITHOUT
ALIGNED SUPERVISION
Yun-Chen Cheng
Chih-Pin Tan
Tzu-Hung Huang
Yi-Hsuan Yang
National Taiwan University, Taiwan
{hhhhaura, tanchihpin0517, zionhuang1107, affige}@gmail.com
ABSTRACT
We introduce Piano2Pop, a music-expansion task that transforms
piano MIDI into instrumental pop productions. Enabled by recent
advances in music generation and polyphonic music transcrip-
tion, our pseudo-supervised approach conditions a pretrained
flow-matching model on piano rolls without aligned piano–pop
supervision. We train on synthetic piano–instrumental pairs de-
rived from pop recordings, comparing pair-free transcription,
including function-selective (FST) and register–density (RDA)
augmentations, with a pair-supervised piano-cover generator as
pseudo-demo sources. On 476 testing real-world piano-cover/pop
pairs, the pair-supervised approach achieves the best Fr´echet Au-
dio Distance, Accompaniment Prompt Adherence, and spectral
shape on real-piano inputs, while FST mitigates piano-heavy out-
puts in pair-free training and remains a promising alternative. All
fine-tuned systems significantly outperform the zero-shot base-
lines, which suggests a role for task-specific modeling.
Index Terms— music generation, piano-to-pop, MIDI condi-
tioning, pseudo supervision, music arrangement
1. INTRODUCTION
A pop song often begins as a demo: a guitar sketch, a topline sung
over an existing track, or a piano accompaniment. Piano is par-
ticularly useful because two hands can convey melody, harmony,
voicing, dynamics, and groove, allowing a writer to commit to
what the song is without fully specifying what it will sound like.
Turning this sketch into a full production requires the producer to
infer its musical intent and realize it through drums, instrumenta-
tion, arrangement, and mixing.
We call this task Piano2Pop: given a piano MIDI demo, gen-
erate a complete instrumental pop production that is coherent, id-
iomatic to pop, and faithful to the demo’s musical intent. While Pi-
ano2Pop has received little attention, the reverse problem of map-
ping pop audio to piano, also called piano cover generation, has
been explored [1, 2]. Viewed through instrumentation, those sys-
tems perform music reduction [3], collapsing a multi-instrument
mix into piano; Piano2Pop instead performs music expansion,
developing the demo’s structure, melody, harmony, rhythm, and
instrumentation rather than merely accompanying an unchanged
piano part.
Existing polyphonic MIDI-to-audio systems focus on synthe-
sis and require aligned MIDI–audio pairs [4]. For the piano-to-
pop setting, such supervision is scarce and inherently messy: a pi-
ano cover and its corresponding pop recording differ in duration,
tempo, and often overall structure, a mismatch already noted by
previous work [2]. We argue that this reliance is no longer neces-
sary. Recent multi-instrument transcription models [5] can recover
symbolic content directly from pop audio, and state-of-the-art mu-
sic generation models [6] can render full productions from various
conditions. Combining the two, we can pioneer a piano-to-pop
system that requires no matched piano-pop pairs.
We propose a pseudo-supervised strategy that derives piano
MIDI pseudo demos from unpaired pop instrumentals to form
time-aligned training pairs. We compare two extraction routes,
distinguished by whether the generator was trained on paired
pop recordings and piano covers.
Pair-free extraction restricts
an audio transcription model’s predictions to piano, yielding a
rendition of the full mix without cover-pop supervision [5]. Pair-
supervised extraction uses a piano arrangement model trained on
cover-pop pairs to generate piano covers [2]. Both provide scal-
able supervision but preserve different musical information and
may differ substantially from real piano demos, motivating two
research questions:
1. Does Piano2Pop benefit from task-specific adaptation
over existing conditioned music-generation systems, as
measured by piano–production adherence and spectral-
shape matchness?
2. How does pair-free versus pair-supervised pseudo-demo
construction affect transfer to real piano covers, measured
by closeness to real-pop audio distributions?
We contribute by formulating Piano2Pop and addressing these
research questions through a piano-conditioned generation frame-
work and an objective evaluation protocol with tailored behavioral
metrics. Audio examples are available on our demo page.1 For
reproducibility, we plan to release open-source code and check-
points upon paper publication.

Orchestration in the Symbolic Domain.
Symbolic piano-to-
ensemble orchestration is a close task-level predecessor to Pi-
ano2Pop: both expand a piano sketch into a multi-instrument
arrangement.
Early work on piano-to-orchestra expansion ex-
plored paired piano and orchestral scores [7].
The scarcity of
such pairs motivated alternatives based on synthetic reductions
and retrieval [8]. These methods output symbolic scores, whereas
Piano2Pop generates instrumental audio directly, combining mu-
sical arrangement with its sonic realization, including timbre and
production texture.

3. METHODOLOGY
As a first study of Piano2Pop, we focus on fixed-length audio seg-
ments rather than full songs: our training pipeline derives piano
pseudo demos from 30-second pop instrumentals, a horizon that
often spans a musical section (e.g., verse or chorus), then uses
these synthetic pairs to fine-tune a pretrained music generator with
a piano-roll encoder. We describe the pipeline below.
3.1. Pair-Free Pseudo-Demo Generation
We generate pseudo demos with MuScriptor [5], a state-of-the-
art multi-instrument transcription model that outputs multi-track,
matrix-like piano rolls (one track per instrument). Since we only
need a piano demo, we force it to transcribe the full pop instru-
mental as a piano piece by restricting decoding to MIDI program 0
(Grand Piano). To assign missing velocities, we rank notes by on-
set energy in a 10 ms framewise log-energy envelope and map
each rank quantile ri using fixed quantile–velocity anchors (q, v),
preserving relative dynamics rather than absolute loudness:
vi = clip(interp(ri; q, v), 25, 95) .
(1)
To further improve pair-free demos, we observe character-
istic relationships between human piano demos and their paired
pop productions, and introduce two complementary augmentation
strategies:
Register–Density Augmentation (RDA). Retrospective analy-
sis shows that both pseudo-demo sources have wider local pitch
spreads than real piano, with pair-supervised covers sparser and
MuScriptor denser (Table 1). RDA tests whether reducing register
and density differences improves transfer through two stochastic
edits: octave folding shifts non-bass notes one octave toward the
local register while preserving bassline and topline boundaries;
function-aware note deletion thins local harmonic groups by note
function, preferentially retaining structurally important notes.
Function-Selective Transcription (FST). Piano arrangement can
be viewed as music reduction [3], in which only selected com-
ponents of a full arrangement are retained. Following this view,
FST separates each instrumental with HTDemucs [14] into drums,
bass, other, guitar, and piano (vocals excluded), then builds four
mixtures: Full (all stems), Harmonic (pitched stems, excluding
drums), No-bass (all stems except bass), and Piano+bass (piano
and bass only).
Each mixture is transcribed as piano.
Before
this augmentation, pair-free training uses only a full-mix-to-piano
transcription. After it, Full repeats that demo, while the other three
add new piano-MIDI variants, growing the pair-free training set.

3.2. Pair-Supervised Pseudo-Demo Generation
The pair-supervised route uses our previous model, PiCoGen2 [2],
trained on paired pop recordings and piano covers to generate
piano arrangements autoregressively from instrumental features.
We generate piano covers of the source instrumentals, creating
pseudo-pairs for the reverse problem: Piano2Pop. Because PiCo-
Gen2 generates one bar at a time, we beat-track the source instru-
mental and linearly stretch each generated MIDI bar to the cor-
responding beat-derived bar boundaries. These generated covers
provide a consistent, model-derived arrangement style beyond the
local edits provided by our register–density transformations.
3.3. Model Architecture
We fine-tune ACE-Step v1.5’s pretrained rectified-flow trans-
former (24 layers, ≈2.39B parameters) [6] with a new piano-roll
encoder.
A frozen ACE-Step v1.5 waveform VAE [6] maps
48 kHz stereo audio to and from 64-channel, 25 Hz latents.
Piano-Roll Encoder.
MIDI is represented as a 176 × T ten-
sor at 25 Hz over pitches 21–108, with binary sustain and onset-
velocity planes (velocities divided by 127). A five-layer resid-
ual 1-D convolutional encoder (kernel 5, width 512, GroupNorm,
SiLU) produces frame-aligned features. This local receptive field
(approximately 0.4 s per side) focuses the encoder on local mu-
sical features, leaving global structure modeling to the diffusion
transformer (DiT) backbone.
Conditioning and Fine-Tuning.
Piano features replace ACE-
Step’s reference-audio conditioning and are concatenated with an
all-ones generation mask and noisy audio latents (192 channels
total). Encoder and DiT learning rates are 10−4 and 10−5.
4. EXPERIMENTS
4.1. Training Setup and Zero-Shot Baselines
We use two disjoint datasets. DB1 is a large collection of real pop
songs without real piano MIDI: 8,876 YouTube tracks (8,463 for
training, ≈554 hours; 413 for validation), from which we derive
pseudo-labeled piano demos for training. DB2 is a smaller set of
real pop songs with matching real piano MIDI covers, reserved for
evaluation (Section 4.2).
Across all settings, we fine-tune pretrained ACE-Step v1.5
base for 500,000 steps, training only the piano-roll encoder for the
first 2,000 steps and jointly fine-tuning it with the DiT thereafter.
Pseudo-Demo Construction Ablations.

4.2. Evaluation Dataset
DB2 provides real piano–pop pairs for evaluation. We construct it
by pairing pop-song piano backing tracks from Sing2Piano2 and
KaraoKeysPH3 with their corresponding instrumentals. Retain-
ing original-key covers with duration differences of at most 5 s
yields 476 held-out pairs. These songs are excluded from DB1
(training and validation), and their piano-cover channels are dis-
joint from those used to train PiCoGen2. For each track and input
condition, we evaluate five 30 s windows, using strictly matched
window starts and deterministic seeds across systems.
We evaluate two input distributions at inference time. Training-
distribution inputs are synthetic piano MIDI derived from
held-out instrumentals using the corresponding training-data
source: MuScriptor transcriptions for Base, FST, and RDA, and
PiCoGen2-generated covers for PiCo. Real-world inputs are hu-
man piano covers transcribed using the high-resolution model of
Kong et al. [15], selected for its ability to estimate note velocities.
Manually Aligned Oracle Subset. Because the 476 pairs are
not temporally verified, we construct a 33-pair oracle by manually
shifting and pitch-preserving time-stretching each instrumental to
its piano. These pairs define the frozen APA reference (R, R′),
anchoring a correctly matched piano demo and production.

Bass+Drums and Piano Energy. Piano expansion must reassign
notes in the demo: low pitches toward bass, beats and downbeats
toward drums, and the melody toward a lead instrument. We ob-
served that some systems lack low-frequency energy or stay close
to the piano input. We treat these as failure modes and measure
two stem shares. We separate each excerpt with HTDemucs [14]
and report per-song mean shares of total power. B+D is the joint
bass+drums share; Piano is the piano-stem share. Both are re-
ported for real-piano inputs only (Table 2); closeness to GT is the

5. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Benefits of Pair-Supervised Pseudo Demos. Among fine-tuned
systems, PiCo has the best real-piano APA (0.964), FAD (0.231),
and spectral-shape RMSE (1.044; Table 2). Thus, pair-supervised
pseudo demos yield the closest adherence, distributional, and
spectral match to real productions under this evaluation. Its stem
profile also indicates stronger transformation (Figure 2), having
the least difference in instrument-energy shares from GT.
Augmentations for Pair-Free Pseudo Demos. RDA offers no
consistent objective gain over Base. FST and Base have similar
training-distribution FAD, but FST is much stronger on real pi-
ano (Table 2, Figure 2), in terms of objective metrics. In particu-
lar, Base and RDA under-produce bass and drums, whereas FST
restores rhythm-section energy closer to GT. Together, these re-
sults suggest that retained musical functions may matter more for
transfer than matching register and density alone. Though slightly
weaker than PiCo on real-piano objectives, FST remains a promis-
ing pair-free alternative with no pop-piano generator.
Baselines versus Fine-Tuned Piano2Pop Models. Both zero-
shot baselines fail at Piano2Pop. On real-piano inputs, all fine-
tuned systems significantly outperform them on APA and spectral-
shape RMSE (p<0.05; paired song-cluster bootstrap, 95% CI ex-
cluding zero), aligning with informal listening. They also signif-
icantly improve FAD over MuseControlLite; PiCo and FST show
the best real-piano performance (Table 2, Figure 2). ACE-Step
cover has near-floor APA and fails to match the input, which might
be due to its audio-latent conditioning: a large stylistic gap from a
piano demo to a full pop production is difficult to cross. MuseC-
ontrolLite, however, has the largest real-piano FAD and spectral-
shape error and remains largely piano, despite conditioning on
melody, dynamics, and rhythm, which should better bridge that
gap. We think zero-shot use, not only the stylistic gap, may be
limiting and calls for task-specific modeling.
6. CONCLUSION
We demonstrate Piano2Pop, expanding piano MIDI into instru-
mental pop productions through pseudo-supervised fine-tuning of
a flow-matching model on synthetic pairs. On real piano cov-
ers, pair-supervised pseudo demos achieve the best objective re-
sults, while function-selective transcription remains a promising
pair-free alternative. Both zero-shot baselines fail, suggesting that
Piano2Pop should be a task of its own and needs task-specific
modeling.
