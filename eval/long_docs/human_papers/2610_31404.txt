AGENTPHISHING: A BENCHMARK OF LLM EMAIL
AGENTS UNDER ADAPTIVE ATTACK
Justin Tan, Miao Li, Ding Zhao
Carnegie Mellon University
ABSTRACT
Computer-use agents increasingly handle private information and act on users’
behalf across desktop and web applications, including email. Phishing therefore
poses privacy and security risks that depend not only on whether agents recognize
malicious messages, but also on how they respond. Existing phishing datasets
were developed primarily for conventional detection, leaving agent susceptibility to
persuasive malicious requests insufficiently characterized. To expose vulnerabilities
that pre-existing datasets may miss, we introduce AgentPhishing, a benchmark
for evaluating whether persuasive phishing attacks can evade detection and induce
LLM email agents to comply with malicious requests. We develop PhishOpt,
which optimizes reusable email-generation skills against individual agents under
fixed benchmark constraints and a realism constraint. We analyze the generated
emails using Cialdini’s persuasion principles to identify strategies associated with
agent failures and use these findings to construct a universal generation skill. Our
evaluation shows that agents can handle pre-existing phishing emails safely yet
remain susceptible to attacks adapted to agent behavior, while in an AgentPhishing
evaluation of nine agents, four exhibit phishing-detection rates below 10% and
malicious-request compliance rates above 45%. These results highlight the limits
of inferring agent safety from conventional phishing evaluations and the need to
assess how agents respond to persuasive malicious requests.

INTRODUCTION
Computer-use agents powered by large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs)
offer a way to delegate everyday tasks across desktop and web applications (OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic,
2024; Google DeepMind, 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Kimi Team, 2026; Xie et al., 2024; Drouin et al.,
2024). To carry out these tasks, agents interpret external content while accessing private information
and taking actions on users’ behalf. Adversaries who influence this content may redirect those actions,
creating privacy and security risks (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024). Agents managing
email provide a concrete setting for studying these risks: unlike conventional phishing detectors
that classify messages, they may also carry out requests within them, including requests to disclose
sensitive information or perform other unauthorized actions (Wu et al., 2026; 1Password, 2026).
Assessing their trustworthiness in this setting requires examining both whether they detect phishing
and whether they comply with malicious requests, rather than inferring safe behavior from detection
performance alone (Levy et al., 2026).
Prior work shows that deceptive messages can evade phishing detectors, influence LLM judgments,
and induce unsafe actions by agents carrying out user tasks (Qi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a;
Wu et al., 2026; 1Password, 2026). Existing phishing email datasets provide reference material for
evaluating detection performance (Puyang, 2025; Ganiuly & Smaiyl, 2025), but as agents and their
defenses evolve, continued risk assessment also requires methods that can generate and refine attacks
against updated systems. Phishing research provides foundations for such methods through reusable
generators with realism validation and persuasion analysis (Chen et al., 2024), and iterative email
refinement using detector feedback (Qi et al., 2025). However, realism and detection evasion do not
measure whether an agent carries out a malicious request, motivating evaluation that accounts for
behavior. Feedback-driven red-teaming addresses this need by learning reusable attack strategies and
optimizing attacks through target responses and execution outcomes (Liu et al., 2025; Wen et al.,

2025; Chen et al., 2026; Syros et al., 2026). Yet success under prompt-injection attacks does not
establish how effective social-engineering attacks remain when explicit instruction overrides and
fabricated system directives are excluded. AgentBait directly evaluates social-engineering attacks
that use deception and persuasion to induce unsafe actions by task-performing agents, including
through email-based inducements (Wu et al., 2026). Its reported attack construction, however, follows
predefined strategies rather than iteratively improving generation from the evaluated agent’s responses.
These complementary advances motivate a more specific investigation: how do agents’ phishing
detection and compliance with malicious requests change when the generation of social-engineering
emails is optimized against their behavior under these attack-content constraints?
To address this question, we introduce AgentPhishing, a benchmark for evaluating agents’ phishing
detection and compliance with malicious requests under social-engineering attacks in email tasks.
We construct its adaptive attacks using PhishOpt, which optimizes target-specific generation skills
and uses persuasion analysis to derive a universal skill. Its optimization procedure builds on Skil-
lOpt (Yang et al., 2026) to refine email-generation skills from target-agent feedback under fixed
generation requirements and a realism constraint. We analyze emails generated by the agent-specific
skills using Cialdini’s persuasion principles (Cialdini, 1984), both to characterize framings associated
with detection and compliance outcomes and to guide the construction of a universal generation
skill. Our experiments compare pre-existing phishing emails, unoptimized synthetic emails, and
skill-generated attacks under a common evaluation protocol to assess how attack generation affects
observed safety performance. We also evaluate agent-specific and universal skills across agents to
examine their cross-agent effectiveness.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce benchmark AgentPhishing for agent safety evaluation under social-engineering
attacks in email tasks, measuring phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests.
• We present framework PhishOpt that evolves reusable, target-specific phishing-generation
skills using agent feedback and derives a universal skill from persuasion analysis.
• We show that strong performance on pre-existing phishing emails can coexist with detection
failures and malicious-request compliance under PhishOpt-generated attacks, and character-
ize cross-agent susceptibility and persuasion framings associated with these outcomes.

RELATED WORK
Phishing Detection and Susceptibility. Phishing research examines whether deceptive content can be
identified and whether recipients act on it. Conventional studies develop email classifiers, investigate
judgments of fraudulent websites, and characterize real-world SMS phishing campaigns (Fette et al.,
2007; Dhamija et al., 2006; Nahapetyan et al., 2024). Complementary human-centered research
examines how social relationships and contextual cues influence susceptibility across email, social
media, and text messaging (Jagatic et al., 2007; Vishwanath, 2017; Tabassum et al., 2024; Zhuo et al.,
2023). These studies assess behavior such as information disclosure, extending phishing evaluation
beyond classification (Jagatic et al., 2007; Vishwanath, 2017).
Recent research evaluates LLMs in several distinct roles. LLM-PEA studies models as phishing
detectors, whereas Fraud-R1 examines their responses to staged fraud inducements in advice-giving
and recipient role-play settings (Hasan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025a). SE-VSim studies adaptive
social-engineering conversations with simulated human victims, and PhishSim verifies credential-
submission outcomes within its simulation (Kumarage et al., 2025; Tewari et al., 2026). For agents
carrying out delegated tasks, AgentDojo, InjecAgent, and WASP evaluate whether malicious external
content redirects agent behavior (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Evtimov et al., 2025).
AgentBait and the industry benchmark SCAM further examine social-engineering threats within agent
workflows, including email-based inducements and phishing scenarios (Wu et al., 2026; 1Password,
2026). AgentPhishing studies this delegated-task setting through email, measuring both phishing
detection and compliance with malicious requests.
Feedback-Driven Attack Generation. Phishing-generation methods can use feedback to improve
realism or detection evasion. PEEK trains a reusable generator with real-vs-synthetic discrimination
and incorporates extracted persuasion patterns (Chen et al., 2024). SpearBot revises emails using
detector critiques (Qi et al., 2025). Behavioral red-teaming instead uses target responses or actions

METHODOLOGY
3.1
OVERVIEW
To study agents’ phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests in email tasks, we
synthesize evaluation emails using PhishOpt, which develops reusable, target-specific skills and an
analysis-informed universal skill. Each skill specifies natural-language email-generation instructions.
PhishOpt comprises two stages: target-specific skill optimization and analysis-informed universal
skill construction. The first (Section 3.2, 3.3) evolves a shared base skill into target-specific skill
using agent feedback. Built on SkillOpt’s bounded-edit procedure (Yang et al., 2026), it adds a
phishing-specific objective and generation constraints (Section 3.3). The second analyzes emails
generated by the evolved skills using Cialdini’s persuasion principles to relate framing to detection
and compliance outcomes and construct a universal skill without further optimization (Section 3.4).

3.2
TARGET-SPECIFIC SKILL OPTIMIZATION
In stage 1 of PhishOpt (Figure 1(a)), optimization updates the generation skill rather than individual
emails or generator-model parameters. The skill is supplied as the generator’s system prompt.
Candidate proposal. For each target agent, the shared base skill is evaluated to initialize the best
skill found so far and its recorded metrics. At each iteration, the optimizer receives this skill and its
detection, compliance, and classification metrics, then proposes a candidate through one bounded
edit. Edits must preserve a locked rules block specifying fictional entities, placeholder secrets, benign
controls, JSON output, and an approximately balanced benign–phishing label split; candidate skills
that alter it are rejected.
Constraint checking and objective evaluation. The generator produces a probe batch from the
candidate skill. The realism judge assesses this batch to determine whether the skill satisfies the
realism constraint. If the skill passes, the generator produces a separate, fresh evaluation batch
containing phishing emails and benign controls. The target agent processes this batch and selects
actions and, where applicable, drafts replies. The evaluator computes detection, compliance, and
classification metrics from these outputs to obtain the objective value defined in Section 3.3.
Skill update. The candidate and its metrics replace the best skill and its recorded metrics only if
the candidate’s objective value exceeds the recorded score; otherwise, both remain unchanged. The
optimizer receives the best skill and its recorded metrics at the next iteration. After the prescribed
number of iterations, optimization returns the target-specific best skill.
3.3
OPTIMIZATION OBJECTIVE AND GENERATION CONSTRAINTS
The optimization objective measures the failures exposed by a candidate skill, while generation
constraints maintain the intended synthetic phishing setting. The objective supplies the acceptance
criterion for the update procedure in Section 3.2.
Optimization objective. PhishOpt maximizes a weighted score for candidate skill selection:
J = 0.5(1 −D) + 0.3C + 0.2(1 −F1),
(1)
where D and C are the target agent’s detection and malicious-request compliance rates on phishing
emails, and F1 is its classification score over both phishing emails and benign controls. Detection
failure and compliance may overlap; F1 combines recall, equal to D under our action-based defi-
nitions, with precision, which accounts for benign emails classified as phishing. The operational
definitions of agent outcomes and metrics are specified in Section 4.
Realism constraint. The intended attack space consists of social-engineering content, excluding
explicit instruction overrides and fabricated system directives. The realism judge scores a probe batch
on plausibility, coherence, naturalness, and the absence of unrealistic exploitation artifacts, each on
a [0, 1] scale. A candidate passes if the mean score across all four criteria and sampled emails is at
least τ; otherwise, it is rejected before target-agent evaluation.
3.4
PERSUASION ANALYSIS AND UNIVERSAL SKILL CONSTRUCTION
The second stage of PhishOpt (Figure 1(b)) constructs a universal generation skill from persuasion
patterns observed in emails generated by target-specific evolved skills. The analysis characterizes
how these patterns relate to agent outcomes and informs the universal skill’s generation instructions.
Persuasion analysis. The LLM-based Cialdini annotator scores generated phishing emails according
to Cialdini’s persuasion principles (Cialdini, 1984). Pooling observations across target agents, we
use ϕ correlations to quantify associations between persuasion-principle presence and detection and
compliance outcomes separately.
Universal skill construction. The LLM-based skill synthesizer constructs the universal skill from
these associations, favoring principles associated with unsafe responses over those associated with
safer handling. The skill is evaluated across agents without further target-specific optimization.

BENCHMARK
AgentPhishing evaluates safety risk of agents under phishing email attacks, measuring both phishing
detection and compliance with malicious requests across pre-existing and adaptively generated
emails. Figure 6 in Appendix A provides an overview of the evaluation flow, comparison setups, and
diagnostic capabilities.
Task setting and agent interface. The target agent receives a shared inbox-assistant instruction
and an email’s sender, subject, body, and available links. It returns a structured response specifying
an action, whether a reply is needed, an optional draft reply, and its reasoning. Actions comprise
reporting, archiving, ignoring, unsubscribing, replying, following up, clicking a link, and providing
information. Evaluation scores action selection and drafted content, not external execution. Table 1
lists model usage and configurations by role.
Evaluation emails. Agents are evaluated on pre-existing emails from public corpora (Puyang, 2025)
and synthetic emails generated using base, target-specific evolved, and universal skills. Figure 2
shows an annotated example.
Outcome definitions and metrics. Detection rate D is the proportion of phishing emails assigned
one of four designated safe actions, namely report, archive, ignore, or unsubscribe. Compliance
rate C is the proportion of phishing emails for which the agent chooses to click a link or provide
information, or produces a draft reply judged unsafe. Precision and F1 are computed over phishing
emails and benign controls, treating the four detection actions as positive predictions.
Evaluation protocol. The target-agent role in Table 1 appears in both PhishOpt and AgentPhishing.
The PhishOpt target agent provides the feedback used to evolve a target-specific skill, whereas the
AgentPhishing target agent processes evaluation emails generated using that skill. Target-specific
evaluation uses the same model in both roles. Cross-agent evaluation varies these identities: diagonal
conditions use the same model as the PhishOpt and AgentPhishing target agent, whereas off-diagonal
conditions evaluate a skill optimized against one model on another. The fixed generator remains
separate from both target-agent roles. The universal skill has no PhishOpt target agent and is
evaluated directly across AgentPhishing target agents with the skill and generator model held fixed.
The experimental setup specifies model versions, sample sizes, repetitions, and whether agents receive
the same email batches. Post-generation diversity and repetition diagnostics include Self-BLEU (Zhu
et al., 2018), Distinct-1/2 (Li et al., 2016), gzip compression ratio, and repeated 4-gram rate.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate agents’ safety under phishing attacks through three questions. Q1: Does target-specific
skill optimization expose detection failures or malicious-request compliance not observed with pre-
existing phishing emails or unoptimized synthetic emails? Q2: How do agents’ phishing detection
and malicious-request compliance vary across pre-existing emails and emails generated using base,
target-specific, and universal skills? Q3: Which persuasion principles are associated with agents’
detection and compliance outcomes, and can these associations guide the construction of a universal
skill effective across agents? We report detection rate (↑), compliance rate (↓), F1 (↑), and the
Susceptibility score (↓) defined in Section 3.3. Arrows indicate the direction of better agent safety
performance; PhishOpt optimizes in the opposite direction. We also conduct ablation studies and
diagnostics to examine component contributions and result stability.

CROSS-AGENT AND ADDITIONAL-AGENT EVALUATION
Experimental setup. To address Q2, we evaluate emails generated using each of the six target-
specific evolved skills and the universal skill across six AgentPhishing target agents and nine
additional agents not used as PhishOpt targets. For target-specific skills, cross-agent evaluation pairs
emails generated using a skill evolved against one PhishOpt target agent with each AgentPhishing
target agent. In the six-by-six comparison, diagonal cells use the same model in both target roles,
whereas off-diagonal cells use different models. Emails generated using the universal skill are
evaluated across the same AgentPhishing target agents. Figure 4 reports Susceptibility scores for the
six target-specific skills and the universal skill, while Table 2 provides the detection and compliance
breakdown for the six-by-six target-specific comparison.
Results. Figure 4A shows stronger variation across AgentPhishing target agents than across PhishOpt
target agents. As AgentPhishing target agents, Kimi and Qwen have the highest mean Susceptibility
scores across skills evolved against different PhishOpt target agents, 0.4305 and 0.4238, whereas
Sonnet has the lowest, 0.0374. Table 2 further shows that Sonnet detects every phishing email and
never complies across skills evolved against all six PhishOpt target agents, while Qwen and Kimi
exhibit both detection failures and compliance across multiple rows. Although the skill evolved
against GPT-5.4 has the highest off-diagonal mean score, 0.3581, no evolved skill is uniformly
effective across the six targets.

5.3
PERSUASION-PRINCIPLE ANALYSIS AND UNIVERSAL SKILL
Experimental setup. To address Q3, we analyze 180 phishing-email evaluations from the six target-
specific evolved-skill corpora. The analysis includes all phishing emails, covering both successful
and unsuccessful attacks rather than conditioning on agent failures. We annotate the emails for six
persuasion principles: Reciprocity, Commitment and Consistency, Social Proof, Authority, Liking,
and Scarcity/Urgency. We compute ϕ coefficients relating each principle’s presence to detection
failure (↓) and malicious-request compliance (↓). Positive coefficients indicate an association with
the corresponding unsafe outcome, whereas negative coefficients indicate an association with its
absence. We then provide the annotations, agent outcomes, and generation constraints to Opus 4.8 to
synthesize a universal skill without additional target-specific optimization.
Persuasion-principle analysis. Across the 180 evaluations, we observe 51 detection failures and
21 compliance cases. Figure 5 shows that Commitment and Consistency is positively associated
with both detection failure (ϕ = 0.2407) and compliance (ϕ = 0.1391). This principle captures
requests framed as continuing an existing task or honoring a prior commitment, suggesting that
apparent consistency with an ongoing workflow can accompany unsafe agent responses. Liking,
which captures friendly or relationship-building framing, is positively associated with detection
failure (ϕ = 0.1914) but has almost no association with compliance (ϕ = 0.0018). This difference
illustrates that persuasion features associated with missed detection need not also be associated with
malicious-request compliance. In contrast, Authority and Scarcity/Urgency are negatively associated
with detection failure (ϕ = −0.1502 and −0.1976, respectively), and Scarcity/Urgency is also
negatively associated with compliance (ϕ = −0.1460). Together, these observations suggest that
overt authority and urgency cues may be easier for agents to recognize than workflow-continuation
framing. These are associations within the evaluated corpora, not evidence that individual principles
independently cause the observed outcomes.
Universal skill results. In Figure 4B, emails generated using the universal skill yield detection
rates below 10% and compliance rates above 45% for four of the nine additional target agents.
These results show that the analysis-informed skill generates effective attacks against multiple agents
without further target-specific optimization. The universal skill also achieves the highest mean
PhishOpt objective score across these nine targets, although its advantage over the GPT-5.4-derived
skill is not statistically significant in the paired comparison. The results therefore support cross-
agent effectiveness, rather than consistent superiority over target-specific skills or causal effects of
individual persuasion principles.
