ABSTRACT
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting elicits step-by-step rationales that appear to
explain model predictions, but there is no guarantee these steps causally drive
the final answer. In standard autoregressive generation, dense attention allows
each reasoning step to attend to the full context, enabling the model to derive
answers through direct lookup while generating plausible-sounding but post-hoc
justifications. We introduce CausalCoT, an inference framework that enforces
causal transparency by decomposing problems into a directed acyclic graph of
atomic sub-questions, evaluating each node in an isolated context containing only
its direct causal parents. This Markovian structure removes attention pathways to
non-parent content, making downstream predictions sensitive to local reasoning
rather than global context leakage. Across a diverse suite of reasoning benchmarks,
CausalCoT preserves competitive predictive performance while improving expla-
nation faithfulness, increasing causal sensitivity of intermediate reasoning steps by
an average of 12 and up to 21 percentage points over the strongest baseline. The
structured dependency graph additionally enables test-time interventions in which
local corrections propagate predictably through downstream reasoning.

INTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains like healthcare,
where user trust is crucial (Chen et al., 2024). Here, non-experts frequently rely on LLM-generated
rationales, treating them as transparent windows into the model’s decision-making (Barez et al.,
2025). However, this trust is often misplaced. While techniques like Reinforcement Learning
from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022), Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), and Chain of Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2023) produce highly plausible
text, they optimize for human preference rather than mechanistic transparency. Consequently, these
explanations are frequently unfaithful to the model’s internal logic (Mayne et al., 2026; Matton et al.,
2025; Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023). Alarmingly, even researchers sometimes misuse
these persuasive post-hoc rationalizations with true explanations (Barez et al., 2025).
This fundamental disconnect is a critical barrier to safe deployment. Specifically, standard CoT
prompting suffers from severe causal opacity driven by attention leakage, the phenomenon where
dense, global attention mechanisms allow a model to implicitly bleed information across its entire

To bridge the gap between plausible generation and faithful reasoning, we introduce Causal Chain-
of-Thought (CausalCoT)1. CausalCoT enforces a strict transparent inference structure to prevent
attention leakage, ensuring that a model’s stated reasoning correctly dictates its final prediction. Our
approach operates in two phases: (i) the model decomposes a complex query into a topological plan,
a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of interdependent sub-questions; (ii) each node is evaluated in
isolation, constrained entirely to its specific query and the explicitly routed answer of its direct parents
(or the initial input, for root nodes). By enforcing this Markovian property, we construct a network
of question-answer pairs where each step is causally driven only by its local dependencies. This
architectural constraint removes the spurious contextual leakage of standard autoregressive generation
during the evaluation phase. Furthermore, it natively supports counterfactual simulatability, allowing
users to intervene on internal reasoning nodes and propagate those logical changes downstream. A
comparison between CoT and CausalCoT is shown in Figure 1.

BACKGROUND
Chain-of-Thought and LLM Explanations. LLMs are fundamentally optimized to model and
generate human-like text. Because human explanations are typically structured to be clear, helpful,
and persuasive, LLMs naturally exploit these stylistic traits, especially when fine-tuned via RLHF,
where helpfulness and clarity are explicit reward signals. To enhance complex reasoning capabilities
at inference time, prompting strategies such as CoT (Wei et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023; Besta et al.,
2024) and related evolutions encourage the model to output a step-by-step rationale before producing
a final answer. Recent training paradigms, such as GRPO (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), even bake
this step-by-step generation directly into the model’s policy. While these mechanisms produce
highly plausible rationales that are easily trusted by non-expert users, they optimize for coherent text
generation rather than enforcing strict dependencies between intermediate reasoning steps.
The Illusion of Faithfulness. Generated reasoning traces frequently fail to reflect a model’s true
decision mechanisms (Matton et al., 2025; McMillan et al., 2025; Lanham et al., 2023). This
unfaithfulness typically manifests either as counterfactual inconsistency (Turpin et al., 2023; Matton
et al., 2025; Mayne et al., 2026), where context perturbations change the model behavior in ways
that contradict its stated rules, or as post-hoc rationalization (Lanham et al., 2023; Bogdan et al.,
2025), where intermediate steps act merely as superficial justifications lacking causal influence
over predictions. Here, we focus on mitigating post-hoc rationalization by enforcing a transparent
inference process where each step is causally determined solely by its predecessors or the input. To
quantify post-hoc rationalization, we evaluate causal reliance via intermediate step perturbations
(Lanham et al., 2023): modifying, truncating, or corrupting reasoning steps during inference; if
modifying a step leaves the final prediction unchanged, the trace lacks causal efficacy and is unfaithful.
See Appendix B for details on faithfulness metrics.

METHOD: CAUSAL CHAIN-OF-THOUGHT (CAUSALCOT)
3.1
RATIONALE: THE ROOTS OF UNFAITHFUL REASONING
We hypothesize that the unfaithfulness observed in standard LLM reasoning stems from two funda-
mental mechanisms inherent to their architecture and training:
1. Information Leakage via Attention (Lanham et al., 2023; Radhakrishnan et al., 2023): In
standard autoregressive generation, the Transformer’s attention mechanism allows every generated
token to attend to all preceding tokens. Crucially, this leakage is transitive: because a previous token’s
latent representation has already absorbed information from earlier tokens across previous layers,
attending to it implicitly carries forward the entire historical context. Consequently, each reasoning
step is influenced by the entire context window, both directly and indirectly. Even when tokens are
logically unrelated to a specific intermediate deduction, non-zero attention scores permit spurious
information to seep through. This explains why intervening on a generated reasoning step often fails
to alter the final prediction: the model bypasses the explicit stated logical chain and retrieves the
necessary signal directly from the original input (as illustrated in Figure 2).
2. Reward-Driven Token Optimization (Turpin et al., 2023; Matton et al., 2025; Perez et al., 2022;
Chen et al., 2025):

3.2
THE CAUSALCOT FRAMEWORK
To address these fundamental flaws, we introduce Causal Chain-of-Thought (CausalCoT). Our
framework explicitly decouples the generation of a logical plan from its execution, structuring
inference to suppress attention-based leakage and enforce strict dependencies. As illustrated in Figure
2, CausalCoT shifts away from the leakage-prone, continuous attention of standard autoregressive
reasoning by dividing the inference process into two distinct isolated phases: Graph Generation and
Context-Isolated Question Evaluation. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix G.
Formal Problem Setup. Let X ∈X denote the input prompt (e.g., a complex reasoning question)
and Y ∈Y denote the target final answer. We assume access to a pre-trained LLM, denoted
as a policy πθ. In standard autoregressive CoT prompting, the model generates a sequence of
intermediate reasoning tokens R = (r1, r2, . . . , rm) prior to predicting the final answer, such that
Y ∼πθ(· | X, R). Because the generation of each intermediate token rt is heavily conditioned on the
entire preceding context—i.e., rt ∼πθ(· | X, r<t). This dense attention mechanism creates severe
causal opacity. It makes it impossible to guarantee whether a given token rt is logically derived from
relevant predecessors, or spuriously extracted from X or unrelated segments of R<t. Our objective is
to replace the opaque autoregressive reasoning trace R with an externally auditable directed graph
structure, where each intermediate generation is conditioned only on explicitly routed inputs. This
does not claim to expose the model’s internal computation; rather, it constrains the deployed inference
procedure so that downstream generations cannot directly attend to non-parent reasoning content.
Phase 1: Graph Generation. Rather than allowing the model to autoregressively stream a continuous
CoT, we first prompt the LLM to act exclusively as a planner. Given an input prompt X, the model
decomposes the problem into a set of discrete, atomic sub-questions and constructs a DAG, denoted
as G = (V, E). Here, each node vi ∈V represents a specific sub-question, and each directed edge
(vi, vj) ∈E indicates that the derived answer ai for question vi is necessary to solve vj. By explicitly
mapping these dependencies a priori, we leverage the LLM’s robust zero-shot planning capabilities
while strictly preventing it from simultaneously executing the logic. This decoupling reduces one
source of entanglement between planning and execution: the structural design of the reasoning path
is not contaminated by the model’s bias toward plausible explanations that do not align with the
execution.
4


Phase 2: Context-Isolated Question Evaluation. Once the graph G is instantiated, we execute the
reasoning trace by traversing the graph topologically. Each node is evaluated using the same LLM,
but strictly within an isolated context window:
• Root Nodes: For any initial node with no parents (vroot), the LLM is prompted only with
the original input X and the specific sub-question defined by vroot.
• Internal and Leaf Nodes: For any descendant node vj, the LLM is prompted with the sub-
question vj alongside the answers of its direct parent nodes, denoted as Pa(vj). Crucially,
the model does not have access to the overarching global input X, nor to any sibling or
parallel reasoning branches.
By enforcing this strict topological evaluation, we remove direct attention links from a node to non-
parent reasoning content. The generated answer to a specific question vj is determined exclusively by
Pa(vj). This forced Markovian property guarantees that the final prediction is a faithful product of
the intermediate steps. The complete CausalCoT inference procedure is formalized in Algorithm 1.
3.3
INFERENCE PROPERTIES AND HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP INTERVENTIONS
The structural transparency of CausalCoT natively unlocks several powerful inference-time properties
that are fundamentally intractable under standard autoregressive generation. By decoupling the
reasoning plan from its execution, users can actively interact with and steer the model’s logic at
multiple stages:
• Structural Auditing and Test-Time Interventions: Before reasoning begins, the gener-
ated DAG G = (V, E) is fully exposed for a priori inspection. Experts can rectify flawed
logic, value misalignments, or missing steps by adding or removing sub-questions v ∈V,
modifying context, or redefining edges E. Inspired by concept interventions in interpretable
architectures (Koh et al., 2020; Dominici et al., 2024), experts can also perform test-time
surgical corrections on incorrect or hallucinated intermediate nodes; due to topological iso-
lation, only downstream descendants require recomputation. Unlike standard autoregressive
CoT, where dense attention causes localized edits to trigger unpredictable cascades that
destabilize reasoning, CausalCoT enables predictable, efficient steering to improve final
predictive performance.
• Faithful Counterfactual Simulation: The forced Markovian property natively supports
counterfactual simulations via explicit causal interventions (conceptually equivalent to
Pearl’s do-operator, e.g., do(vi = x)). Users can cleanly intervene on the value produced
by a node (or on the information in the input), and dynamically propagate these changes
through the graph (keeping it fixed). Since the environment is sealed against non-causal
leakage and the ancestors of v are dropped from every downstream context and cannot
re-enter the resulting final answer, Y is a faithful reflection of the counterfactual intervention,
under the same generated graph.

EXPERIMENTS
We design our experiments to evaluate the efficacy, faithfulness, and intervenability of CausalCoT
compared to standard autoregressive reasoning. Specifically, we aim to answer the following research
questions:
• RQ1 (Performance): Does enforcing a strict causal graph degrade task accuracy compared
to standard CoT?
• RQ2 (Faithfulness): Are the intermediate steps genuine drivers of the generation? Specifi-
cally, if an intermediate reasoning step is modified, does the model structurally rely on it,
and does the final prediction shift in a predictable direction?
• RQ3 (Intervenability): Can CausalCoT reliably integrate test-time interventions to correct
intermediate reasoning steps?

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets. We evaluate CausalCoT across performance, faithfulness, and intervenability using diverse
benchmarks. We first use GPQA (Rein et al., 2023), a graduate-level science dataset, to ensure our
model maintains competitive reasoning capabilities. Next, we incorporate MedQA (Jin et al., 2020)
to test multi-step clinical deduction in high-stakes domains. Finally, we leverage the Counterfactual
Simulatability Suite (CF Suite) (Mayne et al., 2026) across health, business, and ethics to quantify
causal transparency and responsiveness to interventions. Combined, these benchmarks provide a
robust foundation for assessing both task accuracy and model interpretability.
Models and Baselines. We evaluate three families of models (Gemma 3, GPT-5.4, and Claude
4.5/4.6) across varying parameter scales. We compare CausalCoT against standard CoT prompting
and Factored Decomposition (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023). Factored Decomposition isolates reasoning
by breaking queries into sub-questions and evaluating them in restricted context windows. However,
it suffers from two structural flaws: intermediate steps lack explicit parental routing, and the final
prediction concatenates all generated text, reintroducing pervasive attention leakage. CausalCoT
resolves these vulnerabilities by enforcing a DAG architecture, guaranteeing pure context isolation.
Further details on baseline mechanics and comparison with CausalCoT are further explained in
Appendix C.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate our three research questions using the following metrics:
• Predictive Performance (RQ1): We report standard Accuracy (Acc) to determine if
CausalCoT’s strict DAG constraints degrade foundational problem-solving capabilities.
We also report Pass@3 (the probability of generating at least one correct answer across
three independent inferences). Because CausalCoT strictly enforces the generated topology,
a malformed initial graph guarantees predictive failure. We therefore evaluate whether
multi-sample generation mitigates zero-shot planning bottlenecks by providing multiple
opportunities to produce a valid response.
• Faithfulness and Predictability (RQ2): To quantify the causal influence of intermediate
reasoning, we adapt established step-perturbation methodologies (Lanham et al., 2023).
By injecting targeted errors using a frontier model (GPT-5.4) into intermediate nodes, we
measure the frequency of downstream answer changes. To distinguish genuine causal
adherence from arbitrary perturbation instability, we introduce a Predictable Change metric.
This metric relies on an LLM judge that determines the logical downstream implication
of each injected error, accounting for instances where a perturbation is insufficient to alter
the final label. We then evaluate whether the model’s corrupted conclusion matches this
counterfactual target, confirming that downstream predictions predictably adhere to the
modified premise.
• Test-Time Intervenability (RQ3): To evaluate the practical utility of our framework’s
"human-in-the-loop" capabilities, we simulate expert interventions using a frontier proxy
model (GPT-5.4) tasked with identifying and correcting erroneous reasoning steps. Both
CausalCoT and Factored Decomposition support multi-level interventions, allowing the
proxy to explicitly correct either an initial sub-question (planning) or a generated intermedi-
ate answer (execution). However, CausalCoT’s strict DAG topology provides the routing
necessary to propagate these surgical edits to downstream dependent steps. On the other
hand, CoT supports only the correction of intermediate steps. By measuring downstream
predictive improvements across an intervention budget of k ∈{1, 2, 3}, we quantify how
efficiently these edits correct the model’s reasoning.
