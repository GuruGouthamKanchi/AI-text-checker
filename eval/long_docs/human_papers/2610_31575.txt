Dynamics of random recurrent neural networks:
colored noise and sublinear activations
Germán Mato1,2, Facundo Rigatuso1,2, Gonzalo Torroba1,2
1 Instituto Balseiro, UNCuyo and CNEA
2Centro Atómico Bariloche and CONICET

Abstract
We study the dynamics of recurrent neural networks with random couplings, sublin-
ear odd activation functions, and temporally correlated noise. Using large N methods,
we determine stationary correlations and distinguish the loss of local linear stabil-
ity from the onset of chaos, characterized by a positive largest Lyapunov exponent.
These thresholds delimit an intermediate regime in which local instability coexists with
asymptotic stability under a common noise history. Their separation depends on both
the noise correlation time and the asymptotic growth of the activation function. We
show that internal noise with any finite correlation time cuts off the divergence of
the network’s intrinsic correlation time τcorr at the edge of chaos; it exhibits a critical
scaling τcorr,max ∼1/√D0, where D0 is the integrated noise strength. Static random
input instead produces coincident thresholds and a divergent correlation time. We also
calculate the frequency-dependent participation dimension from a four-point correla-
tion function. Its zero-frequency value is inversely proportional to the fourth power of
the network correlation time, predicting a smooth minimum at the weak-noise chaos
threshold. Numerical simulations support the large-network predictions and reveal ap-
preciable finite-size effects.
Keywords: neural population dynamics; recurrent neural network; nonlinear dynamics;
chaos

Introduction
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) play a key role for various computational tasks in biolog-
ical and artificial systems [1, 2]. An important goal is to understand their dynamics, given
that the dynamical state of the system strongly influences its computational capabilities [3].
The seminal work of Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers [4] established that large recur-
rent networks with random, asymmetric couplings and nonlinear activation can undergo a
transition to chaos. Crucially, networks that operate near the transition to chaos are found
to perform optimally [5, 6, 7]. The dynamical mean-field theory of [4] characterized the ac-
tivity correlations and largest Lyapunov exponent. Subsequent path-integral work provided
a systematic derivation of the mean-field description and its stability properties [8]. These
results made random recurrent networks a tractable setting for studying how microscopic
interactions generate complex temporal activity.
Fluctuating inputs add important modifications to this problem.
Correlation theory for
stochastic neural networks showed how network interactions shape temporal and cross-neuron
correlations [9]. Additive noise was shown early to suppress chaos in random neural networks
[10]. Deterministic periodic input can also suppress intrinsic chaos, with an effectiveness that
depends nonmonotonically on the driving frequency [11]. For driven random networks, [12]
found that time-dependent internal white noise can suppress chaos and shift its onset to
stronger recurrent coupling. Their analysis also revealed the existence of an intermediate
regime between the onset of local instability and the transition to chaos. Thus, the response
to a small perturbation and the long-term growth of differences between trajectories describe
distinct aspects of the dynamics. The intermediate regime is locally unstable but nonchaotic.
These two transitions also provide a useful framework for interpreting changes in population
covariance and effective recurrent coupling [13]. Related work on spiking circuits identified
mechanisms for asynchronous chaotic rate fluctuations with distinct characteristic time scales
[14].
In this work we study two aspects of RNNs that can qualitatively affect the dynamics: the
temporal structure of disordered input and the activation function. First, prior works have
mostly focused on the case of internal white noise, which corresponds to a vanishingly small
correlation time. However, realistic internal fluctuations arising from neural and synaptic
processes can persist over a finite interval. We will model this with an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
(OU) input that introduces a tunable correlation time τn. Varying τn allows us to ask how
internal colored noise affects network correlations, local instability and the onset of chaos.
Second, the activation function affects the dynamics in key ways. Many classical analyses
use a bounded, saturating response, but other response shapes may be relevant over the
range of activity sampled by the network. For example, type-I excitability is associated
with a square-root dependence of firing rate on input near onset [15, 16, 17]. Near critical
behavior generated by linear activation functions has been found to be relevant for describing
brain activity [18, 19]. Previous work has shown that nonlinear response properties influence
effective recurrent coupling and population correlations [13, 20]. In this work we use a smooth
2


family of activation functions that spans saturating and sublinear (nonsaturating) responses
and approaches a linear response as a limiting case.
Our goal is to determine, both theoretically and via numerical simulations, the dynamics
of RNNs. In particular, we seek to understand how the colored noise and the form of the
activation function affect the unstable dynamics and the development of chaos. We derive
the large-network correlation equations, and a matched short-/long-time solution that repro-
duces the simulated correlators without measured input. We then examine two dynamical
thresholds: local instability, from the local stability criterion, and chaos, from the sign of the
largest Lyapunov exponent, which we obtain from a Schrödinger problem whose potential is
built from the correlator of the derivative of the activation function. The two thresholds are
distinct, with an intermediate regime that is locally unstable but not chaotic, and simula-
tions map their separation as a function of the activation exponent and the noise correlation
time. Near the edge of chaos, internal noise of any finite correlation time cuts off the di-
vergence of the intrinsic correlation time with a universal exponent, τcorr,max ∼D−1/2

in the limit D0 →0 of integrated noise strength. For weak noise the maximum sits at the
chaos threshold. The exponent is the same for white and colored noise. We also investigate
whether these dynamical regimes differ in the geometric organization of population activity,
as characterized by the participation dimension [21]. Its evaluation requires a four-point
correlation function, which captures the contribution of correlations between neurons to the
effective dimensionality of the activity. We find that the zero-frequency participation di-
mension is controlled by the same intrinsic time scale, DPR(0)/N = (τ/τcorr)4, so that it

determine the relevance of finite-size effects in the different regimes.
The paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 introduces the random recurrent network,
the colored-noise process, the family of activation functions, and the numerical methods.
Section 3 develops the large-N mean-field theory, analyzes the temporal correlations, and
derives criteria for local linear instability and chaos. Section 4 studies the dynamics in the
weak disorder limit, obtaining explicit results for the instability and chaos thresholds and for
the network correlation time. Section 5 compares the theoretical predictions with simulations
and presents phase diagrams showing how both transition thresholds depend on the noise
correlation time and activation function. Section 6 examines four-point correlations and the
participation dimension of the network activity. Section 7 contains a discussion of the results
and conclusions. The appendices provide details of the collective-field and replica derivations
and discuss finite-size effects.

chaotic regime is defined by the largest Lyapunov exponent ΛL becoming positive. We will
give more details of the theoretical calculations below in Sec. 3.3. The largest Lyapunov
exponent is estimated with the standard Benettin two-replica algorithm [23]: two copies
of the network are evolved under the same realization of W and driven by the same noise
history ξi(t) (i.e. the same ηi(t) at every step), starting from initial conditions separated by a
small distance d0; after each step the separation d(t) = ∥ϕ(1)(t)−ϕ(2)(t)∥is measured and the
second trajectory is rescaled back onto the sphere of radius d0 around the first, accumulating
log(d/d0). After discarding an initial transient, the running time-average of this quantity
converges to ΛL.
The scan is organized as a double loop over network size N and coupling λ at fixed p (with
β, Dξ, τn, τ fixed); for each (N, λ) several independent W-realizations are drawn, and for
each W several noise replicas are run. This nested structure allows the disorder-vs-noise
decomposition of the variance of any observable X (e.g. Cϕ(0) or ΛL) into a between-W
(quenched) component and a within-W, between-replica (annealed) component, which is
the numerical counterpart of the self-averaging property assumed in the large-N derivation
of Sec. 3. Additional diagnostics recorded for each (N, λ) include the spread of the time-
averaged activity ¯ϕi across neurons (a check that no neuron is atypical), and the power
spectrum of ϕi(t) (used to detect any oscillatory instability).
