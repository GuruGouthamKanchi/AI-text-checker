Abstract
World action models increasingly rely on pretrained video
models assumed to capture physical dynamics for pre-
diction, planning, and action generation. Existing evalu-
ations assess plausibility, probe representations, or mea-
sure predictive surprise, but rarely identify which history-
conditioned physical expectations conflict with observa-
tions. We introduce PhysTrace1, a training-free frame-
work aligning MLLM-generated, history-conditioned ex-
pectations with observed video windows to temporally lo-
calize physical violations and provide interpretable descrip-
tions without task-specific probes or classifiers. We also in-
troduce IntPhys2-Timing, adding violation intervals to Int-
Phys2. PhysTrace achieves a Hit Rate of up to 70.7% across
physical principles on IntPhys2-Timing and outperforms
the compared methods in overall possible–impossible dis-
crimination accuracy on IntPhys2. Applied to reconstructed
videos along the trajectories of pretrained diffusion/flow-
based video models, PhysTrace reveals attribute- and noise-
dependent patterns in physical violation localization, en-
abling interpretable physical consistency analysis across

1. Introduction
In robotic control, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) mod-
els have rapidly advanced by leveraging semantic knowl-
edge and generalization capabilities acquired through large-
scale vision-language pretraining for robot action genera-
tion [8, 15, 19, 30, 49]. More recently, research has increas-
ingly explored leveraging future dynamics and predictive
and spatiotemporal priors acquired through video pretrain-
ing for action prediction and planning [3, 16, 20, 23, 39, 45].
In particular, diffusion/flow-based generative video models
have emerged as key pretrained backbones for these ap-
proaches, as they can directly generate future visual dy-
namics [9, 28, 29, 35]. This growing reliance on pretrained
video models makes it increasingly important to determine
whether they have learned the physical dynamics required
for robotic control.
To this end, various benchmarks and evaluation meth-
ods have been proposed to assess intuitive physics, phys-
ical plausibility, and motion consistency in input or gen-
erated videos [2, 7, 11, 25, 32, 46, 48]. In parallel, prob-
ing studies have investigated the physical information en-

line of work has also extended beyond encoder-based rep-

tations of diffusion-based video generators, showing that
physics-related information can be decoded from pretrained
representations. However, these approaches primarily focus
on predefined physical labels, model-level preferences, or
physics-related signals encoded in intermediate representa-
tions. Even when a physical inconsistency can be detected,
they provide limited insight into which history-conditioned
physical expectation conflicts with the observed behavior.
More fundamentally, physical consistency is not a static
visual property of individual frames, but a temporal prop-
erty that emerges from changes in object identity, state, mo-
tion, and interaction across time. Sparse or short observa-
tions may therefore miss brief violations or lack the context
needed to determine what should have occurred. Likewise,
a single plausibility score cannot reveal where the expected
and observed dynamics begin to diverge. Evaluating physi-
cal consistency thus requires continuously relating local ob-
servations to the physical expectations established by their
temporal history.
Rather than probing intermediate representations, a nat-
ural alternative is to directly ask a Multimodal Large Lan-
guage Model (MLLM) to assess physical plausibility from
video. However, Pixels to Principles [6] reports near-chance
performance of recent MLLMs in challenging intuitive-
physics settings on GRASP [17] and IntPhys 2 [11], de-
spite physics-related cues being recoverable from their vi-
sual representations. Similarly, QuantiPhy [22] shows that,
in quantitative kinematic inference from video, multimodal
models often rely on pretrained world knowledge rather
than faithfully integrating visual evidence with the provided
physical prior. These findings suggest that current MLLMs
are unreliable as direct judges of physical validity. Instead,
we use the MLLM for reasoning over temporal context to
formulate expected states, while physical consistency is as-
sessed against the corresponding video evidence.

a

in videos through history-conditioned temporal video-

the general contextual reasoning capability of an MLLM to
formulate the state expected at the current time based on
prior observations as temporal context. It then compares
this expected state with the actual video observation in a
shared multimodal embedding space, thereby tracing phys-

events, this process enables temporal localization of physi-
cal violations while the jointly generated expected and ob-
served states provide an interpretable description of which
physical expectation diverges from the actual observation.
To quantitatively evaluate this temporal behavior, we con-
struct IntPhys2-Timing by augmenting IntPhys2 [11] with
violation intervals, enabling evaluation of physical viola-
tion localization in video reconstructions across generative
timesteps.
In summary, we propose a training-free framework for
tracing physical consistency over time through history-
conditioned temporal video-language alignment, together
with IntPhys2-Timing for evaluating the temporal localiza-
tion of physical violations.

2. Related Work
2.1. VLA and World-Model-Based Policies
VLA models couple pretrained vision-language represen-
tations with robot action generation, allowing large-scale
multimodal knowledge to inform language-conditioned
control [8, 15, 19, 30, 49]. Beyond this paradigm, re-
cent work has explored leveraging predictive dynamics and
spatiotemporal priors learned from video for robot policy
learning. VPP [16] extracts predictive visual representations
from a video diffusion model for action prediction without
explicit future visual rollouts.
More direct approaches, including World Action Mod-
els (WAMs) and related world-model-based policies, couple
future prediction with action generation or planning [3, 20,
23, 39, 45]. Video Policy [23] couples future video gener-
ation with action generation, while DreamZero [45] jointly
models future world states and actions using a pretrained
video diffusion backbone. EVA [39] generates future vi-
sual rollouts and uses an inverse dynamics model to derive
executable actions and provide an executability signal for
aligning the video world model. Cosmos Policy [20] gener-
ates actions as latent frames and predicts future states and
values for model-based planning, whereas V-JEPA2-AC [3]
predicts action-conditioned future states in latent space for
planning. Despite their architectural differences, these ap-
proaches share the goal of exploiting predictive dynamics
learned from video as priors for robot control.

2.2. Pretrained Video World Models as Policy Pri-
ors
Prior work has explored different classes of video mod-
els for learning representations and dynamics relevant to
robot control. Self-supervised video representation mod-
els learn temporal structure without explicit robot actions.
V-JEPA2 [3] follows a latent predictive objective, while
VideoMAE V2 [38] learns general spatiotemporal repre-
sentations through masked video reconstruction. A separate
line of work develops robot-specific, action-conditioned
world models. DreamDojo [13], for example, learns interac-
tion dynamics from large-scale human videos and is subse-
quently adapted to generate future visual states conditioned
on robot actions.
Another line of work builds general-purpose generative
video models, such as Stable Video Diffusion [9], Wan [35],
and the Cosmos-Predict family [26, 28, 29]. These
diffusion/flow-based models directly generate temporally
evolving visual sequences and have increasingly been re-
purposed as pretrained backbones for representation-based
policies and WAMs [16, 20, 23, 45]. Unlike latent predic-
tive or robot-specific world models, our work focuses on
this class of pretrained generative video models and ex-
amines whether their generated dynamics preserve physical
consistency. Specifically, we evaluate Wan2.1, whose pre-
trained video backbone is adopted by DreamZero [45], and
Cosmos-Predict2, which is adapted for visuomotor control
and planning in Cosmos Policy [20].
2.3. Physical Understanding and Probing in Video
Models
A broad range of benchmarks have been proposed to evalu-
ate physical understanding in videos, including input-video
benchmarks for intuitive physics and plausibility [11, 21,
32, 43] and evaluators for physical plausibility and motion
consistency in generated videos [2, 7, 25, 48]. Among them,
IntPhys2 [11] evaluates four intuitive-physics principles—
permanence, immutability, spatio-temporal continuity, and
solidity—through video-level discrimination between phys-
ically possible and impossible events. However, it does not
provide temporal annotations indicating when a physical vi-
olation occurs. To evaluate temporal localization, we intro-
duce IntPhys2-Timing, which augments impossible videos
with temporal annotations of their physical violations.

Existing approaches either probe physics-related infor-
mation from internal model representations or evaluate
physical plausibility at the level of an entire video or
video pair. Methods such as TRAJAN [2] temporally lo-
calize motion inconsistency, while predictive violation-of-
expectation (VoE) approaches [11, 14, 24] measure time-
resolved surprise from discrepancies between predicted and
observed representations. However, such surprise signals
primarily quantify model-dependent prediction error and
do not explicitly identify which physical expectation es-
tablished by prior observations conflicts with the current
observation. PhysTrace instead externalizes the history-
conditioned expected state and directly aligns it with the
current video observation, enabling temporally localized
and interpretable physical diagnostics.
3. PhysTrace: Temporal Physical Consistency
Tracing
In this section, we introduce PhysTrace, which traces
changes in physical consistency over time by repeatedly
measuring the alignment between expected video dynamics
and actual observations. We also describe IntPhys2-Timing
and the evaluation metrics used to quantify temporal local-
ization performance. The overall framework is illustrated in
Fig. 2.
3.1. Temporal Prediction–Observation Alignment
Given a video V , we divide it into temporal windows of
fixed duration Tw and analyze them with a hop size of ∆t.
For each window Vi, a fixed number of frames are sampled
and provided to an MLLM. All temporal windows use the
same instructions and structured output format.
At each window, the MLLM generates two textual de-
scriptions. The current state si describes the objects and dy-
namics directly observed in Vi, without using history as vi-
sual evidence. In contrast, the prediction pi describes the
state that should be observed in the current window based
on the history Hi−1 accumulated from preceding windows.
Predictive VoE formulations typically quantify surprise by
comparing a predicted future representation with its sub-
sequent observation [11, 14, 24]. Such future-directed pre-
diction can shift the detected discrepancy according to the
prediction horizon. PhysTrace instead generates the history-
conditioned expected state pi for the current window and
compares it directly with the same Vi. The conditions gov-
erning these outputs are detailed in Appendix C.

State si and Prediction pi descriptions, we manually
annotate reference descriptions for the original IntPhys2
videos [11] and evaluate the outputs of several MLLMs

3.2. History-Conditioned Prediction and Selective
State Retention
Physical violations can occur at different temporal scales.
Short temporal windows are effective for capturing brief,
localized changes, whereas events unfolding over longer
durations require broader temporal context. In particular,
some violations can only be identified by relating multiple
brief events separated in time. Simply increasing the win-
dow size, however, may obscure these short events and re-
duce temporal localization precision. Therefore, PhysTrace
maintains local temporal windows while selectively retain-
ing salient observations from preceding windows in the his-
tory Hi−1.
In addition to State and Prediction described in
Sec. 3.1, the MLLM produces a Trusted output for each
window. The Trusted indicator ti ∈{0, 1} determines
whether the currently observed state si is retained in the
history for subsequent predictions. Retaining every obser-
vation would accumulate repetitive or uninformative infor-
mation, potentially diluting salient events. Therefore, ti = 1
is assigned only when a new object appears, new motion be-
gins, or a meaningful state change occurs that is not already
represented in the history. The history is updated as follows:

When a physical violation occurs, the alignment between
the history-conditioned prediction pi and the actual obser-
vation si decreases in the corresponding window. If the
newly observed state si is retained in the history after a
physical violation, subsequent predictions are conditioned
on the updated history. Consequently, the alignment trace
can emphasize the transition at which the observed dynam-
ics first deviate from the expected physical behavior, rather
than remaining low throughout subsequent windows. Addi-
tional qualitative examples are provided in Appendix B.
3.3. Temporal Evaluation with IntPhys2-Timing
The original IntPhys2 provides only video-level annotations
indicating whether each video is physically possible or im-
possible, without specifying the temporal location at which
a physical violation occurs. To evaluate the temporal local-
ization performance of PhysTrace, we construct IntPhys2-
Timing2 by augmenting the impossible videos in IntPhys2
with temporal violation annotations. Of the original 506
video pairs, we exclude seven invalid pairs in which the pos-
sible and impossible videos are identical, leaving 499 valid
pairs before category selection. We further retain eight of
the 16 categories, excluding those with ambiguous object
identities, substantial occlusion, cluttered scenes, or atypi-
cal structures that make temporal violation annotation dif-
ficult. For each impossible video, we use its correspond-
ing possible video as a reference to identify the interval in
which their physical behaviors begin to diverge. We then
manually refine the temporal boundaries to annotate the in-
terval in which the physical violation actually occurs.
IntPhys2-Timing employs two evaluation metrics: Min-
imum Alignment Score (MAS) for measuring video-level
physical inconsistency and Hit Rate for temporal localiza-
tion. MAS is defined as the lowest alignment score in a
video’s temporal alignment trace, representing the largest
measured mismatch between the history-conditioned ex-

4.2. Model-Specific Evaluation Setup
To apply PhysTrace to pretrained diffusion/flow-based
video generative models, we account for their model-
specific input configurations and noise schedules. Wan2.1
and Cosmos-Predict2 process 81 and 93 frames, respec-
tively, at a nominal rate of 16 fps, whereas the original Int-
Phys2 [11] videos consist of 636 frames (60 fps, approxi-
mately 10.6 s) at a resolution of 512 × 512. Rather than re-
constructing the original video recursively in separate seg-
ments, we uniformly sample frames across its full duration
to match each model’s input length and reconstruct the en-
tire sequence in a single pass. Specifically, we sample 81
frames for Wan2.1 and 93 frames for Cosmos-Predict2 from
the original 636 frames. The reconstructed videos are saved
at 7.5 fps and approximately 8.8 fps, respectively, to pre-
serve the original duration of approximately 10.6 s, with
all frames resized to 256 × 256. For PhysTrace evaluation,
we provide all frames within each temporal window (2 s,
hop 1 s), without additional frame subsampling, to both the
MLLM and the alignment scoring model. This corresponds
to 15 frames per window for Wan2.1 and approximately 18
frames for Cosmos-Predict2. Finally, to avoid the influence
of scene descriptions on the reconstructed outputs, we set

the video models’ text-conditioning prompts to an empty
string in all generative-trajectory experiments.
5. Experiments
For video–text alignment scoring, we use PE-AV-Large [37]
(see Appendix A). Among the open-source MLLMs [4, 27],
we adopt Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking [4], with a maximum
generation length of 8,000 tokens and a reasoning budget of
3,000 tokens. Unless otherwise specified, PhysTrace uses a
temporal window of Tw = 2s and a hop size of ∆t = 1s.
To generate history-conditioned expected states, the MLLM
prompts follow a consistent format across the four intuitive-
physics principles, with principle-specific instructions. All
experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.
5.1. Temporal Localization of Physical Violations
on IntPhys2-Timing
To evaluate temporal localization on IntPhys2-Timing, we
compare PhysTrace with a VoE-inspired baseline [11, 14,
24] implemented using V-JEPA2 [3]. For PhysTrace, we
uniformly sample 60 frames from each temporal window
of the original IntPhys2 videos and use the same sampled
frames for both the MLLM and alignment scoring model.
The VoE baseline computes time-resolved surprise from V-
JEPA2 representations and identifies the interval with the
highest surprise as the predicted violation location. For both
methods, a prediction is counted as a hit if the predicted
interval overlaps the annotated violation interval, allowing
temporal localization performance to be compared using the
same Hit Rate criterion. Table 2 presents the resulting Hit
Rates across the four intuitive-physics principles.
PhysTrace achieves its highest Hit Rate on immutabil-
ity, which involves changes in intrinsic object properties
such as color and shape. Compared with the VoE base-
line, PhysTrace more accurately localizes physical viola-
tions across three of the four physical principles. Further-
more, unlike the VoE baseline, PhysTrace provides inter-
pretable State and Prediction descriptions, allowing
us to identify which history-conditioned expectations con-
flict with actual observations (see Appendix B).

5.3. Possible-vs-Impossible Classification Accuracy
on IntPhys2
Table 3 evaluates possible–impossible discrimination on
IntPhys2 [11]. MLLM baselines directly classify each video
as physically possible or impossible, while predictive mod-
els such as V-JEPA2 [3] compare prediction-based surprise
between paired videos. In contrast, PhysTrace compares the
MAS of each possible–impossible pair, counting a predic-
tion as correct when the impossible video has a lower MAS
than its possible counterpart.
PhysTrace achieves the highest overall accuracy of
59.9%, outperforming the compared methods on the
Medium and Hard subsets. While Pixels to Principles [6]
reports near-chance performance of MLLMs in challenging
intuitive-physics settings, our results suggest that tracing
history-conditioned expectation–observation discrepancies
provides a useful signal for discriminating physical consis-
tency without direct MLLM judgments of physical validity.
