Benchmarking Learnable Representations for Gigapixel Image Compression and
Super-Resolution
Matteo Fusconi1, Alex Costanzino1, Pierluigi Zama Ramirez2, Luigi Di Stefano1
1 CVLab, University of Bologna
2 Ca’ Foscari University of Venice
Abstract
Learnable Representations (LRs) can fit parametric fami-
lies of functions to diverse kinds of signals. These emer-
gent neural-native data formats are gathering interest in the
scientific community due to their compactness, resolution-
agnosticism and amenability to direct neural processing. On
the other hand, gigapixel images are becoming increasingly
widespread across a variety of scientific, cultural, and in-
dustrial domains. In this paper, we present GIGABENCH,
the first benchmark and experimental evaluation focused
on deploying LRs with gigapixel imagery. GIGABENCH
includes images collected, acquired, and synthetically ren-
dered across diverse domains, and addresses image com-
pression and super-resolution. We adapt a representative
set of LRs, spanning Implicit, Hybrid, and Explicit methods,
so as to operate reliably at the gigapixel scale on consumer
hardware. Through systematic evaluation across reconstruc-
tion quality, model size, compression and inference times,
we identify consistent trends across methods and suggest
actionable guidance for future investigation. The project
page is available here.
1. Introduction
The emergence of Learnable Representations (LRs) has
opened up a different paradigm for image storage, recon-
struction and processing. Rather than representing an image
as a discrete grid of pixel values, LRs enable encoding it
as a continuous function, defined by a set of optimizable
parameters, that maps spatial coordinates to color values.
Prominent variants of LRs include Implicit Representations
[12, 30, 45], which encode an image in the weights of an
MLP; Hybrid Representations [10, 36, 48], which addition-
ally incorporate spatially localized learnable features; and
Explicit Representations [28, 55], which represent images
using Gaussian primitives. LRs have attracted significant
attention for their compactness, resolution-agnosticism and
amenability to direct neural processing. As a matter of fact,
several recent works [3, 15, 20, 52] have shown how the
learned parameters can be fed into specifically designed
meta-networks to perform downstream tasks like classifica-
tion and retrieval, without requiring reconstruction of the
original discrete signal. Such property positions LRs as a
pathway to a new generation of neural-native data formats.
On the other hand, digital imaging is undergoing a quiet
resolution revolution, as gigapixel images are becoming in-
creasingly common across a variety of scientific, cultural,
and industrial domains, from whole-slide pathology imag-
ing [7, 14] and satellite remote sensing [11] to high-fidelity
museum digitisation and panoramic photography [24]. Yet,
the paradigms and tools for handling images at this scale
remain largely anchored to classical pipelines, designed in
an era when such resolutions were inconceivable. Hence, we
pose a timely research question concerning whether and how
LRs can be effectively deployed for gigapixel-scale images,
and which of their variants show the greatest promise for
enabling adoption within this increasingly relevant regime.
However, despite the favourable properties of LRs and the
increasing adoption of gigapixel imagery, the current liter-
ature lacks a comprehensive and rigorous evaluation of LR
methods on gigapixel images. Indeed, the reported experi-
mental findings primarily concern datasets with much lower
and more conventional resolutions, typically ranging from
less than one megapixel to a few megapixels, where the com-
putational cost of fitting LRs remains easily tractable. The
gigapixel regime, however, introduces fundamentally differ-
ent challenges, as fitting a single image may require hours
of training, naive partitioning strategies introduce boundary
artifacts, and memory constraints force architectural com-
promises that do not arise at standard resolutions. Adapting
existing LRs to this setting is therefore a non-trivial task.
In this paper, we introduce GIGABENCH, a benchmark
specifically designed to address the aforementioned scien-
tific questions and foster research into the design of LR ap-
proaches suitable for gigapixel imagery. We curate a collec-
tion of gigapixel images across diverse domains and evaluate
LRs on compression and super-resolution under controlled
settings. Our evaluation spans the three main families of ap-

proaches: Implicit, Hybrid and Explicit Gaussian-based Rep-
resentations. Our analysis reveals several consistent trends
across the three families. Hybrid representations achieve the
highest reconstruction fidelity but are structurally limited to
moderate compression ratios, as their grid-based components
impose a memory floor that prevents compression at extreme
rates. On the other hand, they neatly outperform other LR
approaches when it comes to super-resolution, although a hu-
man evaluation suggests that the merits of LR at the gigapixel
scales are somewhat uncertain compared to classical method-
ologies, such as bicubic interpolation. Implicit representa-
tions, by contrast, can provide high to extreme compression
ratios, trading fidelity for compression. Explicit represen-
tations exhibit the most variable intra-family behaviours in
both compression and super-resolution, with performance
depending heavily on the chosen parametrisation. Across
all families, no single approach dominates uniformly as the
optimal choice depends on the target compression or super-
resolution ratio, as well as on the quality-speed trade-off.
Our contributions can be summarised as follows: (i) we
introduce a curated benchmark of gigapixel images spanning
10 scenes and different domains, released publicly to foster
future research; (ii) we provide a systematic comparison of
representative Implicit, Hybrid, and Explicit representations
to gigapixel image compression and super-resolution; (iii)
we characterise the practical trade-offs among the specific
methods evaluated across fidelity, speed, and downstream
usability, offering concrete guidance for researchers and
practitioners working in this emerging regime.
2. Related Work
Learnable Representations are a continuous signal represen-
tation paradigm in which a function parametrized by a set
of weights is optimized to map coordinates to the values of
a target signal. This abstraction is broad enough to cover
image [9], video [8], and audio reconstruction [45], neu-
ral radiance fields [35], SDF fitting [38], inverse problem
solving, and more. In this work, we focus on images. Their
main advantages include resolution agnosticism, a parameter
count decoupled from sampling density, and full differen-
tiability of the input domain. We divide LRs into three
main categories, described in the following sections: Im-
plicit Representations (IRs), Hybrid Representations (HRs),
and Explicit Representations (ERs).
Implicit Representations. Implicit representations [51]
parametrize the function as a Multi-Layer Perceptron. Their
principal drawback is the spectral bias of MLP-based ap-
proximators, which favors low frequencies and impedes
fitting fine-grained signal content [40]. Following the tax-
onomy of [17], IRs differ along two axes: coordinate en-
coding and activation design, and network architecture. On
the first, random Fourier feature mappings lift input coor-
dinates into a high-dimensional sinusoidal basis before a
standard MLP [46], while a complementary line replaces
ReLU with intrinsically high-frequency activations: peri-
odic sinusoids [45], complex Gabor wavelets [44], Gaus-
sians [41], variable-period sinusoids [30], and oscillator-
based designs [12]. More recent methods select activations
adaptively from a learned dictionary [23]. On the second,
the inductive bias is moved into the architecture: multiplica-
tive filter networks [18] replace additive layer composition
with element-wise products of Fourier and Gabor filters,
BACON [29] adds explicit per-layer bandwidth limits for
multi-scale representation, and INCODE [25] couples the
MLP with a conditioning sub-network that adapts activation
parameters to the input. Beyond signal fitting, IRs are in-
creasingly used as a data representation in their own right,
with the network weights as the object of downstream learn-
ing. Functa [15] treats each datum as a function, learning a
shared prior via per-sample modulations of a meta-learned
backbone for classification and generation in function space.
[13] and [52] instead embed the weights of independently
fitted IRs into a compact latent space, enabling retrieval,
segmentation, and generation without reconstructing the sig-
nal. Recently, [20] fits IRs end-to-end with direct classifier
feedback, addressing the structural inconsistency of weights
optimized without downstream supervision.
Hybrid Representations. As the target signal grows in size
and bandwidth, the spectral bias of pure IRs becomes increas-
ingly difficult to overcome through activation and encoding
design alone. This motivates hybrid representations, which
pair a lightweight MLP decoder with a learnable explicit
structure, such as grids, hash tables, or basis functions, that
injects spatial diversity and localizes capacity where the sig-
nal demands it. ACORN [34] adaptively partitions the input
domain into blocks, each assigned a small MLP, refining the
partition to match local signal complexity. MINER [43] rep-
resents the signal through a Laplacian pyramid of per-block
coordinate networks, yielding a coarse-to-fine multi-scale
decomposition. Instant-NGP [36] couples a compact MLP
with a multi-resolution hash grid of trainable feature vectors,
achieving orders-of-magnitude speed-ups at the cost of hash
collisions. NeuRBF [10] generalizes the regular-grid prior
to adaptive radial basis functions whose centers and shapes
follow the signal, further composed with multi-frequency
sinusoids to recover fine details. MetricGrids [48] extends
the grid paradigm with elementary grids defined in distinct
metric spaces, acting as higher-order Taylor terms that ap-
proximate strongly non-linear signals more faithfully than
linear interpolation alone.
Explicit
Representations.
Explicit
representations
parametrize a signal as a collection of graphic primitives
placed directly in the signal domain, with their location
and shape exposed as optimization variables. They have
been investigated under a variety of primitives, including
Gabor wavelets [50], triangles [21, 22], and anisotropic


Gaussians [26]. Among these, Gaussians adapt most nat-
urally to image fitting: GaussianImage [55] reduces 3D
Gaussian Splattings [26] to eight parameters per primitive
and a tile-based rasterizer operating at 103 FPS. Subse-
quent work scales the paradigm to ultra-high-resolution in-
puts via feature-aware sampling [28] and multi-scale seeded
parametrizations [37], with further variants refining initial-
ization and density control [53].
Positioning. Despite the breadth and maturity of these meth-
ods, existing evaluations most commonly rely on Kodak [27]
and DIV2K [1], datasets originally designed for classical
compression benchmarking rather than for assessing neural
representations. Yet even these are not used consistently,
as different works evaluate on different subsets, at different
resolutions, under different compression ratio definitions,
and with different metrics, making cross-paper comparisons
unreliable even when the data is the same. No prior work
has systematically evaluated these methods at a gigapixel
scale, a regime that introduces qualitatively distinct chal-
lenges in fitting cost, memory, and rate-distortion behaviour.
GIGABENCH addresses that gap.
3. Benchmark
3.1. Data Collection
GIGABENCH comprises 10 images, curated from publicly
available sources according to four design principles:
• Gigapixel Resolution. Each image meets or marginally
exceeds the one-gigapixel threshold (in practice, 1.0–1.19
GPx). We enforce this floor so the benchmark deliberately
clears the symbolic gigapixel mark, and caps it both for
uniformity across the set and to keep fitting feasible on
consumer hardware;
• Domain Diversity. The images span panoramas, industrial
scans, scientific histopathology slides, satellite imagery at
varying scales, and synthetic images. This range addresses
several domains where gigapixel images are becoming
increasingly relevant;
• Lossless Appearance. Many gigapixel images distributed
online are stored as JPEG files and thus carry compression
artifacts. We exclude these images: since each represen-
tation method introduces artifacts of its own, pre-existing
ones would add up and be difficult to disentangle from
reconstruction errors;
• Licensing. We retain only images whose original license
permits research use and redistribution.
The dataset is released as a bundle under the Creative Com-
mons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 license, with attri-
bution to this work, plus the individual attributions to the
original authors of the images. Fig. 1 depicts the images com-
prising our benchmark, while Tab. 1 reports their metadata,
with more details provided in the Supplementary.

3.2. Methods Selection
We evaluate compression and super-resolution performance
across several LRs drawn from Implicit, Hybrid and Ex-
plicit Representations. As for super-resolution, we also
compare LRs to classical interpolation methods, such as
nearest-neighbour, bicubic, and Lanczos. We select, within
each family, at least one seminal method alongside one rep-
resenting the current state of the art. Moreover, the methods
are used in different configurations, spanning three orders of
magnitude of compression ratio (101, 102, 103). Within the
family of Implicit Representations, we choose SIREN [45]
as the seminal model, FINER [30] as a major advancement,
especially in fitting sharper details, and FDHO [12] as a
very recent improvement, yet it was tested at a very small
scale for Image Compression. All three models come in two
architectures, as defined by the number of hidden layers and
hidden features, and denoted by the pair (L, F): a (5, 256)
architecture, as proposed in [45], along with a (8, 1024) vari-
ant, matching respectively the ∼100 and ∼1000 scales
(skipping ∼10 as it would have been computationally un-
feasible). As for Hybrid Representations, we consider the
seminal Instant-NGP [36], and the two main follow-ups
NeuRBF [10] and MetricGrids [48], each at two hash-table
sizes, 224 and 222, denoted as (T = 24) and (T = 22), re-
spectively. The first is the value recommended by [36, 48]
for gigapixel fitting, while the second is recommended to fit
images in general. These configurations address the ∼10
scale, landing at ∼5 and ∼15 respectively. Concerning
Explicit Representations, we choose the seminal Gaussian-
Image [55] method and the more recent SmartSplat [28], as
it has been trained and evaluated at the highest resolution
(DIV16K dataset), while also being robust enough to con-
verge at very high compression ratios. Given the possibility
to choose the number of Gaussians, we could select config-
urations that match 15, 100, and 3000 compression ratios.
However, as the forward step of Gaussian splatting scales
linearly with the number of pixels, it is unfeasible to com-
pute it for gigapixel images on a consumer GPU. For this
reason, we split the image into tiles and leverage gradient
accumulation to reduce the memory footprint while main-
taining the same theoretical accuracy. Additional details in
Sec. I and Algorithm 1 of the Supplementary.

3.3. Metrics
Compression. We evaluate reconstruction quality via Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity In-
dex (SSIM, reported in the Supplementary) [49], which to-
gether capture both pixel-level fidelity and perceptual struc-
ture preservation. Compression efficiency is measured by
the Compression Ratio (CR), defined as the ratio between
the size in bytes of the uncompressed image and that of the
stored Learned Representation:

To characterize computational cost, we report Compression
Time (CT), the time required to fit the representation to the
given image, and Inference Time (IT), the time required to
decode the full image from the fitted representation. CT is
particularly critical in the gigapixel regime, where it reaches
several hours, and IT affects the practical adoption of the rep-
resentation whenever the image must be fully reconstructed
for downstream use. While PSNR and CR are intrinsic to the
representation and thus directly comparable across methods
regardless of codebase, CT and IT are runtime measurements
of each method’s reference implementation.
Super-Resolution. In the LRs setting, Super-Resolution
consists of querying the fitted model on a coordinate grid
finer than the one used during training, with the expecta-
tion that the reconstructed image preserves edges and fine-
grained patterns more faithfully than classical interpolation
methods. To address this standard setting, we downsam-
ple the GIGABENCH images by ×4 and ×8, then fit LRs
on the downsampled versions and assess the reconstruction
quality of images sampled on a finer grid relative to the
corresponding original. Yet, it has been observed that the
popular distortion metrics used to evaluate reconstruction
methods, like PSNR and SSIM, may correlate poorly with
human perceptual judgement [5]. This is particularly the
case in super-resolution, where perceptually relevant cues
relate to sharpness, texture fidelity, and artifact structure
rather than pixel-level distortion. Therefore, to complement
our experimental assessment of LRs for super-resolution, we
designed and carried out a human evaluation study. Partici-
pants were recruited through an open call, with the study link
publicly released and further spread by word of mouth. Each
was shown the outputs of four methods: the best-performing
model from each of the Implicit, Hybrid, and Explicit LR
families, plus the best classical algorithm, and asked to rank
them by perceived quality, i.e., sharpness of detail, absence
of artifacts, and overall visual fidelity. Stimuli were 20482
crops, manually selected to be visually engaging and to span
a diverse spectrum of low- and high-frequency content. All
