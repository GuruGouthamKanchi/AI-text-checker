Abstract
Video object removal and injection are reciprocal editing oper-
ations between object-present and object-absent video states,
yet they differ substantially in training difficulty. Removal
can be effectively bootstrapped from video inpainting and
further adapted to erase object-related interaction effects. In-
jection, however, requires large-scale paired supervision to
model object appearance, dynamics, and scene-dependent ef-
fects, while perfectly aligned real-world pairs are difficult to
collect and synthetic data lacks sufficient realism and diversity.
To address this challenge, we present UniVOE, a removal-
driven framework for training both video object removal and

Introduction
Video object removal and injection are critical in visual con-
tent creation, with broad applications in film post-production,
advertising, and virtual production. Unlike image editing,
video editing must account for not only the target object it-
self, but also its dynamic imprint on surrounding scene. A
moving object may cast shadows, produce reflections, and
contact surfaces. Editing only the object but leaving these
interaction effects untreated during removal or synthesizing
them incorrectly in injection will produce perceptible arti-
facts. A practical tool should jointly edit the object and its
effects.
Recent diffusion-based video editing methods made sig-
nificant progress in fidelity and temporal consistency (Yang
et al. 2026c; Li et al. 2025a; Zhou et al. 2023; Kushwaha et al.
2026; Yang et al. 2026a). Some works further recognize the
close relationship between object removal and insertion (Fu
et al. 2026): effect-aware removers erase shadows and re-
flections together with the target object, while reciprocal
formulations use insertion as an auxiliary inverse task to im-
prove removal. General video inpainting systems also support
adding or removing objects (Bian et al. 2025). Nevertheless,
realistic object injection remains difficult. It must halluci-
nate absent foreground, infer plausible object motion, and
predict interactive effects such as shadows and reflections.
The main bottleneck is supervision. Ideally, both directions
should be trained with paired videos of the same scene in
object present and absent states under identical lighting, and
background dynamics. Such pairs are hard to capture in the
wild, while synthetic rendering, despite perfect alignment,
lacks diversity, photorealism, and dynamics of real footage.
Our key insight is that object removal and injection share
the same paired data structure, but are asymmetric in learn-
ing difficulty. Let Vobj and Vabs denote the object present
and absent states of the same video. The two tasks can
be viewed as conditional generation in opposite directions:
πrem(Vabs | Vobj, c) ⇐⇒πinj(Vobj | Vabs, c), where c denotes
the editing condition, such as text prompt in Fig. 1. The same
pair can supervise both directions: removal learns to elimi-
nate the object and its effects, while injection learns to predict
them. However, removal can be scaled from video inpaint-
ing, which provides abundant supervision for background
completion, temporal structure, and scene consistency. With
limited paired Vobj-Vabs data, this prior can be adapted to
complete object removal and effect erasing. Injection, in con-
trast, requires cleaner supervision for object appearance, mo-
tion, and interaction. This reciprocal-but-asymmetric struc-
ture motivates removal-driven strategy: strengthen easier re-
moval direction, and use it to derive supervision for injection.
We propose UniVOE, a removal-driven framework for re-
alistic video object removal and injection. As shown in Fig. 2,
UniVOE first trains an effect-aware remover by transferring
scalable inpainting priors to object removal and refines with
limited object-present/absent pairs (Miao et al. 2026; Fu et al.
2026). The trained remover is then applied to real object-
present videos to derive object-absent counterparts, while
the original videos serve as injection targets containing real
object motion and interaction effects. These removal-derived
pairs, together with limited open-source data, supervise an

However, pixel-wise supervision alone cannot fully close the
perceptual gap of injection: for an object-absent video, mul-
tiple appearances, trajectories, and contact patterns may be
plausible, while failures such as over-saturation, unnatural
motion, missing contact shadows, or unrealistic reflections
are better captured by perceptual judgment than by recon-
struction error. We therefore introduce an online post-training
stage tailored to object injection. It employs a proprietary re-
ward model, finetuned from Qwen3-VL (Bai et al. 2025),
to rank the motion quality and naturalness of edited videos,
steering optimization toward model’s performance ceiling.
More importantly, as an editing task, the complete frame
sequence should be fully covered during ranking and train-
ing. To make online post-training feasible for large diffusion
models at realistic video resolutions and durations, we de-
sign a VRAM-efficient Group-Relative Advantage Regres-
sion (GRAR). It avoids the memory overhead of maintaining
a reference policy while preserving reward-guided training.
Experiments demonstrate that UniVOE achieves SOTA em-
pirical results on both video object removal and injection.
As shown in Fig. 1, for removal, it robustly eliminates target
objects together with their shadows, reflections, and con-
tact cues. For injection, it synthesizes objects with plausible
motion and interaction effects. Compared with strong open-
source and commercial systems, UniVOE is competitive and
clearly stronger in challenging interaction-effect cases that
prior methods often mishandle. Our contributions are sum-
marized as follows:
• We propose an effective training framework to optimize
video object removal and injection models with limited
real-world paired data, named UniVOE. We exploit their
reciprocal-but-asymmetric training structure: removal is
first scaled with easier inpainting task and then reused as
a supervision engine for the harder injection direction.
• To enhance the realism of injection, we develop a novel
post-training algorithm, called GRAR, to overcome the
limitation of pixel-level reconstruction and align the in-
jected results with human preference. Experiments verify
its effectiveness in improving injection quality.
• We apply various techniques to removal model for large-
scale training data curation. First, object deletion is boot-
strapped with video inpainting. Next, we adapt the model
with paired data to enable interactive effects removal.

• Extensive comparisons with open-source and commercial
systems show that UniVOE achieves SOTA performance
on both object removal and injection, especially in chal-
lenging interaction-effect erasing and prediction.
Related Work
Video Object Removal
Video object removal is a special case of video inpainting: the
masked region must be filled with plausible and temporally
coherent background. Early methods rely on temporal adver-
sarial losses, optical-flow propagation, or spatio-temporal
attention to improve completion quality (Chang et al. 2019;
Zeng, Fu, and Chao 2020; Liu et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022; Zhou
et al. 2023; Zhang, Rao, and Agrawala 2023; Gu et al. 2025;
Gao et al. 2025), recent diffusion-based pipelines extend the
generative range to large holes (Li et al. 2025a; Bian et al.
2025). These generic inpainters fill the masked region but are
not designed to clean up object-induced effects outside the
mask. A second line of work, namely effect-aware removal,
targets the shadows, reflections, mirror images, and deforma-
tions that an object leaves on the surrounding scene (Miao
et al. 2026; Kushwaha et al. 2026). EffectErase (Fu et al.
2026) treats object insertion as an auxiliary inverse task to
regularize removal. In contrast, we treat removal as a super-
vision engine for injection: a trained effect-aware remover
converts object-present videos into object-absent counter-
parts for paired supervision of object injection.
Video Object Injection
Object injection places a target object into a host scene
while keeping it visually compatible with background. At im-
age level, prior work has explored exemplar-guided editing,
object compositing, and zero-shot object-level customiza-
tion (Yang et al. 2023; Song et al. 2023; Lu, Liu, and Kong
2023; Chen et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025b). Lifting these to video
is harder: the inserted object must remain temporally consis-
tent and move coherently. Recent video editing methods add
controllability through first-frame editing, reference-object
preservation, or compositing (Ku et al. 2024; Mou et al.
2024; Wei et al. 2024; Tu et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2026b;
Ye et al. 2025), but they typically expect the user to specify
object identity, motion track, and other injection-related con-
trols up front. Unlike these methods, UniVOE offers lighter
interface (text by default, with an optional reference image).
This enables flexible use and large-scale paired data curation.
Post-training for Preference Alignment
Aligning generation results with human perception gener-
ally takes one of two routes. Offline preference optimi-
sation (Rafailov et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023; Prabhudesai
et al. 2024; Wallace et al. 2024) fine-tunes the model on
a fixed set of pairwise preferences. It is simple and stable,
but bound to expensive human annotation, and the model
never trains on its own samples. Online post-training instead
scores samples drawn from the current policy on the fly,
which saves manual annotation and allows the model to ex-
plore its higher performance boundaries. Within online for
diffusion and flow-matching, recent work explores PPO-style
trust regions (Liu et al. 2026) and negative-aware fine-tuning,
exemplified by DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al. 2025). Although
these are effective on image models, applying them off-the-
shelf to large video backbones for editing tasks runs into
a memory wall: keeping a frozen reference policy along-
side a trainable one, plus storing rollouts of long video la-
tents, needs larger VRAM. Therefore, we propose a VRAM-
efficient post-training strategy tailored to video editing. It fol-
lows a single-pass reward-weighted regression but discards
the reference policy, named GRAR.
Method
UniVOE contains two models, an effect-aware remover πrem
and an injector πinj, which share the same structure but are
trained for opposite directions of object present/absent trans-
formation. Both are built on Wan2.1-14B (Wan et al. 2025).

Stage 1: Effect-Aware Video Object Removal
As Fig. 2 shows, video inpainting and object removal share
a common goal: recovering background occluded by tar-
get region. This similarity allows us to bootstrap object re-
moval from inpainting data. Further, removal requires erasing
object-induced interaction effects, e.g., shadows and reflec-
tions that extend beyond object. We train remover in two
steps: inpainting pretraining and effect-aware fine-tuning.
Video inpainting.
We employ SAM2 (Ravi et al. 2025)
to segment foreground objects in real-world videos, obtain
their masks M. We collect over 63K high-quality videos
containing salient objects and apply Gaussian dilation to M
to ensure sufficient coverage of object boundaries. In train-
ing, we randomly sample two videos, V idA and V idB, and
use M from V idB to mask corresponding regions in V idA,
producing realistic object-shaped missing regions with tem-
poral variations. The masked V idA is used as input, while
the original V idA serves as the ground truth. The removal
model is optimized with the reconstruction loss in Eq. (2),
enabling it to learn a strong scene completion prior for plau-
sibly recovering background content occluded by objects.

Effect-aware fine-tuning.
An inpainting model fills con-
tent inside the masked region, while object removal requires
eliminating object-induced effects, such as shadows, reflec-
tions, or other interactions that may extend beyond the object
mask. To make the remover effect-aware, we further fine-tune
it on synthesized and captured object-present/object-absent
video pairs collected from prior datasets (Miao et al. 2026;
Fu et al. 2026), yielding 68K filtered training pairs. During
training, we use inflated object mask M that fully covers
the target object but leaves interaction effects outside. This
encourages the model to recover the complete object-absent
scene rather than merely inpainting the object region, while
improving robustness to inaccurate masks at inference time.

Vobj and Vabs denote the object-present and object-absent
videos, respectively. Removal condition crem supports both

As shown in Fig. 3(b), the reference-guided mode pro-

Specifically, we edit the first frame of Vobj using the same re-

When Iref is provided, UniVOE prepends it to Vobj as an
additional conditioning frame. Correspondingly, one noise
frame is prepended to the latent noise sequence and one all-
zero mask frame is prepended to the mask sequence, so that
the video, noise, and mask remain temporally aligned. The
reference frame provides an explicit but static object-absent
prior, guiding the model to remove the dynamic object con-
sistently with the edited first frame throughout the video.
Inference Acceleration.
When applying remover to large-
scale data curation, its inference cost directly limits the scale
of paired data we can generate. To accelerate it, we distill re-
mover from the default 50-steps sampling into 12-steps. Con-
cretely, we shorten the flow-matching sampler to 12-steps
with a fixed sigma shift of 5.0, and finetune with Eq. (2). To
synchronize timesteps of the backbone and branch, we add
a LoRA (Hu et al. 2022) of rank 32 to attention and feed-
forward layers of backbone, jointly update the full branch to-
gether with LoRA. In inference, we directly remove objects
in 12-steps, making large-scale object-absent video genera-
tion practical. Note that our removal results in this paper are
all generated from distilled 12-steps model, which yields a
substantial speed-up with imperceptible quality degradation.

reflections, and incoherent motion are perceptually critical.

Advantage regression.
Post-training methods mainly re-
quire double models, a reference policy, a trainable policy,
with multiple forward passes. This is because, limited by
reward hacking, updates driven solely by rewards cause the
model to overfit or collapse into a spurious optimum, far
removed from the high-quality distribution learned in pre-
training. Hence, reference policy is necessary to constrain
post-training scope, but inevitably increases VRAM over-
head, limiting its potential for use in larger-scale models.
Existing methods are mainly applied to image models or
small video backbones such as 1.3B (Pan et al. 2025). But
as to stronger video model such as our injector with 23B
parameters, using off-the-shelf solutions is infeasible. One
workaround is to reduce VRAM usage by sampling partial
frames for training. However, for video editing tasks, abnor-
mal editing effects in any frame are intolerable, i.e., training
with full frame sequence is essential for editing. In our case,
using 97 frames of 480p leads to unacceptably VRAM load.
To this end, we propose Group-Relative Advantage Re-
gression (GRAR), a single-policy surrogate that preserves
preference-aligned optimization without a separate reference
model. We rethink the role of reference policy from the nature
of video editing. Reference policy constrains the magnitude
of post-training for generation. However, for editing, input
video itself constrains the output content, i.e., the model does
not generate a new video from scratch but merely modifies
parts, with the core content directly dictated by input video.
Therefore, GRAR only regress single trainable policy with
reward values without redundant reference policy.

Experiments
Implementation Details
Both remover and injector share the same model structure,
each contains 23B parameters, including a 14B frozen Trans-
former backbone, a 3B trainable control branch, and other
modules such as VAE, etc. To support practical video edit-
ing lasting over nine seconds (i.e., more than 200 frames),
we train with 97 frames of video clip with resolution of
832×480. Learning rate for remover and injector pre-training
is 1e-5, and injector post-training is 1e-6. During inference,
both remover and injector support 2 conditioning paradigms:
text-only or text-and-reference image. As some compared
methods require reference image to inject objects. For fair
comparison, here we compare removal with text-only input
and compare injection with text-and-reference input.
