Major Revision, Minor Accountability: LLMs as Reviewers of
Published Articles in Top-Tier Journals
Bastien Le Guellec1,*, Su-Hwan Kim2, Raphael Bentegeac1 and Aghiles Hamroun1
1CHU Lille, Lille, France
2Technical University of Munich (TUM), Munich, Germany
*Corresponding author: bastien.leguellec@chu-lille.fr
Abstract
With the growth of scientific output, the peer review system is approaching a breaking point:
reviewers are increasingly hard to secure, and turnaround times lengthen accordingly. LLM-
assisted or LLM-generated peer review has been proposed as a remedy, but how such models
would treat manuscripts already published in top-tier journals – the closest thing the current
system has to a gold standard of scientific validity – is not known. Here we assembled a corpus of
200 articles, each released under a CC BY 4.0 license and published in 2026, from 20 general-
science, life-science, and clinical-medicine journals including Nature, Science, and Cell, and
asked four current LLMs – two proprietary, two open-weight – to produce independent peer-
review reports of each one under a blinded protocol. Their decisions were overwhelmingly harsh
and starkly inconsistent with each other: 605 of 800 reviews (75.6%) recommended major
revision or rejection, but the rate at which any single model did so ranged from 17.5% to 100%
(p < 0.001) – disagreement on manuscripts that had, by construction, already survived real
editorial review at the journals in question. When the same manuscripts were doctored with one
deliberately planted, field-specific methodological or statistical flaw, detection rates varied from
17.0% to 60.5% across models (p < 0.001). Recommending revision for papers that had already
passed editorial review (low specificity) while missing flaws deliberately placed in front of them
(low sensitivity), these models produce output that should be read for what it is – an uneven,
framing- and model-dependent signal.
1
Introduction
Submission volumes have grown faster than the supply of reviewers willing and able to evaluate
them [1, 2, 3]. Editors report escalating difficulty securing the two or three qualified reviewers a
typical manuscript requires, and turnaround times have lengthened accordingly [2, 4].
Part of this growth stems from career-evaluation systems that continue to reward the number of
publications (publish or perish) [1]. The sharp acceleration observed since 2024, however, is linked to
the development of generative AI tools [5, 6]: many steps in writing a scientific article can now be
assisted, or even automated. Growth has been particularly rapid in fields that do not depend on
experiments in the physical world [7], to the point that editorial policies are being revisited [8]. One
proposed remedy is to limit the number of submissions allowed per author [9].
Paradoxically, the very tools behind this rise in submissions can also be turned toward addressing
it. Many tasks in the reviewing process can now be assisted or automated: summarizing the
manuscript, drafting an initial pass at the review, or flagging obvious problems before the reviewer
commits their own attention. A 2025 Frontiers Media survey found that 53% of reviewers were
already using AI for peer review tasks [2]. Journals and conferences themselves lead this
adoption [10]: every main-track submission to AAAI-26 (22,977 papers) already received an AI-
generated review as part of a field deployment [11], and NEJM AI now offers an accelerated review
track that combines LLM analysis with human editorial judgment [12].
Despite the growing reach of LLMs in reviewing, there has been comparatively little systematic
evaluation of whether LLMs are actually good at the core task being delegated to them. A small
empirical literature bears on the question: in an expert assessment of reviews of 82 published Nature-
family papers, a reviewing agent built on GPT-5.2 scored above each paper’s top-rated human
reviewer on a composite of correctness, significance, and sufficiency of evidence, but AI reviewers


2
overlapped with each other far more than human reviewers did (21% versus 3%) [13]. Howell and
colleagues found that LLM reviewers are susceptible to bias, penalizing lower-prestige author
affiliations [14]. They have also been shown to be vulnerable to cosmetic rewriting, or paper
laundering [15]. Interestingly, AI-assisted reviews at ICLR systematically raised acceptance
probability near the decision threshold, pointing towards indecision [16].
However, no study has investigated how LLMs would review papers already published in top-tier
journals. These papers are the closest thing to a collective ground truth of what deserves to be
published (see Discussion for a contextualization of this claim). By measuring how many of them
would receive a favorable recommendation, we assess these models’ specificity: their ability to
recognize a sound paper as such. In addition, by injecting errors into the manuscripts, we assess their
sensitivity: their ability to detect a flaw [13, 17].
2
Methods
2.1
Manuscript corpus
We assembled a corpus of 200 recently published research and review articles (ten per journal) from
20 peer-reviewed journals spanning general-science, life-science, and clinical-medicine venues, from
broad-acceptance mega-journals (Scientific Reports) to flagship venues (Nature, Science, Cell):
Nature, Nature Medicine, Nature Communications, Nature Genetics, Nature Materials, Scientific
Reports, Science, Cell, Cell Reports Medicine, PNAS, eLife, PLOS Biology, PLOS Medicine,
Circulation, the European Heart Journal, the Journal of Experimental Medicine, JAMA Internal
Medicine, JAMA Oncology, JAMA Psychiatry, and the Lancet Global Health. Every included article
met four criteria: publication in 2026; a CC BY 4.0 license; a body of at least 2,000 words, excluding
short-format letters; and no correction, erratum, or retraction notices.
Full text was retrieved as JATS XML: via the Springer Nature Open Access API for the five
Springer-published journals, and via the Europe PMC API – which exposes license terms directly –
for the remaining 15. Title, abstract, and body text (including figure/table captions and in-XML table
content) were concatenated into plain text per article; rendered images and supplementary files were
not included.
2.2
Reviewer models and review protocol
Four LLMs served as reviewers, all accessed via the OpenRouter API:
•
z-ai/glm-5.3-flash (hereafter GLM-5.3 Flash)
•
openai/gpt-5.6-sol (hereafter GPT-5.6 Sol)
•
google/gemini-3.7-flash (hereafter Gemini 3.7 Flash)
•
deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731 (hereafter DeepSeek v4 Flash)
Models were selected on two criteria: a competitive ranking on the OpenRouter leaderboard (top
50) and a price below $5 per million output tokens. Each manuscript was submitted to each reviewer
model twice: once as a first submission, and once presented as a revision already through one round
of review. The two system prompts were identical except for their opening framing sentence, named
the manuscript’s true target journal, and asked for the review the model would submit to the editor: a
summary, major and minor concerns, and a final decision (accept, minor revision, major revision, or
reject). Models were not told the manuscript had already been published. The first-submission prompt
read:
You are a reviewer for {journal}. You will be shown the full text of a submitted
manuscript. Write the review you would hand to the Editor.
Structure your review as:
SUMMARY: <2-3 sentence summary of what the paper claims to show>
MAJOR CONCERNS:
- <substantive issue that would need to be resolved before publication, or
"None.">
MINOR CONCERNS:


3
- <smaller issues, wording, presentation>
Then end your response with exactly one line in this format, with no other text on
that line:
DECISION: <accept|minor_revision|major_revision|reject>
In the revision round, the opening sentence instead read: “You are a reviewer for {journal},
conducting the second round of peer review. You will be shown the full text of a submitted manuscript,
revised after an initial round of review.” Everything else, including the required output structure and
the final decision line, was unchanged. The decision was parsed programmatically from each
response. Unless stated otherwise, results refer to the first-submission round; the paired comparison
between rounds is reported in §3.2. Figure 1 summarizes the corpus, the reviewer panel, and the three
experiments.

off-panel judges.
We treated the four decision categories as an ordinal severity scale (accept = 0, minor revision =
1, major revision = 2, reject = 3) for quantitative analysis, while retaining the categorical labels for
descriptive reporting.
2.3
Error-injection experiment
To test whether reviewer models actually detect serious problems rather than producing generically
plausible criticism (Experiment 2), an off-panel model (x-ai/grok-4.6, not part of the reviewer
panel) planted one deliberate flaw into each manuscript as a targeted, verbatim string substitution
rather than full-manuscript regeneration. Flaws were assigned round-robin to one of two categories:
statistical (an analysis that does not match the data’s actual structure – for example, treating clustered
observations as independent) or methodological (an outcome assessed with undisclosed access to
biasing information, or an instrument that does not isolate what is claimed). Each flaw was designed


4
to be substantive and field-specific, severe enough to independently warrant major revision or
rejection.
Each of the four reviewer models then reviewed the altered manuscript under the protocol of
Experiment 1. Detection was judged by Claude Opus 5, given the manuscript’s central conclusion, the
planted flaw, and the review text, and scored as yes, partial or no; only yes counts as a catch below, so
the 125 partial verdicts (15.6% of the 800 reviews) are counted as misses. Scoring partial verdicts as
catches instead would raise detection from 60.5% to 84.0% for the best model and from 17.0% to
29.5% for the weakest; GPT-5.6 Sol and GLM-5.3 Flash would still rank first and second, and the two
weakest would tie.
2.4
Review-quality judging experiment
The first two experiments assess a reviewer model’s editorial decision (Experiment 1) and its ability
to detect a specific known flaw (Experiment 2). Neither speaks directly to the quality of a review’s
actual content when nothing is deliberately planted – whether its concerns are substantive or
superficial, and how it compares to the other models’ take on the identical manuscript. To address this
(Experiment 3), for each of the 200 manuscripts we took all four reviewer models’ Experiment-1
reviews of the true, unmodified manuscript and had two off-panel judges score them side by side: x-
ai/grok-4.6 and minimax/minimax-m3.
To keep the judges’ assessment blind to model identity, the four reviews for a given manuscript
were relabeled "Review A," "Review B," "Review C," and "Review D" in an order randomized
independently per manuscript, and the judge prompt made no reference to the reviews being AI-
generated at all – they were presented exactly as four anonymous referee reports. Given the
manuscript and its four relabeled reviews, the judge was asked to: (i) identify every major concern
raised across all four reviews and group substantively identical concerns – even if differently phrased
– into a single cluster, recording every review letter that raised it; (ii) classify each concern cluster as
relevant (a real, well-founded issue that could legitimately affect the paper’s conclusions or
acceptance) or nitpicking (a technically valid but minor, pedantic, or already-addressed point); (iii)
rate each review’s overall constructiveness on a 1–5 scale, independent of how harsh or lenient its
recommendation was; and (iv) name the single best review of the four and give a one-sentence
justification. The full judge prompt is reproduced in Appendix A. Both judges scored all 200
manuscripts, but for six of them minimax/minimax-m3 exhausted the default 4,000-token completion
budget on its reasoning trace and returned no parseable output; those six were re-run with a 16,000-
token budget, which is the only protocol difference between them and the other 194.
2.5
Statistical analysis
All statistical tests were computed in Python (SciPy 1.16, statsmodels 0.14). Differences in decision
severity across the four reviewer models (Experiment 1) were assessed with the Kruskal-Wallis test,
appropriate for an ordinal outcome without a normality assumption. Because the same 200
manuscripts were reviewed by all four models, these observations are not independent across models;
as a clustered robustness check we additionally computed a Friedman test, which treats manuscript as
a blocking factor. Differences across journals were assessed with a Kruskal-Wallis test, and the
comparison of Nature, Science and Cell with Scientific Reports with a Mann-Whitney U test. Paired
changes in the recommendation for the same manuscript and model – between the first-submission
and revision framings, and between the original and the flaw-injected manuscript – were assessed
with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Differences in detection rate across models (Experiment 2) were
assessed with a χ² test of independence on the 4-model × 2-outcome (caught/missed) contingency
table, and, as a clustered robustness check, with a logistic generalized estimating equation
(exchangeable working correlation, clustered on manuscript); detection by flaw category was
compared with a χ² test on the 2-category × 2-outcome table. For the review-quality judging
experiment (Experiment 3), the distribution of best-review picks across the four models was assessed
with a χ² goodness-of-fit test against a uniform null (each model picked best equally often, 25%); the
relationship between the number of reviewers independently raising a concern cluster and its
relevance was assessed with a χ² test of independence on the 4-agreement-level × 2-category
contingency table; agreement between the two judges was summarized with Cohen’s κ for the best-


5
review pick and a Spearman correlation for the constructiveness scores. We report descriptive means
on the four-point ordinal severity scale (accept = 0 through reject = 3) alongside these rank-based tests
for interpretability; all inferential tests, however, use ranks or exact paired differences rather than the
interval assumption a mean difference implies. All reported p-values are two-sided unless otherwise
noted, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
3
Results
3.1
Reviewer models disagree sharply on the same manuscripts
Across the same 200 manuscripts, the four reviewer models produced markedly different distributions
of recommended decisions (Figure 2): GPT-5.6 Sol recommended major revision or rejection for
200/200 manuscripts (100%), GLM-5.3 Flash for 180/200 (90.0%), Gemini 3.7 Flash for 35/200
(17.5%), and DeepSeek v4 Flash for 190/200 (95.0%) (Kruskal-Wallis H = 465.0, p < 0.001,
ε² = 0.58; Friedman test blocking on manuscript, χ² = 426.5, p < 0.001).
Figure 2. Distribution of recommended editorial decisions returned by each of the four reviewer models across the
same 200 manuscripts (n = 200 reviews per model).
Considering all four models jointly (Figure 3), only 21/200 manuscripts (10.5%) received an
identical decision from every reviewer – always a unanimous major-revision call, never a unanimous
accept – spread across 14 of the 20 journals.
Recommendations did not differ significantly by journal (Kruskal-Wallis across 20 journals, 40
reviews each, H = 23.0, p = 0.24), nor with prestige: the mean recommendation at Nature, Science,
and Cell (1.74 on the four-point scale, n = 120 reviews) was not significantly more lenient than at
Scientific Reports (1.98, Mann-Whitney p = 0.081).

Both judges named the same winning review. GPT-5.6 Sol’s was picked best of the four in 94.5% of
manuscripts by x-ai/grok-4.6 (189/200; χ² goodness-of-fit against uniform, χ² = 516.8, p < 0.001)

either judge (Figure 6a). GPT-5.6 Sol also raised the most relevant concerns, was rated most

identically on constructiveness (item-level Spearman ρ = 0.83 across 800 review-level scores;
Figure 6b–d). The two judges picked the same best review for 79.0% of the 200 manuscripts (Cohen’s
κ = 0.23, deflated by the concentration of wins in one model rather than by disagreement about which
model wins). Both also treated cross-model agreement as a relevance signal: concerns raised by a
single reviewer were judged nitpicking far more often than concerns raised by all four (x-ai/grok-

versus 20.5% of 3,112 clusters, χ² = 88.6, p < 0.001), an overlap that is structured by model
(Figure 7): Gemini 3.7 Flash’s small pool of concerns is almost entirely a subset of the other three
models’, which overlap with each other more evenly.

Discussion
Here, we show that reviews produced by four LLMs of articles published in top-tier biomedical
journals lead to variable, model-dependent, and generally harsh recommendations. All tested LLMs
lacked both sensitivity to errors and specificity, recommending major revision or rejection for papers
that had nevertheless been published in top journals.
The main finding of this study is the heterogeneity of the recommendations received for the same
manuscript depending on which model is queried. No manuscript that had actually been published
was unanimously accepted. The only decision the four models ever agreed on, for 21 of the 200
manuscripts, was major revision. This finding should not be considered specific to LLMs: human
reviewers are also known to be highly variable. Committees re-scoring the same submissions in the
NeurIPS consistency experiments disagreed on roughly a quarter of accept/reject decisions, with
about half of the accepted list changing under a re-run [18, 19], and inter-rater reliability across


9
journal peer review is low by any measure [20, 21]. Here, we show that an algorithmic solution,
which carries epistemological hopes of more objective decision-making, is itself similarly variable
and fragile, sensitive to both the model and the framing of the context. We do not argue that LLM-
generated reviews are no more valid than human reviews – the results reported by Kim and colleagues
are actually rather encouraging [13] – but that it is urgent to recognize that LLM assistance in editorial
decision-making is dependent on multiple factors, few of which are controllable or transparent. For
example, we show that Gemini 3.7 Flash is the most lenient of the tested models, but there is no
indication that further versions will follow the same pattern. An editor integrating an LLM into the
editorial process would therefore need to continuously monitor the model’s behavior on a local
benchmark.
We also show a tendency for LLMs to produce harsh reviews, suggesting major revision or
rejection for the majority of manuscripts. This behavior likely originates from a shared incentive
between models and reviewers to appear authoritative by not accepting a newly submitted manuscript
at face value. Trained to be thorough and to appear smart, these models may be optimizing for major
revision, the soft middle ground between rejection, which conveys the impression of knowing more
than others, and acceptance, which runs against the “fear of missing out” on a great manuscript. From
the authors’ standpoint, it offers no guarantee, yet warrants additional work.
For a reviewer, a major revision decision can mean two things: the precise identification of one or
more problems that can be resolved, or a failure to commit to a decision, which can in turn generate a
delay before rejection. Two findings support the latter interpretation for LLMs. First, inserting a
blocking methodological error barely changed the decision, even when the error was identified in the
review text, suggesting that the decision is not entirely derived from the review comments. Second,
framing the submission as a revision significantly shifted the decision toward acceptance, even though
the manuscript content remained unchanged. These findings highlight the fragility of editorial
recommendations and their bias toward indecision, which may have consequences for researchers.
This distinction also has epistemological implications. A human expert is accountable to the editor
who invited them, who can weigh their assessment and choose not to solicit their opinion again. On
the other hand, a machine’s review, while displaying the same formal apparel and authority, carries
something else, even when the concerns themselves are reasonable. Human judgment, expressed in a
human’s name, should be what calls for human actions – be it additional experiments, invitation to
submit elsewhere, or frame the problem differently. We argue that a machine’s output can inform that
judgment, but it should not substitute for it. The real danger is therefore a shift in function, that a tool
built for assistance takes a certification role it has no epistemological legitimacy to occupy. As
technology becomes mature and omnipresent, claims that this shift is inevitable will circulate, and are
already taking precedent [12].
If the decision itself carries a weak signal, our results nevertheless open the door to collaboration.
Indeed, the comments raised by the models themselves, particularly when they converge across
several models, appear to contain a signal that could help identify points of vulnerability. Future
studies should examine this convergence of signals through expert assessment and determine whether
it can provide insights into a manuscript that human reviewers had not detected.
This study has several limitations. First, our gold standard rests on the assumption that papers
published in the included journals are of sufficient quality. Retractions do occur, and scientific
controversy can discredit a manuscript. However, considering all articles as equally unreliable would
plunge research into a relativism that prevents authors and editors from engaging with these issues,
and we therefore make the explicit assumption that the editorial work of the journals concerned
provides a reasonable basis of trust. Second, none of the reviews underwent expert assessment. Kim
and colleagues have already undertaken this substantial work [13] and demonstrated the relative value
of these reviews. We are not addressing exactly the same question, instead focusing on LLM
recommendations, which have been little explored in the context of biomedical peer review. Third, the
LLMs selected were not the most expensive or largest models available. This choice was driven by
financial and ecological considerations in this exploratory project, as each experiment carries real
consequences in terms of carbon impact. Finally, the open-license articles selected may have leaked
into the models’ training data. We restricted the corpus to articles published in 2026, but some articles
may have been available earlier as preprints, which could have mildly contaminated some
experiments – although recognition of an article would more likely have had a positive effect.
