Auditing whether a text-to-image (T2I) model was fine-tuned on a particular image collection is difficult: a model
can absorb collection-level visual characteristics without reproducing training images. Proactive dataset-use
auditing plants a shared signal in a dataset before release, then later checks whether that signal appears in a
suspect model’s generated outputs. Existing approaches often trade off verifiability, fidelity, robustness, and de-
ployability. We introduce SpectralCanary, which uses a smooth low-pass mask to blend a fixed canary image
into every collection image while leaving captions unchanged and requiring neither auxiliary generation nor per-
image optimization. A lightweight verifier detects the transferred signal in suspect-model outputs. Across eight
tests on the Pokemon dataset and three realistic data transformations, spanning two architectures families,
SpectralCanary achieves the highest average per-generation TPR@1% FPR (91.1%) and fixed threshold model-
level accuracy using 30 generations (94.8%) among the evaluated methods. Across five datasets spanning artis-
tic, medical, satellite, and fashion domains with SD3.5, SpectralCanary averages 92.4% per-generation TPR@1%
FPR while preserving the released images (SSIM 0.989, LPIPS 0.022) with a near-zero mean change FID (mean
ΔFID 0.05) across datasets. Matched-energy ablations on Pokemon and ROCOv2 support our design choice,
low-frequency placement transfers more reliably compared to mid/high-frequency placement. Under the eval-
uated adaptive attacks, the most effective removal requires aggressive modification and incurs more than 25
ΔFID points. These results identify low-frequency visual signals as effective carriers for collection-level T2I
audit traces.

1. Introduction
Creative and scientific image collections embody substantial human labor, expertise, and investment.
Adapting text-to-image (T2I) models to such collections can support legitimate personalization, edu-
cation, design, and scientific communication [7, 11, 27]. The same capability, however, allows a third
party to fine-tune and deploy a model on a collection without the rights holder’s consent, attribution, or
compensation. Because a model can absorb a collection’s visual characteristics without reproducing
individual images, rights holders may have little evidence that their work was used [14, 22]. Practical
dataset-use auditing can strengthen accountability and licensing compliance while allowing images
to remain available for legitimate use.
We study proactive dataset-use auditing, commonly termed data traceability or unauthorized data-use
verification in prior works. A practical method should satisfy four requirements. (Req. 1) Collection-
level verifiability. It should provide evidence from black-box outputs even when no training image
is reproduced. (Req. 2) Fidelity and utility preservation. Protected samples should preserve their
original appearance and the utility of models trained on them, avoiding harm to legitimate use while
resisting easy filtering. (Req. 3) Pipeline robustness. The trace should remain detectable across

Prior approaches typically sacrifice at least one requirement. Reactive auditing: limited collection-
level evidence (Req. 1). Membership and similarity tests rely on sample-specific evidence that gen-
eralization may erase [14, 22]. Pixel-level watermarks: limited robustness (Req. 3). Weak signals
may be attenuated by augmentation, regeneration, or fail to transfer across model backbones [14, 35].
Explicit canaries: fidelity and robustness trade-offs (Reqs. 2–3). Repeated templates can be con-
spicuous and filterable, while recaptioning can remove textual triggers [26]. Adjacent data-poisoning
objectives. Poisoning induces chosen behavior rather than provides evidence of dataset use. Persis-
tent behavior may not be unique or reliably elicited, so it does not by itself establish use of a particular
collection [12, 34]. Across proactive methods, auxiliary generation, filtering, and iterative optimization
can also limit deployability (Req. 4) by increasing computational cost or requiring external models,
domain-specific assumptions [12, 14, 26]. These limitations motivate a subtle, transferable, caption-
independent, and inexpensive trace.
We propose SpectralCanary, whose key design choice is the frequency band used for the shared
trace. Before release, the dataset owner chooses one fixed canary image. For each collection image,
SpectralCanary mixes a small amount of the canary’s low-frequency Fourier content into the original
while retaining most of its spectrum and leaving its caption unchanged (Fig. 1(b)). The protected
spectra are thereby shifted toward a common low-frequency target, creating a collection-level signal.
Low frequencies encode broad spatial structure that neural networks preferentially learn [25, 38] and

Robustness tests cover a convolutional latent-diffusion U-

and recaptioning. Direct baselines include pixel-level traces (SIREN [14], DIAGNOSIS [35]) and ex-

Auditing effectiveness and robustness. For per-generation discrimination, AUC measures verifier-
score separation, while TPR at 1% FPR measures detection of outputs from protected-data mod-
els. Across eight Pokemon settings, SpectralCanary achieves 98.0% AUC and 91.1% TPR. In fixed-
threshold model-level verification using 30 generations, it achieves 94.8% average accuracy under
the same settings. Fidelity and utility preservation. SSIM and LPIPS compare original and pro-
tected images, while ΔFID measures the change in generative quality. Across five visual domains
with SD3.5, SpectralCanary achieves 99.5% AUC and 92.4% TPR with SSIM 0.989, LPIPS 0.022, with a
near-zero mean change FID (ΔFID 0.05). On sparse Sketch-Scene drawings, it achieves 100% AUC
and TPR with SSIM 0.997 and LPIPS 0.002. Operational deployability. Protection takes 0.39 seconds

to mid/high-frequency placement. Within the evaluated adaptive attacks, effective removal requires
aggressive modification and incurs more than 25 ΔFID points.
Our main contributions are:
• We identify low-frequency visual structure as an effective carrier for weak collection-level traces
during T2I fine-tuning. In matched band size and perturbation energy ablations on Pokemon and
ROCOv2 with SD1.5, we show that low-frequency placement yields higher per-generation TPR than
mid/high-frequency placement.
• We introduce SpectralCanary, a deterministic and caption-preserving spectral hybridization
method.
It protects each image with one inexpensive transform, requiring neither an auxiliary
generator nor per-image optimization.
• We jointly evaluate per-generation discrimination, KS-based multi-query auditing, fixed-threshold
model-level verification, image fidelity, generative utility, robustness, and protection cost across two
architecture families, five datasets, three direct auditing baselines, an adapted poisoning baseline,
and three realistic data transformations. This evaluation establishes a strong empirical operating
point and characterizes the boundary under adaptive spectral replacement.

Low frequencies encode broad structure that neural networks preferentially

weight frequencies and a small blending strength to control the overall change. Our controlled study
in Sec. 4.5 shows low-frequency placement yields higher detection rates than mid/high-frequency

3. Related Work
Reactive and proactive auditing. Reactive methods test an already trained model using member-
ship inference or similarity between generated and reference images [3, 20, 22, 24]. Such evidence
can weaken when fine-tuning transfers collection-level characteristics without retaining identifiable
information about individual samples. Proactive methods instead modify a collection before release.
Anti-learning methods aim to prevent or redirect customization [1, 16, 31, 33, 44]. Proactive traceability
aims to preserve customization utility while making later use statistically auditable.
Proactive traceability. Pixel-level methods such as DIAGNOSIS [35] and SIREN [14] insert weak
transformations or learned signals that can be detected in generated images. Their subtle signals pre-
serve appearance, but can be attenuated by augmentation, regeneration, or model backbone changes.
EnTruth instead inserts explicit canary samples containing a repeated template and textual trig-
ger [26]. This coherent signal can transfer strongly, but the injected samples are conspicuous and
caption triggers can be removed. Our direct comparisons span the two main proactive families in-
cluding subtle pixel-level traces and explicit visual canary samples.
Relation to poisoning. Data poisoning induces chosen behaviors such as concept corruption, repro-
duction, or persistent branding [12, 34]. Persistence alone is insufficient for auditing. Auditing instead
requires a collection-specific signal detectable under ordinary, trigger-free queries with controlled
false positives. SpectralCanary targets this auditing objective rather than model corruption. Appx. S
provides further discussion and a method taxonomy.

4.1 Experimental Setup and How to Read the Results
Our experiments ask four concrete questions. First, can SpectralCanary reveal that a model was
trained on a protected collection, even when the model architecture or training data are changed?
Second, does adding the trace visibly change the released images or make a model trained on them
produce worse images? Third, is protection fast enough for a large collection? Finally, why do low
frequencies matter, and what happens when an informed attacker tries to remove the trace?
Datasets and models. We cover distinct image domains: charater art (Pokemon-BLIP-Captions [23]),
sparse scene drawings (Sketch-Scene [4]), medical (ROCOv2 [28]), satellite (RSICD [17]), and fashion
(FACAD [39]). We fine-tune Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD1.5) [27] and Stable Diffusion 3.5 (SD3.5) [7]. The
main experiments use LoRA, a common parameter-efficient form of T2I adaptation. Appx. G repeats
the key test with full-parameter fine-tuning.
Comparisons and stress tests. We compare with SIREN [14] and DIAGNOSIS [35], which modify
training images, and EnTruth [26], which adds explicit canary samples. Appx. Q separately adapts
Silent Branding [12], whose original goal is visual branding rather than dataset-use auditing. On
Pokemon, we test each method under ordinary training and after three realistic changes to the training
set: image augmentation, diffusion-based recreation of each training image (image regeneration) [43],
and automatic recaptioning with Qwen2.5-VL 7B [2].
How to read the metrics.
We distinguish the embedded trace from two evaluation levels.
Per-
generation discrimination scores each generated image independently and asks whether outputs from
protected-data models receive higher verifier scores than outputs from clean-data models. AUC sum-
marizes this score separation over all thresholds.
TPR at 1% FPR is the fraction of outputs from
protected-data models detected at a threshold that falsely flags at most 1% of outputs from clean-
data models; thus, a TPR of 90% means detecting about 90 of 100 protected-data-model outputs while
flagging about 1 of 100 clean-data-model outputs. At the model level, we evaluate two decision pro-
tocols.
KS-based multi-query auditing combines scores from 30 generated images in a statistical
test and reports the rejection rate on protected-data models.
Fixed-threshold model-level verifica-
tion aggregates 30 generations from each held-out model and applies a threshold fixed after inde-
pendent calibration, reporting TPR, FPR, precision, F1, and accuracy (Appx. H). For image fidelity,
lower LPIPS [42] means that a protected image is closer to its original. For generated-image qual-
ity, ΔFID = FIDprotected −FIDclean [29]; values near or below zero indicate no measured quality loss,
whereas large positive values indicate degradation. Unless stated otherwise, SpectralCanary uses
𝛼= 0.02, 𝑐= 0.20, and 𝛿= 1. Every model-training and image-sampling setting is repeated with three
independent runs. Detailed protocols are in Appx. A.

per-generation AUC, TPR at 5% FPR, and run-level results are in Appx. C. Appx. J separately reports
KS-based multi-query auditing across query budgets, showing that more queries generally provide
stronger evidence of training on protected data.

4.3 The Same Setting Works Across Five Image Types
We next test whether the same SpectralCanary setting works beyond Pokemon by asking three ques-
tions: Does a single generated output support reliable per-generation discrimination? Do the pro-
tected images still resemble the originals? Does training on those images degrade model outputs?
SpectralCanary performs well on all three questions. At a per-generation FPR of 1%, it achieves
an average per-generation TPR of 92.4% across outputs from models trained on protected data. The
protected images remain close to their originals: average LPIPS is 0.022, where zero means identical
images. Training on the protected images causes almost no average change in model output quality:
mean ΔFID is 0.05, where zero means no change. On the sparse Sketch-Scene drawings, Spectral-
Canary achieves 100% per-generation TPR with LPIPS 0.002. The hardest dataset is RSICD, where it
achieves 78.9% per-generation TPR with LPIPS 0.048.
