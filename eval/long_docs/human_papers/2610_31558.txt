Diffusion Language Models for Perplexity-Aware Black-Box Attacks
Ali Al-Lawati∗, Elsayed Eshra∗, Dongwon Lee, Suhang Wang
The Pennsylvania State University, USA
{aha112,eme5375,dongwon,szw494,}@psu.edu; ∗Equal Contribution
Abstract
Continuous-space attacks find prompts that trig-
ger malicious behaviors, such as overthinking,
in black-box LLMs by sampling or optimiz-
ing over a low-dimensional Gaussian latent
space and decoding it to text through a ran-
dom mapping and nearest-token lookup. Be-
cause the random mapping is not learned from
language, its prompts can be highly unnatu-
ral, and as such easily detectable through input
guardrails, and the risk estimates they yield are
not grounded in natural prompts. In this paper,
we demonstrate that the same continuous latent-
space framework can be coupled directly to a
learned generative distribution over language, a
continuous diffusion language model (dLLM),
which removes the artificial random mapping
while preserving the standard-Gaussian latent
formulation and the downstream sampling or
optimization procedure. We evaluate this ap-
proach under two settings: sampling-based esti-
mation using RareTrap and optimization-based
search using ThinkTrap. We find that character-
level perplexity drops from 103–107 to 3–5 in
RareTrap and from 20–28 to 4.3–5.4 in Think-
Trap. We also find that model risk estimates
can differ by an order of magnitude across the
two mappings.

Introduction
A novel class of attacks on black-box LLMs has
recently been introduced, showing effectiveness
in triggering malicious behaviors, such as over-
thinking (Kumar et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a),
through optimization (Li et al., 2025b) or sam-
pling (Eshra et al., 2026a). These attacks, which
we refer to as continuous-space attacks, introduce
a low-dimensional continuous latent space and use
random projection matrices (Rahimi and Recht,
2007) to map latent vectors to discrete prompts.
This reduces adversarial prompt search to a con-
tinuous low-dimensional space while allowing the
target LLM to be queried only through its outputs.

We refer to this latent-to-prompt transformation
as random mapping. Random mappings enable
tractable continuous-space search on a black-box
target model by decoupling the search process from
the target model. However, despite their effective-
ness, this class of attacks suffers from a key weak-
ness: since random mappings are not learned from
natural language, their geometry reflects the statis-
tical structure of token embeddings rather than the
structure of actual texts. Consequently, the discov-
ered prompts from continuous-space attacks do not
resemble natural language and, as a result, exhibit
extremely high perplexity that makes them readily
distinguishable from authentic instructions (Zhang
et al., 2026). Thus, the prompts that trigger the
attack may be easily flagged by the input guardrails

of the LLM ecosystem. Moreover, because legiti-
mate users are unlikely to issue prompts with such
abnormally high perplexity, the LLM reliability es-
timates (Eshra et al., 2026a) based on a random
mapping distribution do not provide an effective
measure of LLM robustness to inputs that may or-
ganically occur in real deployment.
Therefore, it is critical to develop an effective
framework that generates adversarial and more
natural prompts that trigger malicious behaviors,
e.g., overthinking, and that can either estimate how
likely such behaviors are or search for prompts
that maximize them. However, there are several
challenges to developing such a framework: (i) the
mapping must be deterministic and preserve con-
tinuity to enable effective optimization and prob-
ability estimation; (ii) the framework must make
prompts natural without giving up black-box ac-
cess. Continuous-space attacks offer a natural foun-
dation for these tasks, but their effectiveness de-
pends on the mapping from latent to prompt space.
To address these challenges, the recent intro-
duction of continuous diffusion language models
(dLLMs) motivates us to propose an embarrass-
ingly simple yet effective framework. A dLLM can
serve the role of the random mapping by trans-
forming continuous noise into discrete text through
a learned generative process, while additionally
providing a representation shaped by language. Re-
cent work demonstrates that dLLMs can generate
almost entirely in continuous embedding space be-
fore realizing tokens (Hu et al., 2026), leverage
learned embedding geometry for substantial likeli-
hood gains (Yang et al., 2026), and formulate gener-
ation as a mapping from a latent prior to text (Guo
et al., 2026). As a result, a representation learned
directly for language modeling potentially offers a
more natural decoding space for continuous-space
attacks.
Therefore, in this paper, we investigate a novel
problem of whether a dLLM-based representation
can serve as a drop-in replacement for the ran-
dom mapping used in continuous-space attacks
(Figure 1). Using a pretrained dLLM’s standard-
Gaussian input noise as the latent keeps the map-
ping space continuous and keeps the attack black-
box, since the dLLM needs no access to the tar-
get beyond its outputs.
To this end, we ask
two research questions. (RQ1) Can dLLMs pro-
vide a viable alternative to the random mapping
in continuous-space attacks? (RQ2) Does using
a dLLM-based mapping produce attack prompts
that better resemble natural language? To answer
these questions, we conduct a focused study using
RareTrap (Eshra et al., 2026a), a sampling-based
attack framework for targeted behavior evaluation,
and ThinkTrap (Li et al., 2025b), an optimization-
based attack. Together, these frameworks cover
both sampling- and optimization-based approaches
to continuous-space attacks, allowing us to eval-
uate dLLM-based representations across comple-
mentary search paradigms.
Our main contributions are:
(i) We pro-
pose a plug-in framework in which a dLLM-
based mapping substitutes the random mapping in
both sampling- and optimization-based continuous-
space attacks without changing the search proce-
dure. RareTrap still finds over-generating prompts
on nine of ten targets, ThinkTrap makes compa-
rable progress across mappings, and the mapping
adds only 3–11% to the compute of ThinkTrap
(Findings 1, 2, and 4); (ii) We show that the dLLM-
based mapping’s prompts are far closer to natu-
ral text. Hit prompts (that trigger over-generation)
have character-level perplexity of 3–5 in RareTrap,
against 103–107 under the random mapping, and
4.3–5.4 in ThinkTrap, against 20–28, and condition-
ing on the behavior selects more natural prompts
(Findings 5–6); and (iii) We find that risk esti-
mates built from unnatural prompts can misjudge a
model’s risk on natural prompt inputs. The random
mapping overstates over-generation on most targets
and understates it on GPT-OSS-20B and Claude
Sonnet 4.6. It places Claude Sonnet 4.6 about 40×
below GPT-5.4 in terms of over-generation risk,
while under the dLLM-based mapping the two are
within a factor of 1.6 (Finding 3).
Together, these results show that the mapping
shapes both the prompts found by continuous-space
attacks and the behavior their risk estimates char-
acterize, independently of the search procedure.
Replacing the random mapping with a pretrained
dLLM preserves black-box access to the target, and
adds little computational cost. Furthermore, it turns
continuous-space sampling methods into more re-
liable tools to evaluate models, based on natural
prompts.

Related Work
Continuous-space attacks. Continuous-space at-
tacks build on prompt-tuning methods that opti-
mize a low-dimensional vector projected by a fixed
random matrix into a model’s embedding space,
2


using gradients (Wu et al., 2026) or, for black-
box models, gradient-free search (Sun et al., 2022).
ThinkTrap (Li et al., 2025b) uses this approach
to find prompts that induce long reasoning, while
RareTrap (Eshra et al., 2026a) uses it to estimate
over-generation and degenerate repetition. Both
methods decode the projected embeddings into
their nearest tokens in discrete space (Wen et al.,
2023). The Gaussian latent therefore defines a
tractable distribution over prompts. A complemen-
tary line of work searches a learned generative
model’s latent space to find inputs that remain close
to natural data. Zhao et al. (2018) search a GAN’s
latent space to produce natural adversarial exam-
ples, while REALISTA (Liang et al., 2026) attacks
LLMs by searching combinations of latent direc-
tions, each corresponding to a rephrasing of a given
prompt. We combine these ideas by preserving the
Gaussian latent interface of continuous-space at-
tacks while replacing the random mapping with a
learned mapping based on dLLMs.
Continuous diffusion language models. Continu-
ous dLLMs generate text by starting from Gaussian
noise in a continuous word-embedding space (Li
et al., 2022; Dieleman et al., 2022; Hu et al.,
2026; Yang et al., 2026) or an autoencoder’s latent
space (Lovelace et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2026),
and iteratively denoising it into representations that
are then mapped to tokens. In this work, we uti-
lize Cola-DLM (Guo et al., 2026), whose learned
prior turns standard Gaussian noise into continu-
ous latent representations that are then decoded
into tokens. This provides a learned mapping from
Gaussian inputs to input prompts, similar to how
random mappings are used in continuous-space
attacks.
Readable attacks and perplexity filters. Perplex-
ity filters (Alon and Kamfonas, 2023; Jain et al.,
2023) flag unnatural, high-perplexity prompts.
These filters have motivated attacks that generate
more natural, readable adversarial prompts (Liu
et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2023;
Paulus et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024; Lucas et al.,
2026). However, these attacks often require gra-
dients, log-probabilities, or access to an attacker
LLM to optimize the adversarial prompts. In con-
trast, we improve the naturalness of continuous-
space attacks by replacing their random mapping
with a dLLM-based mapping, while retaining the
original sampling or optimization procedure. This
yields substantially lower prompt perplexity with-
out requiring these additional sources of access or

Resource exhaustion attacks. Prior work stud-

Methodology
Our approach replaces the random mapping used
by existing continuous-space attacks with a map-
ping learned by a dLLM, while keeping the down-
stream search and evaluation procedure unchanged.
We apply this substitution to two such attacks,
RareTrap (Eshra et al., 2026a) and ThinkTrap (Li
et al., 2025b), which pair a random mapping with,
respectively, a sampling-based rare-event estima-
tor and a derivative-free optimizer. This controlled
substitution allows us to isolate the effect of the
prompt representation.
3.1
Preliminaries: RareTrap and ThinkTrap
Both frameworks, RareTrap and ThinkTrap, search
for prompts indirectly through a continuous latent
variable z ∈RD, decoded to a discrete prompt by
a fixed, deterministic projection, and both reduce
the target’s response to a scalar behavioral measure
r(y) (output length, in our experiments). As Fig-
ure 2 shows, a latent z ∼N(0, ID) is projected
to embeddings E = Az, decoded to a prompt
p = NN-decode(E), and sent to the target, where
A is a fixed random matrix mapping the latent into
an embedding space. The response y = M(p) is
scored by r(y). The sampling or optimization pro-
cedure then uses r(y) to propose the next latents.
3


ThinkTrap draws the entries of A independently
from N(0, 1/D) for each prompt token. RareTrap
instead builds A from random orthonormal blocks
multiplied by the square root of the surrogate’s em-
bedding covariance, and adds the embedding mean
as a bias, so projected embeddings are geometry-
aware: centered and scaled like the surrogate’s
token embeddings. In both methods, NN-decode
replaces each projected position with its nearest
token embedding in a surrogate model’s table (the
target is assumed black-box). Let hRP denote the
composition z 7→p from RD to the prompt space
P, such that the pipeline is r(M(hRP(z))), and
M is the target model. Both frameworks query M
only through hRP, and thus require only black-box
access to the target.

the probability that the response length reaches or
exceeds a severity threshold τ. Defining g(z; τ) =
τ −r(M(hRP(z))) as the difference between the
threshold and the response length, we follow Eshra
et al. (2026a) and write this probability as:

Optimization-based search.
ThinkTrap instead
treats −r(M(hRP(z))) as an objective to min-
imize directly.
It maintains a search distribu-
tion over z, initialized at N(0, ID), and uses a
derivative-free optimizer, CMA-ES (Hansen and
Ostermeier, 2001), to iteratively update that distri-
bution toward latents whose decoded prompt makes
the target generate longer output. At every itera-
tion it only evaluates r(M(hRP(z))) at a batch of
candidate z’s it proposes, and only requires hRP to
be a deterministic map from RD to prompts.

The map-
ping hRP is not trained to produce natural lan-
guage. It first applies a random matrix A to the
latent vector, then uses NN-decode to select a to-
ken independently at each position, without ac-
counting for neighboring tokens. As a result, the
mapping does not favor coherent word sequences,

Random Mapping →dLLM-based
Mapping
We instantiate a learned mapping hdiff using Cola-
DLM (Guo et al., 2026), which generates text by
transforming Gaussian noise into continuous text
representations before decoding them into tokens.
The dLLM-based mapping replaces both the ran-
dom projection and nearest-token decoding with
Cola-DLM. Its input z is the Gaussian noise Cola-
DLM consumes, whose dimension D is set by the
framework’s prompt length (Appendix A). We de-
fine:
hdiff(z) = Decode (Φψ(z)) ,
(1)
where Φψ is Cola-DLM’s learned prior, which
transforms Gaussian noise into continuous text la-
tents block-wise, conditioning each block on those
that precede it, and Decode is Cola-DLM’s de-
coder from latents to tokens. The input noise fol-
lows N(0, ID). With inference settings and batch
size fixed, and greedy decoding, hdiff is a deter-
ministic function of z and can replace hRP without
changing the downstream attack procedure. The
only change is the dimension of the latent search
space. Unlike NN-decode, which picks each to-
ken independently, Cola-DLM is trained on text
and generates each block conditioned on the previ-
ous ones to yield more natural prompts, while still
operating in Gaussian space.

4
Experiments
We evaluate the dLLM-based mapping on RareTrap
and ThinkTrap, measuring its effect on attack ef-
fectiveness and prompt perplexity relative to ran-
dom mapping. Section 4.1 describes the protocols,
baselines, and metrics. Section 4.2 asks whether
the dLLM-based mapping still finds the behavior,
what the change of mapping reveals about the mod-
els, and at what cost (RQ1), and Section 4.3 asks
whether its prompts read as natural language (RQ2).
Section 4.4 then discusses two details that affect
how results compare across the frameworks.
4.1
Experiment Setup
RareTrap protocol.
We follow RareTrap’s over-
generation protocol, enabling native thinking mode
where supported and using greedy decoding with a
20,003-token output cap. The random mapping
uses 40-token prompts with D = 200. To ap-
proximately match their length, the dLLM-based
mapping uses 47-token prompts, yielding D =
16 × 47 = 752 because Cola-DLM allocates 16
latent dimensions per token (Appendix A).
As with RareTrap, we estimate the probability of
triggering a response of at least 20,000 tokens using
Subset Simulation at 1,000 samples per level. We
keep the top 10% of each level as seeds for the next,
and run at most five levels. If at least 20 of the first
200 initial samples satisfy the event, we estimate
its probability using direct Monte Carlo instead.
We run the dLLM-based mapping with three seeds
per target and report the median, with per-seed
values and 95% intervals in Appendix E. For the
random-mapping baseline, we use RareTrap’s pub-
lished results. For API targets, decoding uses the
provider’s default temperature, and output length
includes both reasoning and visible tokens.
ThinkTrap protocol.
We follow ThinkTrap’s
overthinking protocol, which decodes the target’s
responses at temperature 1.0 with a 4,096-token
output cap. In each CMA-ES generation, 12 la-
tent vectors are sampled, decoded into prompts,
and sent to the target, and the search distribution
moves toward those whose responses are longest.
At these targets’ output lengths, each generation
costs about 7,000–23,000 tokens (Table 3), so the
10,000–100,000-token budgets ThinkTrap reports
would allow only a few generations, which may be
too few to measure search progress. We therefore
use a budget of 300,000 generated tokens, which
allows 13–40 generations. The dLLM-based map-
ping’s latent is Cola-DLM’s input noise for one
generation block, 16 dimensions for each of the
15 tokens generated after a one-token prefix, so
D = 240, thus matching ThinkTrap’s 15-token
prompt. We run both mappings with the same bud-
get and targets, over three seeds.
Target LLMs.
We evaluate RareTrap on ten tar-
gets, eight open-weight models and two cloud API
models, as detailed in Appendix B. ThinkTrap
uses DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (DeepSeek-
AI, 2025), Qwen3-14B (Yang et al., 2025), and
Phi-4-reasoning (Abdin et al., 2025).
Baselines.
For RareTrap we compare against its
random mapping with the Qwen3-0.6B surrogate,
40-token prompts, and D = 200. For all ten targets
we use the estimates reported by RareTrap and its
released random-mapping populations (Eshra et al.,
2026b) (Appendix D), and we score the prompts
in those populations for the perplexity findings be-
low (§4.3). For ThinkTrap we rerun its random
mapping, the plain Gaussian form of hRP (§3.1),
with our reimplementation of its published map-
ping, using the same Qwen3-0.6B surrogate and
ThinkTrap’s D = 20 (Li et al., 2025b).
