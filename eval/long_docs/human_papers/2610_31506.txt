ABSTRACT
Recommender systems typically serve one user at a time. However in households, members share a
finite pantry and follow different dietary rules, making independent recommendations prone to re-
source conflicts and dietary violations. We formulate household food recommendation as constrained
fair division and present FairFRESH, a neuro-symbolic framework that learns per-member utilities,
allocates foods under explicit welfare objectives, enforces dietary rules through a deterministic filter,
and explains recommendations using Shapley and Banzhaf attributions. Theoretically, we show
that no finite penalty guarantees compliance near a dietary-rule threshold, establish approximation
guarantees for greedy allocation under utilitarian and smoothed Nash objectives in the stock-free
model before filtering, and characterize the scale invariance of Nash welfare. We also use the minimax
theorem to analyze rotating shared meals. On NHANES-derived data, the learned utility model
achieves Recall@20 of 0.293, outperforming five learned recommenders (at most 0.244) but not
a previous-day repetition heuristic (0.321). On held-out four-person households, Nash allocation
reduces Gini disparity by 42% relative to conflict resolution while improving Recall@20 from 0.180
to 0.197. It comes within 2% of an upper bound on the smoothed Nash optimum, and rescaling one
member’s utilities changes at most 1.5% of assignments. All recommendations satisfy the encoded
dietary rules, while attribution scores correlate with utility loss upon component removal (r = 0.76).
In simulation, shared meals reduce pantry use by about one-third, and weekly rotation increases the
worst-off member’s utility by 38% over the best fixed meal.

Introduction
Recommender systems usually model one user at a time. That assumption suits film or music, but not food: meals are
planned around a shared pantry and shaped by the dietary needs of everyone at the table. When two members of a
household are recommended the same scarce item, at most one of them can use it, however highly a per-user model
scores it for both. When members follow different dietary rules, the same pantry supports different safe choices for
each of them (Fig. 1). Household food recommendation is therefore not a ranking problem repeated for every member
but an allocation problem over a shared, finite resource.
Existing systems address parts of this problem. Health-aware recommenders optimize nutrition jointly with preference,
as in MOPI-HFRS [38], or reason over food knowledge graphs, as in SwissFKG [1], but they score each user against
that user’s own history. Caldeira et al. [9] bring pantry state into the model, but only as a per-user ranking feature,

package that every member shares [28, 34], so they never divide a resource that one member’s choice depletes for
another. Dietary safety is typically encoded as a learned penalty or a soft objective, which a strong taste or popularity
signal can outweigh, and explanations, where present, address one user’s ranking rather than a household’s allocation.
Fair division studies how to divide a finite set of goods among agents with different preferences so that the outcome
is efficient and equitable [29], which is the situation of a household sharing one pantry. We introduce FairFRESH,
a neuro-symbolic framework that treats household food recommendation as fair division of indivisible goods under
per-member dietary rules.
A three-stage pipeline produces each household recommendation. In the first stage, a learned utility model combines
taste, pantry, and retrieval-augmented dietary encoders to score every member–food pair. In the second stage, an explicit
welfare rule (utilitarian, max-min, or Nash) allocates foods from the resulting utility matrix, so that no item is promised
to two members. In the third stage, a deterministic symbolic filter removes any food that breaks a member’s dietary
rule. A post-hoc module then explains the verified recommendations with Shapley and Banzhaf attributions over the
pipeline’s components. This division of labor allows the learned components to rank, while the filter alone decides
safety and the explanation module never feeds back into a decision. Every final recommendation therefore satisfies the
encoded dietary rules, whatever the learned scores.
We evaluate FairFRESH on NHANES-derived households of two to six members, comparing it with five recommender
baselines and five allocation strategies. The evaluation addresses four research questions. RQ-A: does a food’s
availability, measured by the member’s previous-day consumption, carry predictive signal beyond taste? RQ-B: how
does the allocation rule change equity when members compete for one pantry, and at what cost in accuracy? RQ-C:
does retrieval-augmented encoding of dietary constraints cover members’ requirements without weakening the safety
guarantee? RQ-D: can cooperative-game attributions explain household recommendations faithfully, without changing
the decisions they explain?
Contributions
This paper makes the following contributions.
1. We introduce FairFRESH, which formulates household food recommendation as fair division of a shared, finite
pantry: servings are private goods allocated under per-member safe sets and a cap of K items (Section 3.1).
We prove that no finite penalty weight can certify safety near a rule threshold (Proposition 1), so FairFRESH
enforces the rules with a deterministic filter, and we show that, at the optimum, allocating within safe sets
dominates filtering afterwards (Proposition 4).
2. The key component of FairFRESH is an explicit allocation layer. One greedy procedure implements the
utilitarian, max-min, and Nash rules; we bound it for the utilitarian and smoothed Nash objectives before
filtering (Proposition 2), find it within 1–5% of exact optima in practice, and show through bargaining theory
that only the exact Nash rule is invariant to how members’ utilities are scaled (Proposition 3). Shapley and
Banzhaf attributions then explain the household allocation, not one user’s ranking (Section 3.5).
3. We extend FairFRESH to dishes cooked once and shared by the household. Using the minimax theorem, we
show that rotating shared dishes over days can only help the worst-off member, and that a linear program
computes the best rotation (Proposition 5).
4. We provide a detailed empirical evaluation on households of two to six members, with all allocation results on
users disjoint from training. The utility model ranks above five learned recommenders, and a repeat heuristic
shows that previous-day consumption carries most of the signal. Under exclusive household allocation, Nash
allocation lowers Gini by 42% relative to conflict resolution with higher Recall@20 and changes at most 1.5%
of its assignments when utilities are rescaled. Shared meals save about a third of the pantry (Sections 5 and 6).

Related Work
Most food recommenders model one user at a time. Freyne and Berkovsky [16] personalize recipes from ratings, Ge et
al. [17] add a health objective, and Trattner and Elsweiler [37] study the healthiness of online recipes. Food knowledge

jointly with preference. Where ingredient availability appears [9], it is a per-user ranking feature. None of these systems
divides a pantry that one member’s choice depletes for another.

household is instead a small, fully known group sharing one pool of resources. Group recommenders aggregate member

Fair division studies exactly this setting. For indivisible goods with additive valuations [29], envy-free allocations
need not exist, but EF1 allocations do, and round robin finds one in polynomial time [10]. Without further constraints,
every Nash-optimal allocation is Pareto-optimal and EF1 [10], but computing one is APX-hard [32, 25], and the
max-min optimum is NP-hard to approximate within any factor below two [4]. Cardinality limits change which of these
guarantees survive [36, 7].
Reviewer assignment poses a closely related constrained problem, where Stelmakh et al. maximize the quality of the
most disadvantaged paper under load constraints [35]; members play the role of papers and foods that of reviewers.
When every agent consumes an item, allocation becomes a public decision judged by how well the common choice
serves each agent [12, 14]. This literature assumes known valuations and has not been connected to learned utilities or
dietary constraints.
Neuro-symbolic methods such as logic tensor networks [11, 2] relax symbolic constraints into differentiable objectives,
which a high taste or popularity score can outweigh. Post-hoc explanation methods such as the Shapley value [26] and
the Banzhaf index [3], evaluated with the faithfulness correlation of Bhatt et al. [5], attribute a single prediction to its
inputs. Neither line addresses a guarantee or an explanation at the level of a household allocation.
FairFRESH takes learned per-member utilities from the recommendation literature and passes them to a fair-division
allocation layer, so the pantry is divided rather than scored separately for each member. Its soft penalty comes from the
same symbolic rules as a deterministic filter, which alone decides compliance (Sections 3.2 and 3.4). Its attributions are
defined over the household allocation rather than one user’s ranking, and shared dishes enter as public goods (Section 6).
3
Methodology
Figure 2 shows the architecture of FairFRESH. The pipeline has four stages: (1) the utility model, (2) the allocation
layer, (3) the symbolic safety filter, and (4) the post-hoc explainability module.
Household inputs feed the utility model, whose scores form the household utility matrix. The allocation layer assigns
foods from that matrix under an explicit welfare rule, and the safety filter removes any food that breaks its member’s
dietary rules. The explainability module then explains the verified recommendations without feeding back into any
earlier stage. We first define the problem and then describe each stage.
3.1
Preliminaries and Problem Definition
Model. A household is a set of members N = {1, . . . , n} sharing a fixed pantry P. Each member i contributes
stated preferences, an interaction history, and a health profile (conditions, dietary tags, and allergies) that determines a
set of dietary rules Ci. Over a food catalog F = {f1, . . . , fm}, FairFRESH learns a utility Ui(f) ∈(0, 1) for every
member–food pair (Section 3.2) and recommends to each member a bundle Ai ⊆F. Bundles are disjoint, because an
item allocated to one member is unavailable to another, and each holds at most K items; Section 6 relaxes disjointness
for dishes cooked once and shared by the household. Since nK is far smaller than m, allocations are partial: most of
the catalog stays unallocated. Utilities are additive, Ui(Ai) = P

Filter placement. The filter runs after allocation so that it stays independent of the allocation rule and can audit any
upstream policy. The cost is that removed foods are not replaced, so a verified bundle can hold fewer than K items, and

which never does worse at the optimum:
