Abstract
The attention mechanism underpins modern large language models, yet their dynamics re-
main only partially understood. Recent perspectives interpret attention blocks as interacting
particle systems, whose mean-field limits correspond to gradient flows of energy functionals
on spaces of probability densities on Rd equipped with Wasserstein-type metrics. We extend
this viewpoint by deriving accelerated attention blocks from inertial, Nesterov-type damped
Hamiltonian dynamics on these density spaces, developing the geometry and differential cal-
culus needed to make the construction rigorous. In particular, we introduce a local-mobility
geometry for the Otto–Wasserstein and softmax–Wasserstein settings, and a quotient Stein ge-
ometry that resolves the degeneracy of the linear-attention kernel to yield a well-defined metric
and differential calculus. We provide rigorous foundations when softmax attention admits a
gradient-flow formulation and when its inertial counterpart admits a damped Hamiltonian on
explicitly characterized domains. For linear attention, we obtain a finite-dimensional solution
theory with sharp conditions for global existence versus finite-time second-moment blow-up.
We further prove a quantitative link between continuous moment blow-up and the discrete-
layer depth at which token norms cross a prescribed threshold.
Finally, motivated by the
finite-token geometry, we propose geometric corrections to token and momentum updates. In
decoder-only, nanoGPT-style experiments on TinyStories and OpenWebText, these corrections
lower validation negative log-likelihood on next-token prediction relative to momentum-only
controls.
Keywords: transformers; attention dynamics; local-mobility geometry; Wasserstein geometry;
quotient Stein geometry; differential calculus; damped Hamiltonian dynamics; finite-time blow-up;
discrete layer depth.

Introduction
This paper develops foundations of infinite-dimensional Nesterov acceleration and connects it to the
mathematical theory of deep transformers—two fields that at first glance appear largely unrelated.
To motivate this connection, we first consider the role of attention in transformers.
The transformer architecture introduced in Attention Is All You Need [77] forms the backbone of
modern large language models. Its attention layers update token representations by allowing tokens
to exchange information across a sequence. The widespread use and societal importance of these
systems make a mathematical understanding of their behavior important. Although attention has
become an active subject of mathematical research, with contributions to approximation theory,
stability, learning, and the dynamics of token interactions [17,24,30,41,45,81,89], even the behavior
of the original transformer architecture remains only partially understood.
Attention layers can be viewed as discrete dynamical systems for token representations [24,
81] and, in a continuous-depth limit, as interacting particle systems [35, 51, 68]. Under suitable
assumptions, a subsequent mean-field limit describes the evolution of the token distribution through
a non-linear transport equation, allowing PDE methods to be used to study collective behavior
[13, 16]. We provide further details in Section 2 and illustrate this passage in Figure 2. In their
recent work, Castin–Ablin–Carrillo–Peyré [16] establish well-posedness of the mean-field PDE for
softmax attention for compactly supported initial data and study Gaussian initial data separately.
General non-Gaussian initial densities with unbounded support remain outside their analysis. As
in their work, we consider probability densities on Rd, allowing unbounded support, which lets us
study growth in token magnitudes, which sphere-constrained models exclude [13,33–35,56].
The transformer PDE arising in the mean-field limit can also be interpreted formally as a
gradient flow of an interaction energy on a space of probability densities equipped with a suitable
Riemannian metric. For softmax attention, Castin et al. [16, Sec. 5] develop this interpretation,
but explicitly leave its rigorous geometric justification open. Before moving on, we highlight several
obstacles to making this gradient-flow interpretation rigorous. The first concerns differentiation
itself: the admissible density classes on Rd are not assumed to carry a smooth manifold structure.
Therefore the relevant tangent and cotangent spaces and notions of differentiation must be specified
explicitly. A further difficulty comes from weak Riemannian metrics, for which even smooth energies
need not admit a gradient vector field; see Section 4.1. To define the Riemannian metrics considered
here, we follow a guiding principle: associate a differential operator, called the mobility operator,
with the attention kernel and use its inverse to define the metric. Making this construction rigorous
requires identifying suitable function spaces on which this operator is invertible. Figure 3 illustrates
this passage from attention kernels to Riemannian metrics.
A natural extension of this viewpoint is inspired by Nesterov acceleration [60] in finite-dimensional
optimization, which, roughly speaking, introduces momentum to accelerate gradient descent; see
Section 3. On spaces of probability densities, this leads us to study infinite-dimensional damped
Newton systems, a particular class of damped Hamiltonian systems. Newton dynamics on such
spaces also arise in geometric hydrodynamics; see Khesin–Misiołek–Modin [40]. Wang and Li [84]
study such damped Newton dynamics as accelerated gradient flows on spaces of probability densi-
ties. However, their geometric construction has serious analytic gaps even in the Otto–Wasserstein
and Stein settings; see Section 4 and Appendix A. The authors of [71] use this framework to derive
inertial PDEs for linear and softmax attention, but leave the underlying geometric construction at
a formal level.
These connections motivate the following question: Can PDE methods, functional analysis, and

Main results.
Our paper makes the following main contributions, whose connections we sum-
marize in Figure 1.
1. Foundations for infinite-dimensional Nesterov acceleration. For linear and softmax at-
tention, we develop the geometric foundations needed to make the formal gradient-flow and damped
Hamiltonian formulations rigorous on specified domains (Corollary 6.4, Theorem 6.7, Proposi-
tion 7.4, and Lemma 7.16). This provides the rigorous justification left open in [16, Sec. 5.4]. For
softmax attention, we introduce a local-mobility geometry, which includes both Otto–Wasserstein
and softmax–Wasserstein geometry on smooth probability densities over Rd (Theorem 4.3). For
linear attention, we introduce a quotient Stein geometry adapted to the bilinear attention kernel
(Theorem 5.3). Since a differentiable structure on the spaces of probability densities involved is not
given a priori, we develop the associated differential calculus directly on these spaces (Appendices B
and C).
2. Well-posedness and blow-up for the linear-attention transformer PDE and its ac-
celerated counterpart. By choosing coordinates adapted to the evolving density, we reduce the
infinite-dimensional dynamics of both equations to finite-dimensional matrix ODEs. The resulting
two-way PDE–ODE correspondences yield existence and uniqueness of strong solutions for admis-
sible non-Gaussian initial data with potentially unbounded support (Theorems 7.6 and 7.18). In
the accelerated case, we require a quadratic initial momentum potential. These reductions also give
conditions for global-in-time existence and finite-time second-moment blow-up (Proposition 7.13
and Theorem 7.23).
3.
From PDE blow-up to growth of token norms across layers. For accelerated linear
attention at fixed token count, we use continuous moment growth to predict the first explicit-Euler
layer crossing a prescribed directional second-moment threshold. The prediction has a bounded
error in layer count as the step size tends to zero (Theorem 7.26).
As a corollary, continuum
moment blow-up implies that every prescribed token-norm bound is exceeded in the discrete layers
for sufficiently small step sizes (Corollary 7.28).
To the best of our knowledge, these are the
first results linking continuum moment blow-up to quantitative threshold-depth predictions and
arbitrarily large token norms in discrete attention networks at fixed token count.
4. Numerical evaluation of geometric attention updates. We derive corrections to token
and momentum updates from the finite-token softmax metric (Lemma 6.11). On TinyStories and
OpenWebText, we compare practical versions of these updates with matched momentum-only con-
trols. The corrections give small reductions in mean validation loss in some settings, at increased
computational cost (Section 9).
We discuss the relation to previous work in Section 10.

Geometry of the mean-field limit.
Under suitable assumptions on the attention matrices, the
mean-field equation (5) admits a formal gradient-flow interpretation. Recall that a Riemannian
gradient flow on a manifold M is specified by a pair (F, G): a smooth functional F : M →R and
a Riemannian metric G. Thus, identifying an energy is only part of the construction; the geometry
determines the corresponding direction of steepest descent.
Making this interpretation rigorous requires specifying the underlying space of probability den-
sities. A central distinction is whether these densities have compact support or extend over all of
Rd. In the latter case, additional integrability and coercivity conditions are needed to justify the
formal geometric operators. For softmax attention, a general rigorous justification of this geometry
for non-compactly supported densities was left open in [16, Sec. 5].
This geometric viewpoint provides a way to introduce momentum into attention dynamics,
following the principle of Nesterov acceleration, as discussed in the next section.
Discretizing
the resulting inertial equations yields attention layers that propagate both token positions and a
momentum variable across network depth.
We close this subsection by recalling the guiding question of this paper:
Can PDE methods, functional analysis, and geometry applied to the mean-field limit
yield new insights into the dynamics of transformers with finitely many tokens and
layers?

Nesterov’s Acceleration Method and Attention Models
The aim of this section is to explain how Nesterov acceleration provides a way to introduce mo-
mentum into attention dynamics and to identify the geometric constructions needed to justify this
approach.
We first recall Nesterov acceleration in Euclidean space and its continuous-time formulation as
the damped Hamiltonian system (10). We then describe the corresponding construction for smooth
probability densities on compact manifolds, leading to the accelerated Otto–Wasserstein equations
(13). This construction relies on the mobility operator being invertible between suitable cotangent
and tangent spaces. We explain why this requirement presents an obstruction in Stein geometry.

Finally, we connect this viewpoint to the linear and softmax attention models introduced in
Section 2. Their kernels give rise to different mobility operators and hence different formal ge-
ometric descriptions. We state the resulting accelerated systems in (17) and (19), and identify
the function-space and differentiability questions that must be resolved to make them rigorous.
These questions motivate the local-mobility calculus developed in Section 4 and the quotient Stein
geometry developed in Section 5.

3.3
Nesterov Acceleration and Attention Models on Rd
This subsection connects the previously discussed interpretation of transformers as dynamical sys-
tems with Nesterov acceleration on Rd, motivating the theory developed later in this paper. At a
high level, each choice of a self-attention kernel in (1) corresponds to a choice of geometry. More
precisely, the softmax attention kernel corresponds to softmax Otto–Wasserstein geometry, while
the linear attention kernel corresponds to a geometry reminiscent of Stein geometry. We begin by
stating at this point formally the analog of (13) in these geometries. At a formal level, the self-
attention kernel determines a mobility operator, whose inverse on suitable tangent and cotangent
spaces induces the corresponding metric. This is the mechanism through which the choice of an
attention kernel determines a geometry.
