Conservative Meta-Agent Search:
Architecture Adaptation under Non-Stationarity
Ali Atiah Alzahrani†
Abstract
Automated design of agentic systems is typically evaluated on static task suites with
immediate scalar rewards. Deployed systems face persistent shift, delayed depen-
dent feedback, correlated component failures, and costly architecture changes. We
formalize meta-agent architecture search as sequential incumbent replacement with
switching costs and hard risk constraints, and introduce Conservative Meta-Agent
Search (CMAS). Throughout, meta-agent denotes the entire outer loop: proposer,
compiler, evaluator, gate, and rollback controller. CMAS retrieves archived archi-
tectures and proposes typed graph edits, then scores each against the incumbent
by paired shadow replay under common random numbers. Component credit is
interventional. Promotion requires an empirically calibrated, variance-penalized
simultaneous score above a pre-specified margin, and is followed by a simulated
canary stage and pre-specified rollback. Backtested return cannot certify meta-level
skill, so we introduce METAINVEST-BENCH, a regime-switching simulator whose
specified worker competence makes a surrogate-optimal architecture computable,
with leakage-controlled replay, adversarial governance tracks, and five reusable
negative controls. Financial streams give an instrumented, risk-sensitive test of
meta-level generalization; we claim no alpha. Across 96 streams, CMAS cuts
normalized mean surrogate dynamic-oracle regret from 0.116 to 0.104 and raises
held-out utility from 0.44 to 0.48 over the strongest adaptive common-budget reim-
plementation; when extra workers add no value, it contracts to near-single-agent
teams. Its deployment inference cost is ∼6% lower, though its search-inclusive
token cost is the highest of the methods compared. The observed per-update false-
promotion rate is 3.4% against a nominal 5% target, and 4.8% in a sealed rerun.
The statistical ingredients are standard. The calibration is empirical: it concerns
an update-level replay statistic on an author-designed benchmark, and guarantees
neither full-utility improvement, nor absence of simulated canary-stage harm, nor
sequence-level error control.

Introduction
Meta-agents act on agentic systems.
They design harnesses, optimize compound workflows,
allocate resources, and supervise workers. An LLM system’s performance depends on weights,
but also on prompts, tools, memory, communication, verification, and control flow [1, 3, 5], and a
growing literature searches code-defined workflows [19], modular components [14], communication
graphs [23, 18], and query-dependent architectures [9, 20]. Yet most evaluations remain static:
candidates receive immediate scores on exchangeable tasks, and the best design is deployed without
modeling persistent change or the risk of a bad update.
Architecture is therefore a decision variable that should track the regime (Figure 1). Investment
systems expose non-stationarity, delayed and dependent feedback, correlated component errors,

persuasive [6, 7, 13]. What is missing is (i) a controlled benchmark whose surrogate-optimal worker
architecture is computable and changes over time, and (ii) a method that screens candidate updates un-
der noisy, delayed feedback [17, 15, 12, 2]. An update whose simulator-evaluated mean improvement
is at most the promotion margin is a false promotion, the meta-level analogue of a false discovery
and a near-neutral change; Table 2 separates it from the outcomes that do make the system worse.
Generated multi-agent systems can also be costlier yet weaker than strong single-agent baselines [4];
a credible meta-agent must learn both when to assemble specialists and when to collapse to a
simpler system. We ask whether a meta-agent can adapt the composition, communication graph,
and compute allocation of a compound agentic system under regime shifts while empirically limiting
false promotion, architecture bloat, and operational risk.
We answer with CMAS (Figure 2). The inner loop executes a bounded agent architecture behind
a deterministic constraint and authorization layer. The outer loop observes the pre-decision regime
descriptor zk and a trace archive, proposes typed edits, evaluates candidates against the incumbent
by paired shadow replay, assigns counterfactual credit, and promotes only when the empirically
calibrated simultaneous score clears the margin. Promotion enters a simulated canary stage and is
reversible. Every method sees the same pre-decision descriptor zk, available before the decisions it
informs, while the latent simulator regime st is evaluator-only. Throughout, meta-agent denotes this
entire outer loop (proposer, compiler, evaluator, credit estimator, gate, and rollback controller), not
the proposal model alone. It is a hybrid: generation is LLM-based and archive-conditioned, while the
compiler, risk projection, authorization layer, gate, and rollback rules are deterministic and outside
anything the LLM can edit (Figure 2, locked box).
Three contributions follow. (1) We formulate agent-architecture search as sequential incumbent
replacement over typed architectures, with a structural distance (Eq. (1)) as switching cost, delayed
dependent utility (Eq. (2)), hard eligibility constraints, an explicit false-promotion target, and meta-
level generalization (Definition 1) as the scored quantity. (2) CMAS couples archive-conditioned
typed edits, paired candidate–incumbent replay under common random numbers, interventional
Shapley-style [34, 35] credit fed back to the proposer, and a conservative promotion-and-rollback
gate. The statistical ingredients are standard; the contribution is their integration. Proposition 1 states
only the idealized conditions under which such a gate would control family-wise false promotion, and
Section 4 scopes the departures. (3) METAINVEST-BENCH’s controlled track makes surrogate-oracle
regret, selection accuracy, and credit fidelity computable, and five reusable controls (clone-collapse,
shuffled-regime, gate-calibration, proposer-value, Goodhart-drift) measure whether adaptation hap-
pens for the intended reason, exposing reward gaming under a return-only evaluator (RQ7).

Departures. The proof is a standard simultaneous-concentration argument [26–28]. Eq. (6) breaks
three of its premises (Appendix A): overlapping windows break cross-update disjointness; the
variance-only penalty drops the Bernstein range correction and plugs in an estimated block count,
so no finite-sample theorem applies; and the deltas are winsorized. We therefore call the gate
empirically calibrated (Figure 4). A sealed-protocol rerun removes cross-update overlap and realizes
4.8% at the 5% target. It does not validate Proposition 1, which concerns replay improvement and
says nothing about a misspecified objective (RQ7). A real deployment would inflate the margin to
ϵ + ξk; every number here uses ξk = 0, and nothing was run live.
Scoping. Within an update, δ is split across the |Hk| candidates and budgeted at δ = 0.05 per
update, which is the denominator that the 3.4% rate and Figure 4 report against. Sequence-level
control would require δk = δ/K or the anytime schedule, neither activated for Track A. The implied
per-stream bound is vacuous, so RQ4’s per-stream rate is covered by no 5% guarantee (Appendix A).

METAINVEST-BENCH: benchmarking meta-level adaptation
No single backtest can establish meta-agent quality, so METAINVEST-BENCH triangulates controlled
capability (Track A), external validity (Track B), and adversarial governance (Track C) over a shared
worker library, risk layer, and budget accounting (Figure 6).
Track A: controlled regime environment. The primary controlled benchmark is a hidden-state factor
process, a Markov regime-switching model after [36]. The simulator specifies regime-dependent
worker competence, so a surrogate-optimal architecture is computable. The regime evolves, a signal
about next-period factors is emitted before the decision, and the return realizes afterward:

Experimental protocol
Baselines. Seven Track A methods: a strong single agent with self-consistency under the same
per-decision deployment budget cap; a fixed full committee; a rule-based regime router; a LinUCB ban-
dit [29] over a 15-architecture menu (partially logged; Table 6); AFlow-style workflow search [14, 19];
MaAS/AutoMaAS-style adaptive selection [20, 9], the strongest adaptive competitor and what “best
adaptive baseline” denotes throughout; and GEPA-style prompt-only optimization [1]. All are scored
against the same surrogate dynamic oracle and share data packets, zk, worker library, risk layer, model
pool, and equal search-inclusive budget caps (Shapley and paired-replay compute counted), with no in-
formation advantage. These are our reimplementations, not official releases, so every superiority claim
is scoped to common-budget reimplementations under a shared risk layer. Each method’s defining
search mechanism is retained; only what the shared environment forces is changed (retained/changed
detail in Appendix C). Routing the best baseline’s proposals through our gate still reaches 0.45 OOD
utility against CMAS’s 0.48 (Table 7), bounding the risk that a weak reimplementation drives the result.
Research questions. RQ1 regret and cost-adjusted utility; RQ2 interventional worker competence
vs. evaluator noise; RQ3 transfer to unseen regimes, libraries, and backbones (Definition 1); RQ4
gate calibration under serial dependence; RQ5 sparsity when extra agents add no value; RQ6
recovery from compromised workers; RQ7 drift under a misspecified (return-only) evaluator.
Metrics. Track A: normalized mean surrogate dynamic-oracle regret; selection accuracy; adaptation
delay (updates until post-shift regret returns within ϵdelay, distinct from the margin ϵ); credit rank
correlation. Track B: CEQ, Sharpe, MDD, CVaR, turnover, factor α. All tracks: cost, size, churn,
violations, rollback precision/recall, and false-promotion rate. That rate is the fraction of per-update
decisions promoting a candidate whose simulator-evaluated mean improvement is ≤ϵ (undefined
where a method never switches).
Statistics. Nested walk-forward with an untouched final test; search is confined to training/validation
intervals. The unit of replication is a stream (96 = 12 seeds × 8 schedules), with paired stream-level
block-bootstrap CIs as the primary inference and schedule-clustered hierarchical intervals as a
sensitivity analysis (Tables 1, 7–10). With only eight top-level schedules, clustered intervals are
read cautiously and significance rests on the paired stream-level difference under common random
numbers; clustering widens intervals without overturning the ablations (e.g. no-paired-evaluation
OOD utility [0.42, 0.48]; Table 10). Paired CMAS-vs-best-baseline differences in regret and OOD
utility exclude zero after Holm correction. The full pairwise matrix is omitted for space (seed
separation in Appendix G).
This simulator-evaluated mean improvement is not directly observed: the evaluator estimates it
from 50 Monte Carlo paths per candidate (SE < 0.004; ties within ±0.005 of ϵ labeled false
conservatively). Score form, block length, winsorization, ϵ, and pool size were frozen at validation
before the untouched final test (Tables 15–16; configuration Table 17).
