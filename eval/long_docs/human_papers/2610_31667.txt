Direct Pass@k Optimization for Thought Pruning: Balancing
Quality and Diversity in Test-Time Reasoning
Mahdi Salmani, Adel Javanmard, Devansh Gupta, and Meisam Razaviyayn
University of Southern California
{salmanis, ajavanma, guptadev, razaviya}@usc.edu
Abstract
Test-time scaling has emerged as a key inference strategy for reasoning models, where a
model generates a large number of candidate solutions in the hope of finding at least one correct
solution. Running all candidate solutions to completion can be computationally demanding, so
recent work prunes the candidate pool after generating only a short prefix, committing further
computation only to the surviving candidates. Existing pruning strategies divide broadly into
two categories: Quality-based methods assign a score to each partial solution and discard those
predicted to ultimately lead to an incorrect final solution, while diversity-based methods select
partial solutions that follow different reasoning trajectories and are therefore likely to cover
different final solutions. The tension between these two objectives is an instance of exploration
versus exploitation, yet little to no prior work on pruning exposes this trade-off directly, let
alone optimizes it. In this paper, we introduce a pruning procedure that addresses this trade-off
directly by optimizing pass@k over the selected solution pool. In particular, it chooses the pool to
maximize the probability that at least one of its partial solutions, when continued to completion,
yields a correct answer. Because this objective simultaneously rewards high-quality partial
solutions and penalizes redundancy among them, quality and diversity are naturally balanced by
the criterion itself rather than through separate ad-hoc design choices. On eight open-weight
reasoning models, our method outperforms existing quality-based and diversity-based pruning
strategies. The gains are particularly pronounced when the pruning procedure keeps a moderate
number of partial solutions for continued generation, highlighting the value of directly balancing
quality and diversity during pruning.

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in complex reasoning,
showcasing a strong aptitude for solving complex problems that require multi-step logic [15, 18,
21, 45, 52]. To further enhance these capabilities, test-time scaling has emerged as a prominent
strategy, where generating multiple candidate solutions for a single problem allows the model to
explore diverse reasoning strategies and achieve substantially better final performance [3, 35, 44, 48].
However, a significant challenge in scaling test-time compute is the prohibitive cost of generating
many full solutions, which we refer to as rollouts moving forward [23, 33]. Beyond generation, there
is also the cost of evaluating the finished rollouts in best-of-N methods, which score each rollout
with a reward model, and this is computationally demanding on a large pool of long reasoning traces
[11, 26, 36]. This computational bottleneck, both in generation and in evaluating all N rollouts in
best-of-N methods, necessitates test-time pruning of partial rollouts before they reach a final answer

One family of methods examines the confidence dynamics of the model during generation,
utilizing metrics such as logit entropy or confidence [17, 30, 43, 50]. The underlying intuition is that
if a model is overly confident at an intermediate decoding step, extending the reasoning process is a
waste of compute; conversely, if the model is entirely lacking in confidence, it is unlikely to reach
a correct solution regardless of the additional compute provided, allowing for early termination.
Another line of work leverages the model’s internal state, specifically the hidden states of its
transformer blocks, to identify which rollouts are worth continuing [1, 54]. By “probing” these
internal states, one can predict whether the model will ultimately arrive at a correct solution,
effectively framing the pruning problem as a classification task. This allows for the early discarding
of partial rollouts that are unlikely to yield a correct final answer. In parallel, a separate line of
research prioritizes representation exploration (RepExp) to select a subset of rollouts that cover as
many distinct reasoning strategies as possible [39]. By selecting partial rollouts whose corresponding
embeddings are dissimilar, RepExp aims to explore a diverse set of reasoning trajectories.
These approaches, however, sit at the two ends of a spectrum, and neither directly optimizes the
quantity we actually care about. The problem with probing methods is that the rollouts they rank
highest tend to be similar to one another. As a result, the probe may be confidently wrong about
all of the kept rollouts at once, and none of them reaches a correct answer. This is the problem
of pure exploitation (Fig. 2a). RepExp, on the other hand, finds diverse rollouts, but because it
ignores the quality of the rollouts, it can spend much of its budget on rollouts that are unlikely to
succeed (Fig. 2b). These limitations motivate selecting the rollouts by the metric we actually want
to optimize, choosing the set S of k rollouts that maximizes pass@k, that is, max|S|=k pass@k(S),
which we formalize in Eq. 3. Optimizing this objective directly keeps the selected rollouts diverse
enough to avoid shared failures while still favoring high-quality ones (Fig. 2c).
To this end, we propose PKTP (Pass@k Thought Pruning), a pruning framework that chooses
the kept rollouts by maximizing pass@k directly. The main difficulty is that, even if the rollouts are
sampled independently, their outcomes are not independent (conditioned on the partial observation
of the rollout).
Hence, pass@k depends on their joint distribution (i.e., rollouts with similar
partial rollouts tend to succeed or fail together). We model this joint distribution with a latent
Gaussian model, in which the correctness of each rollout is obtained by thresholding a Gaussian

Contributions.
First, we cast pruning as maximizing the pass@k of the kept set, and we model
how similar rollouts fail together. Second, we derive a closed-form, submodular approximation of
pass@k that links the probe and RepExp. Third, through experiments on eight reasoning models,
we show that PKTP improves over existing pruning methods, especially for moderate values of k.

our framework can be applied to other embeddings as well. Appendix F gives the details of the
representation we use.

Related work.
Existing pruning methods score each rollout on its own, using the model’s
confidence [13, 30], a reward model [36], or a probe on its hidden states [25, 54], or they select a
diverse set, as RepExp does [39]. Pass@k has also been used as a training objective for reasoning
models [8, 41], but not to decide which partial rollouts to keep. Outside of language models, choosing
a set so that at least one item succeeds has been studied in information retrieval and Bayesian
optimization [6, 34, 46]. Appendix G gives an extended discussion. In what follows, we discuss how
such an objective can be solved.
3
Method
There are some challenges in solving Eq. 3: First, we need to obtain the pass@k of a given set
of rollouts. We address this by learning the joint distribution of the rollouts’ outcomes under a
probabilistic model, from which the pass@k of any set can be estimated. Second, even with such
an estimate, finding the subset of k rollouts with the highest estimated pass@k is a mixed-integer
program. We can therefore view our framework as two components, learning the joint distribution of
the outcomes (Section 3.1), and solving this mixed-integer program at selection time (Section 3.2).

between the kth and (k + 1)th largest qualities exceeds a constant multiple of kρ2, PKTP keeps
exactly the k candidates with the largest qi, as Linear Probe does, and in the same order when this
holds at every budget up to k. At large k, when a few candidates of high quality stand out from
a bulk of lower quality, the objective of PKTP, averaged over the bulk qualities, reduces to the
RepExp objective plus a term for these candidates. Its minimizers therefore keep them and nearly
maximize the RepExp objective. Finally, the conditions of these two results do not conflict. We
construct a single model, with one quality distribution and one value of ρ, in which PKTP follows
Linear Probe at small k, while its averaged objective reduces to the RepExp objective as k grows.

Verification.
A completed rollout counts as correct when its final answer matches the reference
answer of the problem. We take that answer from the \boxed{} expression of the rollout and ask a
Qwen3-32B judge [49] whether it is equivalent to the reference answer. We use a judge rather than a
symbolic checker such as math-verify [24], because benchmarks like AMO-Bench have descriptive
answers that a symbolic checker cannot handle.
Evaluation.
Only a few hundred problems are available for each model, so a single split into

instead. For each model we partition its problems into eight folds, with each benchmark spread
evenly across the folds. Each fold serves once as the test set while the learned components are
trained on the other seven, so every problem is scored exactly once by a model that was not trained
on it. One such pass over all eight folds forms a repetition. Because the outcome of a repetition
depends on how the problems happen to be divided, we run 20 repetitions, each with a new random
partition obtained by shuffling the problems of every benchmark before assigning them to folds, and
with fresh random seeds for training. Every reported number is the average over the 20 repetitions,
and its standard error is computed across them. For RepExp we set the regularizer to τ = 1. For all
methods, the representation hi of a partial rollout is the EMA of its hidden states with rate 10−3.
