A B S T R A C T
In the post-API landscape, social-media profiling has become inexpensive and automated: Large
Language Models (LLMs) can extract structured information from unstructured content and deduce
attributes users never disclosed. A person’s true exposure is not what they post but what can be
inferred from it. As these practices become routine, exposure to social engineering attacks becomes
an operational certainty, demanding a shared framework for assessment and mitigation.
We make this exposure measurable by quantifying how accurately a target can be profiled, and
how far that profile enables social engineering attacks. Because no existing model fully spans this
continuum, we unify Social Media Intelligence (SOCMINT) and Cyber Human Intelligence (Cyber-
HUMINT), two individual-focused disciplines that evolved separately, into a single doctrine-grounded
taxonomy of 38 capabilities across the Cyber Threat Intelligence lifecycle, where user profiling bridges
passive collection and active engagement. Domain experts validated the taxonomy (Fleiss’ 𝜅= 0.92).
We operationalize its core path (extraction, inference, and susceptibility assessment) as an
LLM-augmented multi-agent pipeline, evaluated on real participants’ public data. Susceptibility
is assessed indirectly: the pipeline predicts how each target would rank personalized engagement
options, matching their actual choices significantly above chance. This ranking capacity captures the
personalization on which tailored attacks depend. The underlying profiles are accurate: extraction
reaches 𝐹1 = 97.3%, and inferred attributes score 4.73∕5, both far above rule-based and NER-
based baselines at negligible cost. The framework augments security analysts’ tradecraft rather than
replacing it and enables defenders to gauge their own exposure before an adversary does.
1. Introduction
Social media has transformed how people communicate
and share information. It now has about 5.66 billion active
users (over 68% of the global population), grows at 4.1% per
year, and accounts for more than 2 hours of daily use across
an average of 7 platforms [1, 2]. These public footprints
expose users to security risks that extend beyond what they
post. Beneath visible content lies an inferred layer (organi-
zational access, behavioral traits, communication style) that
users never disclose but that can still be reconstructed from
sparse public signals [3]. This inferred layer is part of a
person’s true exposure, yet current practice neither observes
nor measures it.
The dual-use nature of this environment is evident in
current tradecraft. MITRE ATT&CK documents systematic
reconnaissance through publicly available social data [4].
APT campaigns such as IN(TER)CEPTION [5], Dream
Job [6], EXOTIC LILY [7], and Contagious Interview [8]
begin with counterfeit personas, cloned profiles, and social
engineering on LinkedIn and other platforms before any
technical intrusion. This trend is accelerating. IBM X-Force
reports an 84% increase in phishing campaigns delivering
infostealers identified through social media monitoring [9].
CrowdStrike reports faster adversary breakout times and
malware-free techniques that are often first observable in
social media discourse [10].
The Cambridge Analytica scandal [11] marked the start
of the post-API era. Platforms restricted their APIs, while

GDPR and the Digital Services Act constrained lawful
collection [12, 13]. As a result, profiles can now be built
only by extracting data from unstructured, platform-specific
sources, often through scraping that raises Terms-of-Service
and privacy concerns [14]. Platforms also enforce defenses
through access policies and data-protection measures. Yet
these controls are increasingly fragile. Large Language
Models (LLMs) can extract structured information from
unstructured sources at scale and infer latent attributes that
platforms never explicitly exposed.
This attack surface is hard to measure because most
steps that turn observable data into exploitable intelligence
happen out of sight, before any technical intrusion. These
invisible stages can be reconstructed only by mirroring the
adversary through legitimate disciplines built on the same
capabilities. SOCMINT (Social Media Intelligence) [15]
supports passive, observation-driven collection and analy-
sis of social media data. Cyber-HUMINT relies on active
engagement, with operators interacting with targets to elicit
what observation cannot reveal. Both are already opera-
tional, from government SOCMINT programs that moni-
tor extremist recruitment and foreign influence to Cyber-
HUMINT operations that infiltrate ransomware groups and
darknet marketplaces [16–19].
Both disciplines focus on the same individuals and differ
mainly in depth of engagement, yet they have evolved as
separate workflows. Existing models cover only parts of
this path. Kill-chain and ATT&CK-style taxonomies enu-
merate adversary phases [4, 20], and OSINT frameworks
structure passive collection [21]. However, none formalizes
the passive-to-active continuum at the level of operational

1.1. Major contributions of this work
lThis work makes that exposure measurable so that in-
dividuals and organizations can assess their own risk before
an adversary does. We unify the observation-to-engagement
continuum into a single capability framework and opera-
tionalize its core as an LLM-augmented system, evaluated
with real users (Fig. 1). To our knowledge, this is the first
study that measures not only how accurately such profiles
can be built, but also their impact on users, specifically how
far they translate into susceptibility to social engineering.
Our contributions are as follows:
1. A unified capability framework (Sec. 4) that or-
ganizes 38 capabilities across the five phases of the
Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) lifecycle, with profil-
ing as the transition layer between passive collection
and active engagement. We derive it from established
intelligence doctrine and validate it via expert elicita-
tion (Sec. 6.1; content validity, average I-CVI = 0.90;
inter-rater agreement, Fleiss’ 𝜅= 0.92). No single
source we surveyed spans more than ∼68% of these
capabilities.
2. An LLM-augmented multi-agent system (Sec. 5)
that operationalizes the framework’s core path, in-
cluding cross-platform extraction, inference of attack-
relevant latent attributes, and susceptibility assess-
ment. In contrast to prior isolated, single-purpose
tools, it orchestrates agents across platforms into one
pipeline yielding traceable profiles, as analyst support
rather than a replacement for established tooling. The
design is informed by an analysis of the defenses
deployed by 5 major social-media platforms.
3. A human-subjects study (Sec. 6.2) measuring the
core path in practice: extraction and inference ac-
curacy on participants’ own data, and the extent to
which the resulting profiles anticipate the personal-
ized engagement a social-engineering attack would
exploit, marking the point where exposure becomes
operational. We compare results against rule- and
NER-based baselines (Sec. 7.1).
2. Background and Related Works
Measuring exposure along the observation-to-engage-
ment continuum requires grounding it in existing work and
its gaps, and analyzing the dual-use practice around it (ad-
versarial exploitation and the defensive disciplines that mir-
ror it), the tools that operationalize both, and the platform
constraints that bound them.
2.1. Social-Media Profiling and Intelligence
Profiling individuals from social media spans many re-
search strands that have advanced largely in isolation, includ-
ing cybersecurity, LLM-based attribute inference, extraction
pipelines, and user modeling.
In cybersecurity, a substantial body of work targets
cyber threat intelligence (CTI). Zhao et al. [22] automati-
cally extract and categorize CTI from social-media streams,
and Guo et al. [23] fuse threat signals from heterogeneous
sources into structured intelligence. However, both focus
on threat indicators and reports rather than the individu-
als behind them. From a framework perspective, Pastor-
Galindo et al. [21] survey the broader OSINT landscape with
a section on social media, while Teti [24] conceptualizes the
convergence of passive observation and active engagement
through a data-capture platform, but without a technical im-
plementation. On the offensive side, Heiding et al. [25] auto-
mate the full spear-phishing pipeline (reconnaissance, target
profiling, lure generation) with click-through rates matching
human experts. Focusing on lure generation, Hazell [26]
and Saha Roy et al. [27] produce convincing personalized
phishing at scale and marginal cost, bypassing commercial
guardrails. Yet the profile remains an opaque intermediate
artifact, validated only by attack success. A parallel literature
trains LLM and transformer classifiers to detect threats,
phishing, and abusive content, which lies outside our scope.
For example, San Biagio et al. [28, 29] combine ML and
NLP to identify threats across the surface web, social media,
and darknets at up to 95% accuracy, and Mishra et al. [30]
flag smishing from message content and URL behavior.
One line of work uses LLMs to infer attributes users
never disclosed. Alizadeh et al. [31] show that web-browsing
LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-o3, Llama-3-8B-Web) infer demo-
graphic attributes from X/Twitter usernames, drawing on
bios and follow networks but still guessing, with systematic
bias, from minimal signals. Staab et al. [3] show that LLMs
leak sensitive attributes by inference on real Reddit profiles,
and through chatbots that elicit additional cues, exposing
the limits of current mitigations. Jiang and Ferrara [32]
and Yu [33] combine LLM-encoded text with social-graph
structure to infer partisanship and political orientation from
limited footprints. These works infer specific traits (demo-
graphics, political orientation) in isolation, not within an
operational profiling process spanning extraction to down-
stream engagement.
More broadly, in extraction pipelines, Arjun et al. [34]
propose PeopleXploit, a hybrid OSINT stack orchestrating
web tools for footprint profiling, yet without formal identity
resolution or systematic evaluation. Wang et al. [35] generate

work addresses both gaps: it operationalizes the observation-

human-subjects study (Sec. 6.2).
2.2. Adversarial Use in Practice
Attack campaigns confirm that social media has become
an instrument of collection and engagement for offensive
operations. Across eight documented campaigns (Tab. 1),
attackers follow recurring patterns of social profiling and
human engagement, varying in target and impact. Conta-
gious Interview [8] and Lazarus [5, 6] pose as recruiters
on LinkedIn and GitHub, luring software developers and
defense staff into fake hiring processes that deliver malware.
Kimsuky [42, 47] and APT42 [43] instead pose as fabricated
journalists and researchers, building trust with academics,
policy experts, and political figures before deploying mal-
ware or harvesting credentials. EXOTIC LILY [7, 41] brokers
initial access, building employee personas from LinkedIn
and Crunchbase to plant ransomware; Scattered Spider [44]
and UNC6040 [45] use voice phishing against IT help desks
and cloud administrators to hijack accounts and exfiltrate
data. Anonymous [46] combines OSINT and social engineer-
ing to dox roughly 1,000 members of extremist groups for
hacktivism purposes.
Yet this groundwork is flattened in how intrusions are
modeled: the kill chain [20] runs from reconnaissance to
objectives, capturing the technical attack but compressing
target profiling into a single upstream step rather than a
structured phase. This pre-intrusion phase is where exposure
is built and where defense must act first.
To recover what the kill chain omits, we turn to defend-
ers’ intelligence disciplines, which already treat collection,
profiling, and engagement as structured capabilities. The
framework developed in this work builds on them to make
this phase visible.
2.3. SOCMINT and Cyber-HUMINT definitions
Neither social media intelligence discipline has a single
universally accepted definition. However, a shared opera-
tional understanding is necessary to construct a coherent
capability framework spanning both domains.
Social Media Intelligence (SOCMINT) remains a rela-
tively young field, and its definition continues to evolve with
the platforms it studies. For the purposes of this work, we
adopt the following formulation:

SOCMINT refers to intelligence derived from the col-
lection and analysis of social media data, typically through
passive, observation-driven methods, in compliance with
privacy and transparency constraints. It provides near real-
time situational awareness by enabling the collection and
clustering of content that clarifies unfolding events, accel-
erates emerging threats identification, offers insight into
groups, and may support the identification of criminal intent.
This definition draws on established sources, each em-
phasizing a different facet. The National Policing Insti-
tute focuses on social platforms as web-based systems for
communication within virtual communities [48]; Omand
et al. [15] place SOCMINT within the intelligence spec-
trum, alongside IMINT, HUMINT, and SIGINT; and the
Health Sector Cybersecurity Coordination Center (HC3)
[49] stresses its dual-use nature, emphasizing metadata
(birth date, geolocation, social ties, workplace) alongside
user-published content.
By contrast, HUMINT principles are more stable across
doctrinal sources. We therefore adopt the following opera-
tional definition:
Cyber-HUMINT refers to intelligence derived from in-
formation collected by human operators and primarily pro-
vided by human sources within digital contexts. It transforms
passive surveillance into active collection and is achieved
through observing or directly communicating with people,
according to management and governance practices specific
to the source type.
NATO AJP-2, UK Joint Doctrine, and INCIBE stress
operator training and HUMINT modalities, including de-
briefing, source handling, questioning, interrogation, and
liaison [50, 51]; these sources mark the boundary between
passively collected surveillance outputs and their active
conversion into intelligence. The Italian Security Intelli-
gence System highlights the need to adapt procedures to
each source [52], while the CIA adds a governance layer
through formal authorization and cost–risk assessment [53].
U.S. Army doctrine specifies operational-level procedures
for collector employment and elicitation from sources [54].
SOCMINT and Cyber-HUMINT are complementary yet
distinct: the former observes, the latter engages. User profil-
ing bridges them, turning passively collected social media
data into a structured profile that can support engagement.
Yet prior work models this continuum only conceptually,
without a formal capability model, leaving the continuum
unmeasured. Providing such a model is therefore the objec-
tive of Sec. 4.
2.4. Tools for Social Media Analysis
The security community has developed a wide range of
tools that address specific aspects of identity correlation and
attribute extraction [21].
Enumeration tools verify whether a given identifier ex-
ists across multiple platforms. Sherlock [55] relies on HTTP
status codes, but produces many false positives. WhatsMy-
Name [56] improves precision through a curated dataset of
platform-specific response patterns, at the cost of continu-
ous signature maintenance. Maigret [57] combines both ap-
proaches, extracting basic profile attributes (name, location,
bio) via DOM parsing. Holehe [58] leverages password-
recovery endpoints to detect account registrations without
triggering user notifications on supported platforms.
Aggregation frameworks integrate multiple enumer-
ation modules and data sources into unified workflows,
via modular architectures (SpiderFoot [59]) or graph-based
visual analytics (Maltego [60]). These require significant
analyst expertise in setup and result interpretation, making
them power-user tools rather than turnkey solutions.
Commercial platforms leverage proprietary data access
and advanced analytics, extending coverage beyond publicly
scrapable information. Pipl [61] aggregates records from
people-search databases, public records, and social media
APIs to build comprehensive identity profiles. PimEyes [62]
enables image-based identity search via facial recognition.
Existing tools stop short of profiling itself: they generate
false positives, require frequent maintenance as platforms
evolve, and leave analysts to manually fuse fragmented data,
infer unstated attributes, and assess relevance. Determinis-
tic methods, such as regular expressions and named-entity
recognition, handle these tasks poorly. The LLM-augmented
architecture proposed in this work addresses this gap by
adding semantic parsing and latent attribute inference to the
pipeline, generating structured profiles while keeping human
judgment central.
2.5. Security Constraints of Social Platforms
Platform APIs have evolved from general-purpose data
extraction channels into tightly controlled interfaces de-
signed to reduce privacy leakage and automated abuse.
This platform-governed access model also affects research
reproducibility [63]. We identify four layers of constraints
that shape data accessibility: these constraints define the
operational boundaries within which both legitimate and
adversarial data collection must occur. This section exam-
ines how major platforms implement them, providing an
overview in Tab. 2.
User-controlled visibility. Privacy settings place expo-
sure under user control: content not shared stays inaccessible
regardless of API capability. Yet Instagram, X, and TikTok
accounts are public by default [64–66]. Opting into private
mode restricts posts and content to approved followers,
though TikTok keeps nickname, username, and profile photo
public regardless and defaults under-16 accounts to private.
LinkedIn instead grades visibility by connection degree – full
for 1st-degree, progressively reduced for 2nd-, 3rd-degree,
and out-of-network users [67]. Facebook exposes granular
per-element controls with awareness tools; X additionally
allows disabling lookup by email or phone.

Platform-controlled authorization. Retrievable data is
the intersection of granted scopes, access tier, and endpoint
policy: OAuth tokens are bound to the authenticated user,
so endpoints return only self-data or aggregated signals.
Higher-risk scopes require platform approval and explicit
user consent. Together, these constraints block individual
enumeration and large-scale social-graph reconstruction.
Facebook’s Graph API scopes tokens as user (individual
data, with consent), app (no user data), or page (management
and insights) [68]; since v2.0 (2014), /me/friends returns
only friends who also authorized the app, ending bulk rela-
tionship harvesting [69], and Dev-to-Live transition requires
an App Review tightened after Cambridge Analytica [70].
LinkedIn is approval-gated by business line: its only self-
serve API returns the authenticating user’s own profile [71],
while Marketing and Talent APIs are role-bound to adver-
tiser or organization-admin accounts and policy-restricted
from data export or third-party combination [72].
Instagram retired its Basic Display API in December
2024; third-party access now requires the Graph API, limited
to Business and Creator accounts (presence management,
analytics, hashtag search), with endpoints gated by granular
permissions and App Review [73].
TikTok offers no general public-data API: its Research
API admits only vetted academics in the US and EEA (in-
dependence, funding disclosure, ethical review), with a re-
stricted Virtual Compute Environment for non-profits; Busi-
ness API exposes no raw public data [74].
X tiers access by paid plan (Free, Basic, Pro) with rising
quotas and features such as filtered streams and full-archive
search; it separates App-Only tokens (public data) from User
Context authentication (private or on-behalf access) [75, 76].
Abuse resilience. Platforms enforce access dynamically
through rate limits, behavioral fingerprinting, bot detection,
and CAPTCHAs, and resist scraping through DOM random-
ization, virtualization, and lazy loading. They adapt in real
time to throttle, challenge, or ban suspicious activity.
Facebook runs a dedicated External Data Misuse team
with rate and data limits plus behavioral analysis [77]. Our
analysis (Sec. 5) identifies further client-side defenses, in-
cluding randomized CSS class names (which force reliance
on stable ARIA attributes), lazy loading with placeholders,
text fragmented across <span> tags, and DOM virtualization
that drops off-viewport posts while scrolling. Together, these
mechanisms defeat reliable single-pass bulk extraction. In-
stagram inherits Meta’s centralized detection and rate lim-
iting, restricting accounts that scrape [78]. LinkedIn centers
its Trust ML stack on CASAL (Centralized Abuse Scoring
As a Library), which scores abuse (fake accounts, account
takeover, moderation, scraping) via retrained models [79].
X rate-limits per application and user, locks accounts
behind anti-spam challenges on suspicion, and permanently
suspends non-API automation or rate-limit bypass [80, 81].
TikTok combines CAPTCHAs, rate limiting, and behavioral
analysis of devices, networks, and interaction patterns, plus
undisclosed controls [82].
Terms of Service. Platform Terms uniformly prohibit
scraping, fake accounts, and circumvention of technical con-
trols, exposing violators to account termination and legal
action [83–86]. Yet courts have repeatedly held that scraping
public, logged-out data does not breach these Terms, since
non-authenticated users never assented to them and data
lack authentication barriers: hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn (9th Cir.
2022) [87], Meta v. Bright Data [88], X Corp. v. Bright Data
(N.D. Cal. 2024) [89]. The European Commission’s 2025
preliminary finding also faults TikTok for over-restricting
researcher access under the Digital Services Act [90].
Across the five platforms, defensive postures converge
(rate limiting, behavioral analysis, ToS restrictions), but API
availability diverges. This asymmetry between platform-
controlled access and research needs motivates alternative
collection. Accordingly, our study collected only data par-
ticipants had made publicly visible, accessed through the
authenticated sessions that platform controls require.
