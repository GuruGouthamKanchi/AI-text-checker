Abstract
Multilingual pipelines such as FineWeb2 apply
one recipe across a thousand languages, leaving
the failure modes of individual languages unan-
ticipated. We use an LLM once, as a corpus
debugger rather than a classifier: a tool-using
agent explores the corpus through a read-only,
budgeted API and compiles each finding into an
executable test, which a native speaker then val-
idates. On the 26.5M-document Slovak portion
of FineWeb2, three runs costing $2.21–$2.84
each found seven findings over four phenom-
ena. The largest is a machine-generated con-
tent farm of 1,408,437 documents (5.32%) that
passed the pipeline’s filters, 86.6% of it passing
even stricter thresholds. Blinded labels sup-
port 6 of the 7 findings (defect rates .53–1.00
against .08 in a random control) and do not
support one. The compiled suite scans the full
corpus in 0.7 s of wall-clock time without a
GPU, against $12k–$90k per LLM classifica-
tion pass. The best static sampling baseline
validates more selectors than any agent run, at
under half the cost. The agent contributes ex-
act prevalence, a precision estimate checked
against sampled documents, an account of why
the pipeline missed each slice, and a reusable
test. We release the framework, Slovak test
suite, validation data and agent trajectories.

Introduction
Pretraining corpora are now built by pipelines
rather than by hand. FineWeb2 (Penedo et al.,
2025) derives more than a thousand language-
specific corpora from Common Crawl with one
configurable recipe. That uniformity is what makes
such resources feasible at all. One pipeline, en-
gineered once, yields data for languages whose
communities could never afford a bespoke crawl.
It is also a structural blind spot, as the FineWeb2
authors say themselves. Their language identifier
confuses closely related languages, and they could
not test each language individually, so they ask
users to review the filtering for the languages they
care about. Their one mechanism against cousin-
language contamination, a high-affinity wordlist
filter, is applied only to languages whose measured
contamination exceeded 10%, and not to a mid-
resource language like Slovak with a large, closely
related neighbour.
Reviewing a pipeline for one language is exactly
the work that does not scale. Its established form
is the manual audit (Kreutzer et al., 2022; Caswell
et al., 2020), which is invaluable, and recent single-
language audits still pay off (Signoroni and Rychlý,
2026; Tatariya et al., 2026). But an audit is a one-
shot artefact. It describes a corpus at one point in
time, does not compose into machinery that can
be re-run on the next crawl, and consumes expert
attention linearly. The alternative, running an LLM
over the data as a classifier, turns a curation prob-
lem into an inference bill and still explains nothing.
We explore a third option and use the LLM once,
as a debugger. Our auditor is a tool-using agent
that sees the corpus only through a small read-only
API for aggregate SQL, conditional sampling, text
search, sentence-level language identification, and
defining and evaluating candidate detectors. It is
asked, in deliberately generic terms, to find system-
atic defects and substantiate them. Its prompt con-
tains no taxonomy of expected problems, names no
defect or neighbouring language, and describes the
pipeline only by its steps (language identification,
quality heuristics, deduplication). Each output is a
finding with a description, a cheap executable se-
lector, a prevalence measured on the whole corpus,
and a precision for human validation to establish.
The result is pytest for web corpora: a community
contributes tests, and any future crawl or pipeline
revision is checked against them for the cost of a
scan over metadata the pipeline already computed.

We frame an LLM, used once, as a corpus debug-

data-selection policy. We build a reusable audit en-

under a fixed inspection budget and a prompt that
names neither a defect nor a neighbouring language.
Around it sits a three-stage workflow (discovery by
the agent, validation by a native speaker, deploy-
ment by a test runner with no model) in which
validation can fail a finding. It failed one of our
seven findings, which comes out at a .07 defect rate
and ships flagged. Our audit of Slovak FineWeb2
yields seven findings over four phenomena with ex-
act corpus-wide prevalences and blinded human
precision (Table 2), among them a 5.32% con-
tent farm that the pipeline’s own language, du-
plication and auxiliary-LID signals do not filter,
and a language_score invariant that fails in 11
of 12 FineWeb2 configurations. A per-run budget-
matched comparison against four static selection
strategies shows what the agent does and does not
buy. It validates 2–3 selectors per run against 2–4
for the baselines, so the best baseline finds more
than any agent run, and each phenomenon it val-
idated also lies within reach of a static strategy,
although for the adult spam we analysed that strat-
egy’s sample without running the model on it (§5).
Its contribution is the measurement, the characteri-
sation and the test. Finally, we release the Slovak
regression-test suite, the patcher that turns it into a
drop list, and the agent trajectories (Table 1). Every
defect we report is a residual of an already heavily
filtered corpus, and several are visible only because
FineWeb2 publishes rich metadata.
We ask four research questions.
RQ1 Can an LLM agent with read-only tools
discover systematic defects that a native
speaker confirms?
RQ2 Can these defects be compiled into cheap
executable tests?
RQ3 Do the agent’s findings lie outside the slices

Related Work
Web corpora and their audits.
FineWeb2
(Penedo et al., 2025) extends the FineWeb recipe
(Penedo et al., 2024) to over a thousand lan-
guages using GlotLID (Kargaran et al., 2023)
with a per-language confidence threshold, Gopher-
and FineWeb-style quality filters, and global per-
language MinHash deduplication with rehydration.
Earlier multilingual web corpora (Ortiz Suárez
et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2021; Conneau et al.,
2020; Kudugunta et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024;
Burchell et al., 2025) share its architecture and its
exposure to the LID error that Caswell et al. (2020)
and Burchell et al. (2023) document. Audits of
such corpora are an established genre, covering
205 corpora by hand (Kreutzer et al., 2022), one
English corpus in depth (Dodge et al., 2021), non-
English Wikipedia (Tatariya et al., 2026) and Lom-
bard (Signoroni and Rychlý, 2026). The WaC com-
munity studies the same failure surface from the
pipeline side (Jakubíˇcek et al., 2020; Barbaresi and
Lejeune, 2020; Laippala et al., 2020). We are down-
stream of these, asking how an audit can be made
repeatable and executable. For Slovak, cleaning
practice is established (Pikuliak et al., 2022) and
Czech/Slovak is a known hard case for language
ID (Tichý and Libovický, 2026). Sub-percent con-
tamination still means hundreds of millions of to-
kens (Blevins and Zettlemoyer, 2022), though we
promise no downstream gains (Artetxe et al., 2022).
Tests over data, and agents over data.
Check-
List (Ribeiro et al., 2020) argued that behavioural
tests expose model failures that aggregate accuracy
hides. We make that argument for corpora, and
borrow the pattern of declarative data-quality asser-
tions run on every build (Schelter et al., 2018), ex-
cept that here the assertions must come from people
who know the language. LLM agents have been ap-
plied to corpus-linguistic discovery (Yu et al., 2026)
and to the curation loop, where Kang et al. (2026)
find that coding agents optimise data-selection poli-
cies but tend to tune variants of a known policy.
Ours optimises no downstream metric and outputs
a test suite instead of a policy. We do not claim
that LLMs are good language classifiers. The agent
writes the tests that label the corpus and never la-
bels it itself.

The Audit Environment
Corpus view.
The adapter exposes a corpus as
two DuckDB relations over Parquet. corpus_meta
has one row per document without text and
serves exact corpus-wide prevalence, while corpus
is a deterministic 1-in-13 subsample with text
(2,034,894 documents) for whenever reading is
needed. That split keeps the environment inter-
active, because metadata aggregates over 26.5M
rows return in under a second while scanning
100 GB of text does not.
The columns are
the corpus’s own metadata (URL, crawl, date,
the pipeline’s language and language_score,
minhash_cluster_size, the profanity-list count
n_bad_words, the GlotLID top_langs distribu-
tion, and the fastText, langdetect and Lingua predic-
tions added by the ivykopal/fineweb2-slovak
release), plus language-neutral derived columns
(domain, size counts).
Nothing in the schema
names a language pair or a failure mode.
Tools.
The agent acts through eleven tools, each
listed with its budget cost in Appendix A. They
cover aggregation, sampling and single-document
inspection, text search, host and crawl statistics,
sentence-level language identification, two tools
that measure and sample what a candidate rule
selects, and save_finding. Aggregation is free,
whereas every document read costs one inspection
from a fixed budget, charged once per distinct id.
Every call is logged to a JSONL trajectory, so an
investigation can be replayed. The free aggrega-
tion tool did not stop a query from returning doc-
ument text, for example substr(text, 1, 120)
grouped and counted, so text could in principle
be read without spending budget. We checked ev-
ery logged aggregate call. In the three protocol
runs none returned document text: the queries that
touch text return counts only. The fourth run in
Appendix K returned text fragments in three calls,
the most frequent 100- and 120-character document
prefixes and extracted e-mail strings, and its inspec-
tion count therefore understates what it read. Later
versions of the environment refuse such queries.

The agent runs
one long trajectory in which it orients on statistics,
forms a hypothesis, retrieves evidence and counter-
evidence, refines or rejects it, measures prevalence,
synthesises a selector, checks its precision and
records a finding. Its system prompt (Appendix B)
states the tools, the finding schema and the epis-

Discovery, validation, deployment.
The work-
flow has three stages with different actors and costs
(Figure 1). Discovery is where the agent works,
and it produces candidates. In validation a na-
tive speaker labels a blinded sample of each candi-
date’s documents, after which a model tabulates
that evidence and proposes a verdict per candi-
date group that the annotator adopts or not (§4).
Deployment runs the resulting tests without any
model. Each test is a YAML file carrying its se-
lector, an assertion about future corpora and its
evidence (Appendix L), and a status: accepted
when the blinded labels support it and the adopted
verdict approves it, candidate otherwise. A suite
is a directory of such files, executable against any
corpus implementing the adapter interface.

and runner, the prompt, the suite, and every input
to the numbers in this paper, from trajectories to
verdicts. The paper’s tables are generated from
these artefacts by script.

Experimental Setup
Data.
We audit the Slovak portion of FineWeb2
(slk_Latn) as redistributed with extra LID meta-
data by ivykopal/fineweb2-slovak.
It holds
26,470,482 documents from 351,844 hosts and 96
Common Crawl snapshots (2013–2024), 438 words
on average. No document scores below 0.667, the
language_score threshold FineWeb2 publishes
for slk_Latn. The auxiliary identifiers disagree
with the pipeline label on 3.12% of documents.
Models and settings.
All three agent runs and all
four baselines use claude-sonnet-5 through the
Claude Code CLI (version 2.1.231) at its default
sampling settings, and we set no temperature. Each
agent run is capped at 300 document inspections
and 250 turns, each baseline at 6 turns. We report
inspections, tokens and USD for every run and
baseline. A fourth claude-opus-5 run, outside
this protocol, is reported in Appendix K.
Baselines.
Each baseline is a document-selection
strategy that draws the same 300 documents the
agent could have inspected and shows them to the
same model, without tools or exploration. The
model sees each document as its metadata and first
400 characters, whereas the agent can read whole
documents, and the baseline instructions drop the
requirement that a finding be quantified and have
no prevalence field. We then measure the preva-
lence of each proposed selector ourselves, as for the
agent. RANDOM samples uniformly. LOWCONF
takes language_score < 0.8, which is 0.49% of
the corpus, LID-DISAGREE any auxiliary identi-
fier disagreeing with the pipeline label (3.12%),
and HIGHDUP minhash_cluster_size ≥100
(0.23%). The comparison changes two things at
once. The agent chooses what to read, and it also
has tools the baselines lack (aggregate queries,
prevalence measurement, targeted sampling, de-
tector evaluation). It therefore measures the whole
agentic setup rather than adaptivity alone. For a
fifth, profanity-ranked strategy we analyse only the
sample it would draw, the 300 text-subsample doc-
uments with the most profanity-list matches, and
we did not run the model on it.
Human validation.
We drew 358 documents for
blinded annotation by a native speaker: 25 for
each of the 7 agent findings, 11–12 for each of
12 sampled baseline candidates, and 40 uniform
controls, excluding every document any agent run
inspected, shuffled and shown without provenance,
URL or metadata. The exclusion list was built
from truncated tool logs, so one drawn document (a
code-switched Czech/Slovak item) had in fact been
shown to an agent run by evaluate_detector. It
received no label, so no reported number changes.
Each document gets a language label and a use-
fulness label on the scales in Appendix M. A doc-
ument is a defect if its language label is cs, mix,
other or its usefulness label is junk. sk, sk-pred,
good and noisy are not defects. Slice precision is
the human-judged defect rate and the control gives
the corpus base rate. This estimates precision, how
often a selected document is a defect, not recall,
how much of a phenomenon a selector catches.
Of the 358 documents, 255 carry a label, none of
them “uncertain” (labelled/drawn by source: agent
slices 122/175, LOWCONF 26/36, LID-DISAGREE
30/36, HIGHDUP 28/36, RANDOM 24/35, control
25/40). The shortfall is not a per-selector choice:
labelling ran in two passes with a clarified junk
criterion between them, and the documents neither
pass reached were dropped (Appendix M). Per-
finding counts are in Table 2.

selectors counted per baseline, 21 distinct across
baselines, since RANDOM and LOWCONF share
two. They were clustered mechanically into 11

Results
RQ1. The agent finds systematic defects that a
speaker confirms.
Across three runs the agent
used 197, 180 and 173 of its 300 inspections and
recorded 2, 4 and 3 findings. Figure 2 follows
run 1 from its first free aggregate query to the
saved content-farm finding at logged event 49, by
which point it had spent 162 inspections. Run 1
found the content farm and an adult keyword farm.
Run 2 found the farm, Czech text labelled Slo-
vak, an adult doorway network and a machine-
translated farm on slo.* hosts. Run 3 found the
farm, an adult word-salad farm and Czech/Slovak
code-switched documents. Deduplicating selectors
leaves seven, which collapse by document over-
lap and nesting into four phenomena (Table 2):
content farms on free-hosting subdomains (G01),
Czech or code-switched documents labelled Slo-
vak (G02), keyword-stuffed adult spam (G04) and
the slo.* farm (G05). The largest is a content
farm on *.firebaseapp.com and *.web.app with
1,408,437 documents (5.32%) over 2,851 hosts.
The farm is nearly absent before 2022, rises sharply
from late 2022, peaks at 38.7% of the 2023-06
crawl and is still present in the last crawl, while
the adult spam peaks in 2020 and then fades (Fig-
ure 3). At 1,011 words on average against a corpus
mean of 438 it contributes 12.3% of all running
words. All three runs found it independently, and
we re-derived every statistic ourselves. The agent
also volunteers evidence against its own hypotheses
(Appendix J).
Blinded human labels (Figure 4) support 6 of
the 7 findings at .53–1.00, against .08 in the 25-
document control (interval to .25), so 3 of the four
phenomena are supported. The labels do not sup-
port the slo.* farm, which comes out at .07, and
it ships with status candidate (§4).
RQ2. The compiled tests are cheap.
All seven
selectors are metadata-only, so the suite is exact
over the full corpus. It runs over all 26,470,482
documents in 0.7 s of wall-clock time on one ma-
chine (about 13 s of CPU time across 20 cores),
without GPU or LLM calls. Classifying every doc-
ument with the same model instead costs $3.42 per
thousand documents, measured on 400-character
snippets batched twenty per call. That is $90,456
per pass, or $12,309 at raw token prices without
harness overhead, and it recurs every crawl. The
comparison is not like for like. The tests are cheap
because they reuse columns the pipeline and the re-
distributor already computed in corpus-wide passes
(language identification, auxiliary identifiers, pro-
fanity counts, MinHash clusters), and a selector
