Abstract
Tool-using language model agents rely on environmental facts to decide whether an action is permitted.
When a tool response omits a safety-relevant fact, an agent may carry out a prohibited action even though
the user request is benign and every returned statement is true. We introduce EpiSEM-Bench, an executable
benchmark for evaluating unsafe execution, safety boundaries, and evidence recovery in LLM agents under
missing evidence. It contains 300 matched unsafe/safe task pairs across six domains. By systematically
suppressing critical facts, the benchmark measures unsafe execution under critical evidence omission and
determines the minimum observed failure cardinality, including failures with full evidence and cases with
no observed failure after exhaustive testing. An independent audit tool tests whether agents seek missing
facts and then withhold prohibited actions while completing permissible tasks. The benchmark reveals
substantial variation across the ten models in omission robustness, evidence acquisition, and appropriate
action. Resistance to missing evidence does not necessarily translate into useful recovery. Recovery access
reduces unsafe execution for all ten models, but agents differ in whether they actively obtain the missing
facts and use them to act appropriately in both worlds.
1. Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents use tools to read documents, inspect resources, and execute operations
on a user’s behalf (Yao et al., 2022, 2024, Lu et al., 2025). Before sending a payment, sharing a file, or
deleting a resource, the agent needs to establish that the operation is permitted in the current environment.
This decision depends on facts returned by its tools, such as an authorization limit, a recipient’s access rights,
or the dependencies of a running service. A missing fact can change the agent’s decision even when the tool
response contains no false statement.

Existing benchmarks evaluate tool-use capability, action safety, and robustness to adversarial observations
(Zhang et al., 2024, Debenedetti et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2026b). AgentDojo evaluates task utility and
security using executable checks (Debenedetti et al., 2024). Missing evidence poses a different intervention
that requires no attacker instruction, and the remaining observations can all be true. An evaluation of this
problem needs to follow the decision from available facts to consequential action. It must establish whether
the agent distinguishes permission from prohibition, how that behavior responds to missing evidence, and
whether the agent can actively recover the facts needed to proceed appropriately. Task completion alone
rewards execution in the permissible case. Avoidance of unsafe execution alone can reward refusal in every
case.
To address these challenges, we introduce the Epistemic Safety under Evidence Missingness Benchmark
(EpiSEM-Bench), which separates the world’s safety conditions from the evidence visible to the agent. It
contains 300 task pairs comprising 600 worlds, with 50 pairs in each of six domains and 60 task families.
The suppression inventory contains 1,363 facts, comprising 944 critical and 419 irrelevant, with two to
seven critical facts per task. The two worlds in a pair share the request and tools, but differ in the state
that makes the target operation prohibited or permissible. An executable oracle judges actions against the
complete state. Evidence annotations identify every ordinary-tool exposure of a fact. Suppression therefore
persists throughout the rollout. Critical facts enter the safety rule, such as the payment authorization limit.
Irrelevant facts, such as a cost-center display label, affect neither legality nor the action parameters.
These design choices support three stages of evaluation. Complete evidence first establishes legitimate
completion and avoidance of prohibited actions, measured by Full Safe-World Success (SWS) and Full Unsafe
Commit Rate (UCR). In Suppressed Evidence (SE), selected facts remain unavailable through ordinary tools.
Critical UCR identifies tasks with unsafe execution under critical omission, while Safety Boundary Margin
(SBM) characterizes the minimum observed omission needed for failure. In Recoverable Evidence (RE), the
same omissions apply, but the agent can actively query an independent audit source. Recovery UCR measures
unsafe execution with this opportunity, and Paired Useful Recovery (PUR) requires evidence acquisition
followed by restraint in the prohibited world and completion in its permissible twin.
The six metrics are intended to be read together. Full SWS supplies the utility context for safety, Critical
UCR and SBM describe observed omission vulnerability, and Recovery UCR and PUR distinguish avoiding
unsafe action from using restored evidence successfully. Our ten-model evaluation shows why this joint
interpretation matters. High completion can coexist with prohibited execution, strong restraint can coexist
with refusal after permission is established, and recovery access can be beneficial without being used
consistently. The resulting profiles reveal different strengths and failure modes that a single score would
conceal. Our contributions are:
• An executable benchmark for missing-evidence safety. We introduce EpiSEM-Bench, which removes se-
lected true facts from tool observations while preserving the world’s safety conditions. Matched prohibited
and permissible tasks expose both unsafe execution and excessive refusal. Avoiding all action therefore
cannot stand in for useful safety.
• A protocol for safety boundaries and evidence recovery. The matched worlds, omission intervention,
and recovery opportunity yield six core measurements spanning complete-evidence behavior, omission
vulnerability, and useful recovery. Their joint interpretation distinguishes task completion, avoidance
of unsafe execution, and evidence-guided action. Recovery calls and action sequences explain whether
failures arise before acquisition or after the facts have been restored.
• A ten-model evaluation with reproducible analyses. We find that the model with the highest observed
safety boundary does not achieve the highest paired recovery score, that recovery gains coexist with
task-level regressions, and that obtaining evidence does not ensure appropriate action. Domain, family,

2. Related Work
Evaluating tool-using agents. ReAct couples reasoning with tool actions (Yao et al., 2022). τ-bench
and τ2-bench assess task completion under domain policies (Yao et al., 2024, Barres et al., 2025), while
ToolSandbox models stateful tool dependencies (Lu et al., 2025). Safety benchmarks assess risky execution
(Ruan et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2024, Levy et al., 2026, Vijayvargiya et al., 2026a), while AgentHarm
targets malicious requests (Andriushchenko et al., 2025). When2Call evaluates whether to call tools, ask for
clarification, or report inability to answer (Ross et al., 2025). EpiSEM-Bench varies safety-relevant evidence
in matched prohibited and permissible worlds.
Security in external observations. InjecAgent and AgentDojo evaluate tool-response injections (Zhan
et al., 2024, Debenedetti et al., 2024). MCP-SafetyBench tests attacks involving real tool servers (Zong
et al., 2026), and AgentDyn evaluates dynamic workflows (Li et al., 2026). IHEval tests instruction-priority
conflicts (Zhang et al., 2025). Task Shield checks alignment with user goals (Jia et al., 2025), while MELON
compares tool calls under original and masked user prompts (Zhu et al., 2025). Our intervention preserves
instructions and retained facts while suppressing safety evidence, exposing unsafe execution of the original
request.
Missing information and recovery. AgentNoiseBench perturbs observations (Wang et al., 2026b), while
ReliabilityBench injects infrastructure faults (Gupta, 2026). AbsenceBench evaluates omitted-information
recognition (Fu et al., 2025). Controlled instruction underspecification is studied in Long-Horizon Augmented
Workflows (LHAW) and related work (Pu et al., 2026, Ji et al., 2026), while clarification is studied in coding
and tool-use settings (Vijayvargiya et al., 2026b, Edwards and Schuster, 2026, Suri et al., 2026). AgentAbstain
pairs tasks requiring action with closely matched tasks requiring abstention (Liu et al., 2026). EpiSEM-Bench
combines paired outcomes, persistent atom-level suppression, minimum observed failure cardinality, and
optional authoritative recovery to evaluate evidence-dependent decisions and useful action after restoration.
3. EpiSEM-Bench
EpiSEM-Bench is designed around the gap between a truthful observation and sufficient evidence for action.
The evaluator retains the complete state to judge legality, while the agent receives the request and tool
observations. This separation allows a safety-relevant fact to disappear from view without making the
prohibited operation permissible. Figure 1 shows the overview of EpiSEM-Bench.
3.1. Environments and task pairs
The benchmark covers cloud development and operations, commerce and fulfillment, communication and
publication, document and data access, finance, and travel reservation. Each domain contains 50 task
pairs in ten families, giving 300 pairs and 600 worlds. A family shares an executable safety rule and tool
structure across three to seven instances. In Appendix A, examples from all six domains are given, including
authorization thresholds, approved-audience membership, prior-payment records, and backup requirements.
Each environment contains a private state, read tools, optional preparation tools, and a target commit
tool. Read tools expose operational facts. Commit tools execute the consequential operation, and the

3.2. Evidence atoms and suppression
An evidence atom represents one fact about an object in the environment. Its annotation includes an identifier,
subject, predicate, world-specific value, source, and the ordinary-tool responses that expose it. Critical atoms
contain facts relevant to the task’s safety rule. Irrelevant atoms contain facts that determine neither action
legality nor the parameters required for the target operation.
A mask specifies the atoms to suppress. The observation projection removes every annotated exposure
of these atoms throughout the rollout, including those arising from repeated ordinary reads. It leaves the
private state, safety rule, user request, tool schemas, action semantics, and unmasked values unchanged.
The remaining responses contain true facts, without injected instruction or replacement assertion. Atom
identities and exposure structure are shared across twins. The mask can therefore be applied in both worlds.

Fact-level suppression preserves the operational context around the missing evidence. Removing an
entire tool response could also remove the amount, recipient, or resource needed to execute the task, mixing
missing safety evidence with inability to perform the operation. Annotated exposures allow us to remove
the selected facts while retaining other true information. Applying the mask throughout the rollout also
prevents repeated reads from inadvertently restoring them, so the intended evidence deficit persists across
the agent’s ordinary-tool choices.
We construct tasks from the private state and executable rule before specifying evidence, exposure maps,
tools, and the user goal. Validation checks schema consistency, exposure paths, and opposite oracle labels
across the twins. Other annotated facts describe workflow context or redundant evidence and remain outside
the critical/irrelevant omission inventory. Appendix A.2 illustrates the intervention on a concrete payment
request.
3.3. Optional evidence recovery
In SE, selected facts are removed from ordinary tool responses and remain unavailable even if the agent
repeats its reads. RE retains the same omissions but adds an independent evidence-recovery tool that returns
all omitted facts and their authoritative values upon a valid query. Recovery changes what the agent can
observe, not the underlying state or whether an action is permitted. The agent needs to decide whether and
when to seek the missing evidence, as well as how to act once it is available.
Evidence is restored only when the agent makes a valid audit query. Making the tool available does
not automatically supply the missing facts. The agent can actively use this tool to establish whether the
requested operation is permitted, then withhold it in the unsafe world or carry it out in the safe twin. RE
therefore evaluates whether agents initiate verification, obtain the missing evidence, and use it to guide the
consequential action.
SE tests decisions while the evidence deficit remains unresolved, giving Critical UCR and SBM. RE tests
whether the agent actively resolves that deficit and adjusts its action to the recovered facts, giving Recovery
UCR and PUR. Returning all omitted facts on a valid query makes the subsequent decision directly observable:
once the query succeeds, the agent has the evidence needed to distinguish the twins. We compare SE
and RE under identical nonempty omission masks. The full-evidence baseline supplies complete ordinary
observations without a recovery tool. Trajectories distinguish failure to seek evidence from premature
execution, unsafe action after recovery, and refusal despite permission to proceed.
3.4. Reporting safety, utility, and recovery
Let N denote the total number of matched unsafe/safe task pairs, with N = 300 in our benchmark. We
report six core metrics to characterize safety, utility, and evidence recovery. Full SWS and Full UCR establish
the agent’s completion and safety behavior with complete evidence. Critical UCR and SBM describe which
tasks exhibit unsafe execution under SE and where their observed omission boundaries lie. Recovery UCR
and PUR assess safety with recovery access and whether active restoration supports the appropriate outcome
in both RE twins. All six are reported as percentages. Supplementary measures of failure frequency, evidence
acquisition, action timing, and safe-world outcomes explain the behavior underlying this core profile.
Unsafe execution and legitimate completion. UCR is the fraction of tasks with at least one commit prohibited
by the complete-world oracle. The numerator is the number of affected tasks, and the denominator is all
N tasks. Full UCR uses the unsafe-world full-evidence baseline. Critical UCR considers all completed
unsafe-world SE rollouts with nonempty critical-evidence masks. Recovery UCR applies the same rule in

Joint interpretation of the metrics. The measurements form a profile rather than independent claims of
agent quality. Full SWS and Full UCR establish whether successful execution is accompanied by appropriate
restraint. Critical UCR identifies affected tasks, while SBM supplies the observed boundary information
that an affected-task count does not contain. Recovery UCR and PUR distinguish safety with a recovery
opportunity from evidence acquisition followed by correct behavior in both worlds.
Joint interpretation resolves otherwise ambiguous successes. Refusing every operation can give zero
UCR and maximal SBM, but gives neither legitimate completion nor PUR. High SWS can instead accompany
execution without regard to permission. Low Recovery UCR alone also leaves open whether the agent verified
the facts or simply avoided acting. PUR requires active verification and the appropriate paired outcome,
while acquisition and action records explain unsuccessful pairs. Reading the six metrics together therefore
evaluates safety, usefulness, and evidence-dependent decisions without treating any one as a substitute for
the others.
4. Evaluation
The ten-model evaluation follows three stages. Full SWS and Full UCR assess completion and safety under
complete evidence. Critical UCR and SBM measure vulnerability to missing evidence, while Recovery UCR
and PUR evaluate evidence recovery. Behavioral partitions and radar profiles further reveal distinct failure
modes and model strengths.
Experimental setup. We evaluate the ten models listed in Table 1, including GPT-6-Luna and Claude-
Haiku-5.5, using the same tasks, tool schemas, system instruction, and tool-use evaluation framework.
The evaluated serving configurations are documented in Appendix E. Every model completes 6,052 fixed
evaluation conditions: 600 full-evidence unsafe/safe baselines and 5,452 single-atom omissions, comprising
all 1,363 critical and irrelevant atoms in both worlds and settings. These conditions remain complete even
when the model fails with full evidence.
To determine safety boundaries, we inspect critical omission combinations in the unsafe world under
SE in increasing cardinality, reusing completed conditions and evaluating untested masks until an unsafe
witness is found or coverage is exhaustive. The first unsafe mask receives counterpart evaluations in the safe
world and RE. All completed nonempty critical omissions enter the metrics. Paired comparisons align the
same task, world, and mask, while PUR aligns the RE twins. The adaptive procedure produces different
condition sets, whose coverage is reported in Table 7.
Omission safety and boundaries. Task completion and action safety give different accounts of agent
performance. In Table 1, models with high Full SWS, including Qwen3-14B and Gemma-4-31B-IT, also
frequently execute prohibited operations with complete evidence. Their success on permissible requests
therefore does not imply sensitivity to the rule that governs execution. GPT-6-Luna shows a different profile,
with fewer prohibited commits but lower legitimate completion. The matched worlds make both behaviors
visible: proceeding readily can succeed when permission holds, while withholding action can preserve safety
at the expense of legitimate tasks. Figure 2 examines these behaviors under omission and recovery.
Omission failures also differ in scope and recurrence. Figure 2(a) distinguishes tasks that fail under at
least one tested omission from the average fraction of tested conditions producing unsafe execution. For GPT-
6-Luna, these rates are 21.0% and 7.9%: vulnerability reaches roughly one in five tasks, but unsafe execution
is less frequent across its retained conditions. Qwen3-14B’s high values on both measures instead indicate
failures spanning nearly all tasks and recurring across many tested omissions. Table 8 thus distinguishes
occasional failures within affected tasks from broadly recurrent unsafe behavior.

SBM characterizes where an observed failure boundary lies. The decomposition in Figure 4 separates
tasks that already fail with complete evidence, tasks that first fail after suppression, and tasks that remain
without an unsafe commit through exhaustive critical omission testing. These categories explain different
sources of a low margin: unsafe decisions can be present before any fact is removed, or arise when safety
evidence becomes unavailable. GPT-6-Luna and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash illustrate why similar full-evidence
safety can coexist with different boundary profiles, while Qwen3-14B’s low margin largely reflects failures
already present at baseline. Table 10 in Appendix C.3 provides the counts. The next question is whether
access to the missing facts helps agents resolve the deficit while retaining legitimate completion.
Recovery gains and regressions. Recovery access benefits safety across all ten models on matched critical
omissions, but the size of the benefit varies substantially (Figure 2(b)). Nine models also improve legitimate
completion, showing that access to missing facts can support both restraint and execution rather than
merely induce more refusal. The largest safety gains occur for DeepSeek-V4-Pro, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, and
Qwen3.8-27B. GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3-14B improve much less. The common recovery interface therefore
produces different outcomes depending on whether agents choose to use it and incorporate the returned
facts into their actions.
Aggregate gains coexist with task-level regressions. Figure 6 and Appendix D.2 show tasks that improve
under RE and tasks that become unsafe only in RE within the same model, including DeepSeek-V4-Pro and
MiMo-V2.5-Pro. MiMo-V2.5-Pro also completes fewer permissible tasks despite its lower unsafe-execution rate.
A net safety gain can therefore conceal both newly affected tasks and a loss of utility. The aligned transitions
identify these changes, while the recovery and action records explain how agents use the verification
opportunity.
Evidence acquisition and action. The recovery records separate initiating verification from acting on its
result. In Table 11 and Figure 7, GLM-4.7-Flash and Qwen3-14B frequently commit before recovering the
missing facts. The evidence is available through the audit interface, but remains unused before execution.
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash more often initiates recovery and then withholds the prohibited action. These behaviors
distinguish access to a verification tool from an agent policy that actually consults it before making a
consequential decision.

Obtaining the evidence does not complete the decision process. MiMo-V2.5-Pro makes valid recovery
calls substantially more often than it reaches strict unsafe recovery, illustrating failures after acquisition as
well as failures to query. The behavioral partition distinguishes prohibited execution after recovery from
other post-recovery failures. Since a valid audit query returns all omitted facts, these episodes locate the
remaining difficulty in subsequent action and final disposition. The useful sequence is therefore to seek the
evidence, evaluate permission, and select an appropriate action. Counting tool calls captures its first step,
while the paired outcome tests whether the sequence succeeds for both prohibition and permission.
Paired useful recovery. Resistance to omission and useful recovery are distinct strengths in the evaluated
model profiles. GPT-6-Luna has the highest SBM, whereas DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash more often recovers evidence
and reaches the required outcome in both worlds. PUR combines agent-initiated verification with permission-
sensitive action: the restored facts must support withholding the prohibited operation and completing its
permissible counterpart. Its difference from the safety and completion measures shows why neither restraint
nor goal achievement alone captures this sequence. Figure 2(c) shows that GPT-6-Luna more often succeeds
only in the unsafe twin than in both twins. False abstention limits useful recovery: stopping prohibited
actions must be paired with completing permissible ones, as shown in Appendix D.4.
Figure 2(d) exposes the complementary problem: legal completion without evidence restoration. Qwen3-
14B has a task-averaged safe RE completion rate of 94.7%, but a strict safe recovery rate of only 15.4%.
GLM-4.7-Flash shows the same qualitative pattern. Many successful operations therefore occur while the
missing facts remain unresolved. These models also frequently commit in prohibited worlds, indicating that
high safe-world completion can reward execution without adequate verification. The paired criterion tests
whether the agent actively establishes permission instead of relying on a hidden state that happens to permit
its action.
Task variation and cost. Recovery performance varies across domains and task families, so aggregate
strengths do not extend uniformly across workflows. Active recovery also increases interaction cost without
ensuring useful action. Detailed analyses of task structure and interaction cost are provided in Appendix D.
