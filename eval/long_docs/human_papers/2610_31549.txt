ABSTRACT
Privacy auditing empirically assesses privacy leakage by using membership in-
ference attacks to establish lower bounds on privacy loss. We consider an obser-
vational auditing setting in which the auditor cannot insert canaries, control data
inclusion, or retrain the target model, and must instead rely on fixed sets of known
members and non-members. This setting is relevant for large deployed models or
when retraining is restricted by computational or access constraints. In this case,
non-members available for auditing may differ in distribution from the training
data, causing membership-inference scores to be skewed due to the distribution
shift. We address this problem by estimating the counterfactual in-distribution
non-member score distribution using the available out-of-distribution (OOD) non-
members. Our proposed method learns the relationship between data representa-
tions and membership-inference scores from OOD non-members and transports
it to the target covariate distribution. Because this transport may become unreli-
able under extrapolation or imperfect conditional invariance, we further introduce
a conservative correction calibrated directly to the nominal transported privacy
level. We construct controlled calibration tasks from the available non-member
data to estimate how representation-space displacement inflates the audit, and use
this estimate to correct the transport-induced audit error. Across multiple datasets
with distribution shifts, our method yields substantially more stable privacy esti-
mates while retaining tighter lower bounds than baselines.

INTRODUCTION
Membership inference attacks (MIAs) distinguish training-set members from non-members based
on a model’s behavior, and are widely used to empirically assess privacy leakage of machine learn-
ing models (Shokri et al., 2017; Yeom et al., 2018; Carlini et al., 2022). A fundamental assumption
underlying such comparisons is that members and non-members are drawn from same data distri-
butions. In practice, however, available non-members may differ systematically from the training
distribution (Liang & You, 2024; Duan et al., 2024; Meeus et al., 2025). Under such distribution
shift, membership-inference scores can capture distributional differences in addition to membership
information, effectively confounding membership inference with distribution-shift detection (Duan
et al., 2024; Das et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Consequently, the observed membership signal
can substantially overestimate privacy leakage (Maini et al., 2024; Even et al., 2026).
This issue is particularly consequential for privacy auditing, where MIAs are used to establish em-
pirical lower bounds on privacy loss (Jagielski et al., 2020; Tramer et al., 2022). Most existing
auditing methods avoid this confounding by controlling the training process—for example, by re-
training models (Jagielski et al., 2020; Nasr et al., 2023), randomizing the inclusion of candidate
examples (Steinke et al., 2023; Mahloujifar et al., 2025), or inserting canaries (Pillutla et al., 2023;
Steinke et al., 2023). Such intervention may be unavailable for already-deployed or third-party mod-
els and can be prohibitively expensive for large models (Kazmi et al., 2024; Cebere et al., 2026).

Therefore, we consider an observational auditing setting where the model has already been trained
and cannot be retrained (Kazmi et al., 2024; Cebere et al., 2026). The auditor only has black-box
access to the target model together with fixed sets of examples known to be training-set members
and non-members. This setting removes the ability to randomize membership, and consequently
introduces a challenge that is largely absent when the auditor controls data inclusion: the avail-
able non-members might not follow the same distribution as the training data. Correcting for this
distributional confounding is therefore necessary to obtain a valid lower bound on privacy leakage.
A natural solution is to reconstruct the missing in-distribution non-member score distribution. We
model how the MIA score varies with data distributions among the available OOD non-members.
This nominal transport is motivated by a conditional score-invariance assumption: after conditioning
on the representation, the non-member score distribution is invariant across domains. This allows
the conditional score mechanism learned from the reference distribution to be evaluated under the
target distribution. However, conditional transport alone is insufficient. Even when conditional score
invariance is valid on the common support, the target distribution may place substantial mass in re-
gions that are poorly represented by the available non-members. Predicting scores there necessarily
requires extrapolation. Moreover, the conditional-invariance assumption may be imperfect.
Our key idea is to correct the transport-induced audit error by constructing controlled calibration
tasks within the observed non-member population. We partition the OOD non-members into slabs
separated along the source-to-target direction, train the same score-transport model on a source-side
slab, and evaluate it on a more target-like slab whose true scores remain observable. Because both
the predicted and observed score samples are available, we can directly measure how much such
extrapolation inflates the resulting privacy lower bound. Repeating this procedure over multiple
controlled displacements yields an empirical relationship between representation-space displace-
ment and transport-induced audit error. We then evaluate this error-growth relationship at the actual
OOD-to-IID displacement and use the resulting estimate to correct the nominal transported audit.
As shown in Figure 1, counterfactual score transport (panel (a)) provides a nominal estimate of
the missing IID non-member distribution, while the shift-aware calibration (panel (b) and (c)) de-
termines how much of the resulting privacy evidence should be trusted. In this work, we use the
µ-Gaussian differential privacy (µ-GDP) formulation (Dong et al., 2022) as a framework for quan-
tifying empirical privacy leakage, and report the empirical lower bound on µ.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We estimate the missing IID non-member score distribution by applying a score model learned
from OOD non-members to covariates drawn from the target distribution, under a conditional
invariance assumption (Section 4.1).
• We identify when this assumption may fail (Section 4.2) and construct calibration tasks to
measure how representation-space displacement affects transport-induced audit error, then use
the resulting error-growth estimate to correct the transported audit. (Section 4.3).

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
Privacy auditing. An auditing algorithm takes a learning algorithm A and a privacy hypothesis H,
and tests whether the observed behavior of A satisfies H (Jagielski et al., 2020; Nasr et al., 2023).
Typically, the auditor performs a randomized experiment with known membership labels Ai, applies
a MIA to produce guesses Ti ∈{−1, 0, 1} for each example, where Ti = 0 denotes abstention, and
then evaluates H using the attack outcomes and the known memberships. For an error probability
p ∈[0, 1], the audit is p-valid if P(Reject H | A satisfies H) ≤p. Empirical privacy parameters can
then be obtained from the strongest privacy hypothesis that the audit can confidently reject.
Early approaches often require retraining models on neighboring datasets (Jagielski et al., 2020;
Nasr et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022; Nasr et al., 2023; Zanella-Beguelin et al., 2023), while more recent
one-run methods reduce this cost by controlling the inclusion of multiple candidate examples within
a single training run (Steinke et al., 2023; Mahloujifar et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025; Keinan et al.,
2026). However, these approaches still rely on intervention in the training process, which requires
the membership indicators Ai ∈{−1, 1} to be independent.
Auditing under distribution shift. Distribution shift is known to affect membership inference
by changing the score distributions of non-members, potentially overestimate the privacy leakage
(Yichuan et al., 2024; Meeus et al., 2024; Das et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). Kazmi et al. (2024)
addresses the lack of in-distribution non-members by training a generative model on known mem-
bers and using generated samples as non-members, while accounting for distributional mismatch
between real and generated data through a baseline classifier. Cebere et al. (2026) instead explicitly
corrects the shift through global composition or propensity-based conditional adjustment; its guar-
antees, however, rely on distributional overlap and accurate propensity estimation, and can become
overly conservative under severe shift. Kalemaj et al. (2025) evaluates label privacy without inter-
vening on the training dataset by exploiting naturally occurring variation in the data. Related work
has also used conditional modeling, particularly quantile regression, to estimate example-dependent
non-member score distributions (Bertran et al., 2023). Thaker et al. (2026) study this approach under
class-support shift, where the available background data omit entire classes or subclasses. In con-
trast, we consider covariate shift, where the member and available non-member populations share
the same task and label space but differ in their input distributions.
Measuring privacy leakage with µ-GDP. We quantify empirical privacy leakage using the µ-GDP
scale, which characterizes distinguishability through the hypothesis-testing trade-off between neigh-
boring datasets (Dong et al., 2022).
Definition 1. An algorithm A is µ-GDP if for all neighboring datasets D and D′,
T(A(D), A(D′))(α) ≥T(N(0, 1), N(µ, 1))(α), ∀α ∈[0, 1],
(1)
where T(A(D), A(D′))(α) = inf{βϕ : αϕ ≤α} with Type-I error αϕ and Type-II error βϕ from
all rejection rules ϕ.
For privacy audit with µ-GDP, the auditor performs membership guesses and compares the observed
guessing performance against that permitted by a candidate µ-GDP guarantee, yielding a lower
confidence bound ˆµ on the privacy parameter (Nasr et al., 2023; Mahloujifar et al., 2025).

4.2
WHEN IS NOMINAL TRANSPORT UNRELIABLE?
Recall that identifying FSP
N via Equation (6) requires two conditions: (a) the conditional invariance
assumption (Assumption 1), and (b) sufficient support overlap, supp(PZ) ⊆supp(QZ). Either
condition may fail. The representation Z must retain sufficient information about the target-model
score for conditional invariance to hold, while support coverage is required to avoid extrapolating
the score model beyond the observed source covariates. We distinguish the two failure modes:
1. Lack of support overlap. Suppose Assumption 1 holds on the overlap region, but supp(PZ) ⊈
supp(QZ). Let O = supp(PZ) ∩supp(QZ), Oc = supp(PZ) \ O. Then

Diagnostic assessment of conditional transportability. In order to assess whether the score distri-
bution learned from OOD non-members transfers to the target domain, we define the domain propen-
sity score π(z) = P(D = Q | Z = z, A = −1), where values near 0 and 1 indicate IID-like and
OOD-like representations, respectively. We partition the data into ten propensity bins and, for each
bin I, estimate the OOD non-member score CDF: FQ,I(s) = P(S ≤s | D = Q, π(z) ∈I). For an
observation with score S, we compute its PIT (probability integral transform) value U = FQ,I(S). If
the conditional score distribution is transportable from Q to P, the PIT values for IID non-members
should be approximately uniform, and their ECDF should follow the diagonal.
As shown in Figure 2 for a distribution-shift benchmark DomainNet (Peng et al., 2019) and across
more datasets in Figures 7 and 8, the IID non-member curve is either close to the diagonal or lies
below it. The latter indicates larger IID non-member MIA scores than predicted from OOD non-
members, implying that nominal transport tends to under-correct the shift and can yield an anti-
conservative privacy lower bound. This motivates our calibration tasks to correct the audit error.
